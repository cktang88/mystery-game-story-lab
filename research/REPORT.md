# Fix the truth, then ration it

The strongest lesson across every source is that the great slow-reveal stories are not complicated to follow; they are strict. They fix one true history and a handful of hard rules before any scene is written, show that truth through limited viewpoints, and release it in an order where each answer reclassifies something the audience already saw. Steins;Gate stays readable because it has one remembering witness, one visible number and one problem in focus at a time. Game of Thrones stays readable because 2,007 named characters are filtered through about 14 heavy viewpoints on three plot lines that begin in one castle. NieR and Code Geass are remembered because the final reveal and the protagonist's personal cost are the same act. For this game, that means the four pillars (AGI, aliens, cloning, time travel) should each be taught as a playable rule with a hard limit before it is ever used as an explanation, each should carry one human-sized price, and the player should assemble the truth by identifying, reconstructing and listening, with the game confirming conclusions in batches. The biggest risks are the ones the brief already fears: stacking late twists, payoffs shorter than their setups, and the stock reveals a genre-literate player will test for first (you are the clone, it was a simulation, you caused the loop, the AI turns). Several popular claims in this area are weaker than they sound, and the notes behind this report have real gaps; both are flagged where they matter and collected near the end.

## The great slow-reveal stories climb the same reveal ladder

The surveyed anime and Japanese games share a macro-shape. A small, legible premise is replaced two to four times by a larger frame, and the last frame shows that the enemy and the heroes share an origin or that the heroes' own institution built the conflict. Madoka Magica runs a five-step ladder in twelve episodes, from the cost of the contract, to the monsters being former magical girls, to the alien's cosmic motive, to one rival's repeated month, to the wish that rewrites the system ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madoka_Magica)). NieR:Automata's truths arrive in a fixed order: the invaders are long dead, there are no humans on the moon, and the android force exists to sustain the fiction that there are ([GDC Vault review](https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc2018/GameNarrativeReview/Kleinman_Game_Narrrative_Review.pdf)). The researcher's synthesis of this pattern is a four-rung ladder per thread: **how the power works, what it costs, who designed the system and why, and how the protagonists are its product or perpetrator**. That ladder is an inference from the plot summaries, not a stated method, but it is a usable planning tool.

The most transferable device is "same events, new sensor". NieR Replicant's second run changes nothing that happens and only adds comprehension, subtitling enemy speech so that earlier victories read as atrocities ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NieR); [RPGFan](https://www.rpgfan.com/feature/even-if-our-words-seem-meaningless-yoko-taros-plea-for-universal-empathy-in-nier)). Automata's second route replays the first through a character who can hack and read data, and one analyst argues the device works only because the reread is compulsory ([route-structure study](https://saschb2b.github.io/game-mechanics/games/nier-automata/multi-route-structure)). The same study reports Yoko Taro saying the route system began as a budget measure, which makes it a production technique as much as a narrative one. For a game with an AGI in the cast, a second pass through known scenes with a sensor the human lead lacks is the natural equivalent; that application is the researcher's suggestion.

Knowledge is the other recurring key. Virtue's Last Reward stalls a branch until the player imports a fact learned in another ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-storytelling-secrets-of-i-virtue-s-last-reward-i-)), and 13 Sentinels gates thirteen protagonists' routes on each other while giving the player a flowchart, a chronological archive and a glossary ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/13_Sentinels:_Aegis_Rim)). These answer the practical question of how interwoven threads stay in order. Coherence behind the scenes came from backward design and bookkeeping: Uchikoshi wrote 999's ending first and built backward to avoid confusion ([Wikiwand](https://wikiwand.com/en/articles/Nine_Hours,_Nine_Persons,_Nine_Doors)), and prototyped Virtue's Last Reward in Excel, rewriting every dependent round when one changed ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-storytelling-secrets-of-i-virtue-s-last-reward-i-)).

Western works add reveal engines that do not depend on a single twist. Hyperion nests seven testimonies in different genres, each exposing one face of the central enigma ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperion_(Simmons_novel))). Never Let Me Go tells its clones the truth outright and early, then lets the meaning sink in as their hopes are dismantled ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Never_Let_Me_Go_(novel))). Dark widens by one time period per season around four anchor families ([Wikiwand](https://wikiwand.com/en/Dark_(TV_series))). SOMA teaches its copying rule through play twice before the ending applies it to the player, and its ending is praised as predictable yet still shocking ([Tampere University Game Research Lab](https://blogs.tuni.fi/playlab/features/the-ending-of-soma-and-why-its-excellent/)). Westworld's first season is the proof that fair twists survive being solved: Reddit predicted nearly every mid-level reveal and still missed the top-level design ([The New Republic](https://newrepublic.com/article/139153/reddit-ruin-westworld)).

### Endings that last pay the plan's price

The endings singled out are the ones where the plan's logical completion and a loved character's loss coincide. Code Geass's Zero Requiem is called one of anime's best endings because it completes the hero's logic, fulfils a maxim from the first episode, and leaves one deliberately unresolved image for viewers to decide ([EpicStream](https://epicstream.com/article/code-geass-ending-explained)). The evidence base for Code Geass in the notes is thin: one fan-press explainer, plus a writer's remark that it was a happy ending which reached the notes only second-hand via [FandomWire](https://fandomwire.com/his-life-had-not-been-in-vain-code-geass-writer-saw-lelouchs-tragic-ending-to-be-the-happiest-possible-outcome-of-the-story/) and could not be checked against the original interview. NieR:Automata's final ending asks the player to give up their save for a stranger; Yoko Taro says he had not planned it and removed an idea that would have pushed a specific message ([Siliconera](https://www.siliconera.com/yoko-taro-speaks-varied-endings-meanings-nier-automata/)). The researcher's reading is that top-rated endings share three properties: a first-hour line or rule is fulfilled literally, the win condition and the personal loss are one act, and the audience is left one small thing to decide or give up.

The failures are just as consistent. Critics said Virtue's Last Reward over-stacks reveals at the end, with one reviewer finding them more confusing than surprising ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_Escape:_Virtue%27s_Last_Reward)). Some reviewers found 13 Sentinels jargon-dependent and lacking a central plot ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/13_Sentinels:_Aegis_Rim)). Death Note lost viewers after killing the rival who generated its tension ([Den of Geek](https://www.denofgeek.com/tv/how-death-note-redeemed-itself-in-its-final-episode/)). Evangelion's TV finale dropped the foreshadowed external plot for an interior one and drew backlash severe enough to prompt replacement films ([SYFY Wire](https://www.syfy.com/syfywire/people-really-hated-the-ending-of-neon-genesis-evangelion-heres-why-they-were-wrong)). The mystery-box failures in Western television have documented causes too: Battlestar Galactica advertised a plan its showrunner later admitted did not exist ([The A.V. Club](https://www.avclub.com/battlestar-writers-finally-admit-that-the-cylons-never-1798262967)), and Westworld's second season scrambled a whole season's chronology to protect one twist that did little for its characters ([The Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/2018/06/25/westworld/westworld-finale-haphazard-storytelling)). In Severance's second season the answer-delivering episodes rated highest and the lore detours lowest ([The Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/2025/03/25/severance/severance-season-2-felt-off-lost-comparisons-reddit-theories)). A shipped game has one advantage over serial television: it is finished before anyone sees it, so "no plan" is avoidable by construction.

## Steins;Gate proves strict limits make time travel readable

Steins;Gate's system has few rules, and each tool has one memorable cap: a message to the past limited to 36 bytes, and a memory leap limited to 48 hours ([Votuko fan guide](https://gwern.net/doc/fiction/science-fiction/time-travel/2023-votuko-themechanicsofsteinsgate.pdf)). Only the protagonist keeps his memories when history shifts, so the audience follows exactly one continuous memory. A meter showing a single number arrives in episode 15, when a scoreboard is finally needed ([Wikipedia episode list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Steins;Gate_episodes)). The rule descriptions rest mainly on a fan guide whose interpretive chapter is openly labelled as the author's own, and on TV Tropes, so exact in-story wording is not verified.

The teaching order matters as much as the rules. The audience sees an unexplained anomaly in episode 1, a confirmed experiment in episode 3, written-down rules in episode 6, a low-stakes test in episode 7, one personal use per episode in episodes 8 to 10, and the bill in episode 12 ([Wikipedia episode list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Steins;Gate_episodes)). Kotaku's reviewer credits the result with rules that hold where most time-travel stories break theirs ([Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/steins-gate-the-kotaku-review-1559811854)). The researcher's summary of the pattern is **anomaly, experiment, named rule, personal use, cost**, with no rule lectured before the thing it explains.

The emotional design is a mirror. Each message in the first half grants one friend's deepest wish; the second half makes the hero cancel them in reverse order, erasing both the wish and the friend's memory of it, while refusing sends the player down that character's own ending ([TV Tropes](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Anime/SteinsGate); [GDC Vault review](https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc2023/GNR/RunyonConnor_DigiPenInstituteofTechnology_GNR2023.pdf)). Plot logic and emotional logic are the same action, and the player always knows the checklist. The fate rule is taught by repetition with variation: the same person dies by shooting, by car, by train, until the audience states the rule unprompted ([Wikipedia episode list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Steins;Gate_episodes)).

The ending is the cleanest model of a fair twist in the notes. A direct rescue attempt fails and makes the hero the cause of what he saw in episode 1; the second attempt changes nothing his earlier self observed and alters only what was true behind it ([Wikipedia episode list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Steins;Gate_episodes)). TV Tropes compresses the loophole into one question: did he ever see her die, or only something that made him believe it ([TV Tropes](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Anime/SteinsGate))? No professional critic in the notes argues "earned versus cheat" explicitly; that verdict is the researcher's synthesis plus fan analysis. One detail often cited, that the opening text message is itself the first message to the past, comes from the researcher's memory and was not confirmed in any fetched source.

The flaws are instructive. The writer admitted the original drew criticism for poor early pacing and that the remake cut conversational tangents ([Automaton](https://automaton-media.com/en/news/steinsgate-reboot-developers-on-adding-the-games-harshest-route-yet-their-criteria-for-script-revisions-and-relaunching-the-science-adventure-series/)). The true-ending conditions are opaque without a guide ([GDC Vault review](https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc2023/GNR/RunyonConnor_DigiPenInstituteofTechnology_GNR2023.pdf)). The sequel was faulted, in a mid-season review rather than a verdict on the whole series, for piling up mysteries and red herrings without the original's clear momentum ([Anime News Network](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/review/steins-gate-0/episode-14/.134412)). A story with four speculative pillars sits squarely in that risk. The original is also mostly reading, so it is a model for structure and not for this game's interaction.

### Choose one time model and enforce it

The other admired time-travel stories reach coherence the same way. Dark moves people only in 33-year steps ([Wikiwand](https://wikiwand.com/en/Dark_(TV_series))), gave each decade its own colour scheme, and its creators called "when to reveal what" the main structural question ([The Hollywood Reporter](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/live-feed/dark-final-season-explained-baran-bo-odar-jantje-friese-interview-1302171)). Primer makes the traveller wait inside the box one-for-one and forbids going back before switch-on, but its refusal to restate rules left many viewers confused ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primer_(film))). Outer Wilds resets the world every 22 minutes or so and keeps only what the player has learned ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/live-die-repeat-how-i-outer-wilds-i-piques-curiosity-in-an-ambivalent-solar-system)). The three-way taxonomy the notes draw from these (fixed loop, reset loop with knowledge persistence, overlapping doubles with hard costs) is the researcher's own framing, since no general reference could be fetched. The practical inference is sound regardless: a fixed timeline suits a mystery game because the player's evidence can never be invalidated by later action.

## Game of Thrones stays clear by starting together on three plots

The series' depth and its tracking load are different numbers. A network study of the five books counts 2,007 named characters across 343 chapters but only 14 major viewpoint characters, each with about 154 connections, close to the roughly 150 stable ties often cited as a human limit; the authors conclude the story stays within readers' cognitive limits because it is experienced through those few perspectives ([Gessey-Jones et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.01783.pdf)). The whole thing hangs on three plot lines, and the viewpoint cast grows from nine in book one to 31 by book five ([Wikiwand](https://wikiwand.com/en/A_Song_of_Ice_and_Fire)). The study counts 24 viewpoint characters where Wikiwand says 31, and neither source explains the difference. Martin says he took the shape from Tolkien: nearly everyone begins in one place and is then scattered ([Winter is Coming](https://winteriscoming.net/2019/05/14/george-r-r-martin-on-what-he-borrowed-from-j-r-r-tolkien)).

The television adaptation learned clarity the hard way. Its first pilot failed with test viewers who did not grasp that two leads were siblings, and about 90 percent was reshot ([TIME](https://time.com/4206627/game-of-thrones-creators-talk-original-disaster-pilot)). Map shots cut into the episode broke its flow, so orientation moved to the title sequence ([Art of the Title](https://artofthetitle.com/title/game-of-thrones)), which shows at most six locations per episode, always includes four anchors, and carries plot state such as a changed sigil ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_of_Thrones_title_sequence)). The lesson for a game is that a twist lands only if the audience holds the plain facts it overturns, and that orientation aids belong at chapter boundaries.

Unpredictability is partly an ordering effect. In chapter order the gaps between significant deaths look memoryless, while in the story's own calendar the same deaths cluster with long safe stretches; the study's authors read interleaved viewpoints as what converts realistic clustering into felt unpredictability ([Gessey-Jones et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.01783.pdf)). The shocks themselves were fair because their causes were ordinary on-screen events. Screen Rant traces the Red Wedding through a broken marriage promise, a freed prisoner, an executed ally and a lost half-army, down to the shut doors minutes before ([Screen Rant](https://screenrant.com/game-thrones-red-wedding-clues/2/)). Martin says he targets the expected next step: audiences assumed the hero was safe, and then that the son would avenge him ([TVLine](https://www.tvline.com/2212242/why-ned-stark-game-of-thrones-death-meant-robb-had-die-george-rr-martin/)). He also refuses to change a planted twist because fans guessed it ([The Mary Sue](https://www.themarysue.com/george-r-r-martin-on-book-secrets-and-brienne/)); the central parentage mystery was theorised for about twenty years before its reveal ([The Hollywood Reporter](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/live-feed/game-thrones-finale-jon-snow-mother-twist-means-906436)).

Zeynep Tufekci's explanation of why deaths were affordable is the most useful single idea for a multi-thread mystery. The early seasons told a sociological story in which institutions and incentives shape behaviour, so viewers understood even villains and no one character carried the plot; the late seasons switched to individual heroics ([Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/observations/the-real-reason-fans-hate-the-last-season-of-game-of-thrones/)). If every faction acts from visible incentives, players can reason toward the truth and a death does not break the plot.

The limits are equally documented. Convergence, not divergence, was the breaking point: Martin's "Meereenese knot" was the problem of who reaches one city in what order, and he rewrote for years and added another viewpoint to fix it ([Westeros.org](https://www.westeros.org/Citadel/SSM/Month/2011/07)). Splitting the cast by geography left readers waiting six years for half of it ([Wikiwand](https://origin-production.wikiwand.com/en/A_Feast_for_Crows)). The show's ending failed on missing connective tissue: SlashFilm argues the largest threat was resolved first, leaving an extended epilogue ([SlashFilm](https://www.slashfilm.com/819246/game-of-thrones-ending-explained-a-story-told-in-the-wrong-order)); travel became instant ([SlashFilm](https://www.slashfilm.com/552431/game-of-thrones-pacing)); and long-built setups went unused at the climax, according to one game designer's personal blog, which should be weighed as informed opinion ([Sirlin.net](https://sirlin.net/posts/game-of-thrones-what-went-wrong)). The critics differ on the root cause (storytelling mode, event order, time pressure) and no source ranks them. The thread budget the notes propose (three plot lines, four to six active threads, about nine as a ceiling) is extrapolated from the series' own figures; no fetched source tests how many storylines an audience can follow.

## Grip comes from the order of information, not the size of events

Curiosity research supports the brief's "at any moment the player knows the current question". Golman and Loewenstein model curiosity as the discomfort of an activated question with an uncertain answer, raised by importance, salience and the chance that one fact resolves several questions at once; it vanishes when a person feels certain, and attention drops quickly once an answer lands ([Golman and Loewenstein](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/golman/golman_loewenstein_curiosity.pdf)). The design reading is that every major answer should open a larger question in the same beat. Brewer and Lichtenstein rewrote one narrative in different information orders: the version where readers knew about the threat early scored 4.85 of 7 on liking, the pure-surprise version 4.30, and the version with its resolution removed fell to 2.75 on "is this a story" ([summary, University of Alberta](https://sites.ualberta.ca/~dmiall/694Empirical/Brewer_Lichtenstein_1981.htm)). Hitchcock's practitioner version is fifteen seconds of shock against fifteen minutes of tension ([No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/alfred-hitchcock-and-francois-truffaut-explain-surprise-vs-suspense)). Most secrets in a long game are worth more as dramatic irony or active investigation than as blindsides.

Two popular claims need downgrading. A 2025 meta-analysis found no reliable memory advantage for unfinished tasks, only a tendency to resume them ([Ghibellini and Meier](https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/palcom/v12y2025i1d10.1057_s41599-025-05000-w.html)), so cliffhangers pull people back but do not make content memorable. Spoiler findings conflict: one study found spoiled stories slightly more enjoyable ([The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/enough-with-the-spoiler-alerts-plot-spoilers-often-increase-enjoyment-62154)), another found a pre-story spoiler reduced enjoyment ([University of Arkansas](https://news.uark.edu/articles/38085/fulbright-college-research-team-explores-how-spoilers-affect-reading-enjoyment)). Both used short written stories, so transfer to a long game is uncertain. The safe position is to protect twists and also build for a second playthrough.

Craft authorities converge on causality and kept promises. Parker and Stone require "therefore" or "but" between every pair of beats, never "and then" ([Cracked](https://cracked.com/article_43358_trey-parker-and-matt-stones-surprise-nyu-class-contains-some-great-writing-advice.html)). Sanderson frames a story as promise, progress and payoff, links reader drop-off to missing progress signposts ([Sanderson](https://brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/brandon-sandersons-2025-guide-to-plot-lecture-2)), and holds that a conflict can be solved with a power only in proportion to how well the audience understands it ([Sanderson's First Law](https://brandonsanderson.com/sandersons-first-law/)). Pixar's rule allows coincidence to cause trouble but never to end it ([Open Culture](https://www.openculture.com/2013/03/pixars_22_rules_of_good_storytelling.html)). Much of the remaining craft material (McKee, Truby, Storr, Cron, Aristotle) reached the notes through secondary summaries and should be treated that way: Truby's notes say revelations must each change the hero's course and grow in intensity, and that the best opponent wants the same goal as the hero ([reader notes](https://publish.obsidian.md/aidanhelfant/Literature+Notes/Readwise/%F0%9F%93%9ABooks/The+Anatomy+of+Story)); Weiland roots a character in a Lie, a Want and a Need ([Weiland](https://www.helpingwritersbecomeauthors.com/character-arcs-3/)).

On twists, cognitive scientist Vera Tobin explains why fair ones work: audiences assume characters perceive what they do, the first framing anchors later guesses, and hindsight afterwards persuades people the story played fair ([NPR Hidden Brain](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/661878959)). No controlled experiment comparing fair and unfair twists was found; the standard is craft consensus plus that cognitive account. For endings, the peak-end rule says bounded experiences are judged mostly by their most intense moment and their end, though it is only a moderate predictor and has not been tested on stories ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peak%E2%80%93end_rule)). Oliver and Bartsch tie lasting impression to being moved and made to think, not only entertained ([Penn State abstract](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/appreciation-as-audience-response-exploring-entertainment-gratifi/)).

Games add emotions passive media cannot. Players who performed a violating act reported guilt and afterwards rated the violated moral concerns as more important ([BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/could-violent-video-games-make-players-more-moral-real-world)). Spec Ops: The Line expresses choices through its ordinary mechanics and withholds judgment ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/no-good-deed-narrative-design-in-i-spec-ops-the-line-i-)), but was called heavy-handed for forcing the act ([GDC Vault review](https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc2015/GameNarrativeReview/Wrampelmeier_Spec%20Ops_the_Line%20.pdf)). Emily Short's rule separates two cases: surprise is welcome when the player is exploring for information, while a committed decision should produce "yes, but" (it works, at a cost) ([Emily Short](https://emshort.blog/2019/02/05/story-robert-mckee/)).

## Fair-play mystery becomes deduction by doing

The classic contract is short: everything the detective uses is shown before the reveal, the culprit is someone already met, and a motive exists ([CrimeReads](https://crimereads.com/a-magicians-contract-between-reader-and-writer-rules-for-delivering-a-whodunit/)). Knox's rules ban supernatural agency and unexplained devices, and allow doubles only if the reader has been prepared ([Wikiwand](https://www.wikiwand.com/en/articles/Knox's_Commandments)). A sci-fi mystery threatens exactly those rules, and the researcher's fix follows from the last one: an exotic mechanism is fair once its rules are taught, ideally exercised by the player, well before it explains anything. Clones are literally Knox's twins. Clues are hidden by attention management: bury them among more vivid items, follow them with something exciting, or plant them early and attach meaning late ([CrimeReads](https://crimereads.com/a-magicians-contract-between-reader-and-writer-rules-for-delivering-a-whodunit/)). Christie faked the evidence witnesses trust, such as a "gunshot" staged hours after the killing ([agathachristie.com](https://www.agathachristie.com/news/2022/the-conjuring-tricks-of-agatha-christie)), which maps directly onto audio and timeline mechanics.

The durable twists attack an assumption the audience supplied without being told: that the narrator is honest ([CrimeReads](https://crimereads.com/100-years-of-the-murder-of-roger-ackroyd/)), that the body and time of death are as stated ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Devotion_of_Suspect_X)), that two names mean two people ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Decagon_House_Murders)). These should be used as categories of assumption to attack, not as plots to reuse. The researcher's game equivalent is the unreliable instrument: the trusted tool never shows a false fact, only true facts with a framing gap the player could have questioned. Rian Johnson warns that a whodunit built only on clue-gathering is a long run-up to one surprise and needs "a different engine in the car" ([SYFY Wire](https://www.syfy.com/syfy-wire/knives-out-director-rian-johnson-explains-how-to-build-a-great-whodunnit-mystery)). Twin Peaks shows the same risk from the other side: David Lynch believed the show was finished once its single murder was solved, and the mid-season-two reveal came with ratings already falling ([TVLine](https://tvline.com/2077046/twin-peaks-creators-david-lynch-mark-frost-argued-solve-laura-palmer-murder)). True Detective's director said some supposed clues were accidental, after fans had built theories the ending could not match ([Grantland](https://grantland.com/hollywood-prospectus/feel-let-down-by-the-true-detective-finale-start-asking-the-right-questions/)).

### The verbs that replace reading

The games that make players feel like detectives share one architecture, which a design essay sums up as many channels out and few channels back: clues arrive through many sources, the player states only a conclusion through one constrained mechanic, and the answer space is large enough to resist guessing ([IndieDB](https://www.indiedb.com/tutorials/detective-game-design-problems)). The table below collects the low-text verbs the notes document. All fit the brief's "solve by doing".

| Verb | Reference game | How the conclusion is checked | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Walk a frozen moment, attribute identities and fates | Return of the Obra Dinn | Confirmed in sets of three | [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Return_of_the_Obra_Dinn) |
| Scrub a shared audio timeline, attach names to voices | Unheard | Count of correct answers | [PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/this-new-detective-game-is-all-about-listening-to-solve-mysteries/) |
| Inspect a still scene, fill slots in an account | The Case of the Golden Idol | "Two or fewer wrong" signal | [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/case-of-the-golden-idol) |
| Spy on fixed routines across a short loop, then intervene | The Sexy Brutale | The death is prevented | [Destructoid](https://www.destructoid.com/try-to-wrap-your-head-around-the-sexy-brutales-temporal-metroidvania-approach/) |
| Carry knowledge across a reset to open places | Outer Wilds | The gate opens only if you know how | [GamesRadar](https://www.gamesradar.com/the-making-of-outer-wilds) |
| Traverse an open space for physical evidence | Paradise Killer | Never confirmed; one fixed truth | [PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/how-a-new-indie-studio-created-one-of-the-best-detective-games-ever) |

Lucas Pope decided late that working out who people are was more enjoyable than how they died ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/for-lucas-pope-i-return-of-the-obra-dinn-i-was-a-bunch-of-appealing-design-problems)); identity attribution is the most text-light deduction verb in the notes, and cloning makes it native to this story. Golden Idol's team found that lived-in scenes full of incidental detail overwhelmed players and cut to necessary clues plus deliberate misdirection, refused per-slot validation, and ran at least five to seven rounds of silently observed outside playtests because authors cannot un-know the answer ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/case-of-the-golden-idol)). L.A. Noire is the counter-model: its coarse truth, doubt and lie prompts left a critic unable to convey a correct theory to the game ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/l-a-noire-s-interrogation-system)). Obra Dinn's early demo with deaths out of order confused players until chapters turned the log into a timeline ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/for-lucas-pope-i-return-of-the-obra-dinn-i-was-a-bunch-of-appealing-design-problems)), so scrambled chronology needs an explicit organising frame.

Knowledge gating gives the sci-fi pillars a second job. In a knowledge-gated game the lock exists from the start and the key is only in the player's head; gates need large input spaces, several clues per piece of knowledge, and tolerance for uneven pacing ([Thinky Games](https://thinkygames.com/features/metroidbrainia-an-in-depth-exploration-of-knowledge-gated-games/)). Outer Wilds also found that a dramatic early event failed because players did not yet know what normal looked like ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/live-die-repeat-how-i-outer-wilds-i-piques-curiosity-in-an-ambivalent-solar-system)). Three limits apply. Outer Wilds carries its story mainly through wall text, so it is a structural model rather than a low-text one, and its widely credited ship log is described in the notes from background knowledge only. Photography, stealth tailing and hands-on forensics, all named in the brief, have no sourced implementation in the notes. And the cited games are short (Unheard runs about three hours), with no source on whether these mechanics scale to a long game.

## Four pillars, each with one price and no stock twist

The acclaimed treatments make the Other different in kind or put the audience inside the copy. Blindsight's aliens converse fluently with no comprehension, and its ship's AI steers the mission through the captain throughout, without malice ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blindsight_(Watts_novel))). Story of Your Life's aliens are not hostile; contact changes how the human who learns their script experiences time ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Story_of_Your_Life)). Three-Body's aliens have a comprehensible motive and the real antagonist is a game-theoretic law ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remembrance_of_Earth%27s_Past)). Hyperion's AIs are a civilisation with factions ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperion_(Simmons_novel))). SOMA holds that copying never transfers the self and leaves the player as the one left behind ([Tampere University Game Research Lab](https://blogs.tuni.fi/playlab/features/the-ending-of-soma-and-why-its-excellent/)). One technology-law academic's blog names four AI clichés: the dumb AI, the forbidden AI, the evil AI with thin motives, and the human-in-a-shell AI ([TechnoLlama](https://technollama.co.uk/why-does-ai-in-science-fiction-so-often-miss-the-mark)). That is a single opinion, though it matches common complaints. The researcher's test for an alien or machine mind is whether its want fits in one sentence and is something other than conquest or becoming human.

Every big idea in the admired works carries one human-sized price. Time travel means revoking a friend's wish; an alien ledger means a schoolgirl's despair is fuel; artificial bodies mean two fathers with identical motives fighting over the same girl ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NieR)). The working rule the notes derive is to define, for each pillar, one relationship it damages and one that only it can save.

Here the notes pull in two directions, and the tension should be resolved deliberately. The anime survey finds the most admired final reveal is an identity collapse between "us" and "them", and suggests the alien or AI antagonist turn out to be of human origin. The trope survey finds that exact family of reveals is what a literate player checks for first. TV Tropes catalogues "the protagonist is the duplicate" as a named twist with a stock setup ([TV Tropes](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/TomatoInThemirror)) and lists "the AI was good all along" and its double reversal as standard operations ([TV Tropes](https://www.tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/PlayingWith/AIIsACrapshoot)). Strange Horizons' editors list dream, simulation and insanity reveals, not-actually-human reveals and thwarted time-travel plans among stories seen too often, in a list they call a curio last updated in 2012 ([Strange Horizons](https://strangehorizons.com/wordpress/submit/fiction-submission-guidelines/stories-weve-seen-too-often/)). The resolution this report recommends is to keep the technique (a reveal that reclassifies what was seen) and drop the specific content: pick at most one identity reveal across the four pillars, disclose the others early in the manner of Never Let Me Go, and spend the story on consequences. TV Tropes does not rank tropes by frequency, so "most common" here means heavily catalogued, and the "why stale" judgements in the table at the end are the researcher's inference unless a source is named.

On originality, the craft sources draw the line at expression. Genre, tropes, structures and techniques are shared material; another writer's prose, specific characters, distinctive world details and dialogue are not ([Kirkus](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/writers-center/writing/inspiration-or-imitation); [Novlr](https://www.novlr.org/the-reading-room/what-is-the-difference-between-plagiarism-and-inspiration)). The working test is whether a reader of both would see two clearly different stories. These are craft guidelines and not legal advice; no legal source was consulted. The advice on subversion is to keep the trope and change one governing rule applied without exception, the way A Song of Ice and Fire keeps fantasy staples but enforces realistic consequences ([Writer's Digest](https://writersdigest.com/write-better-fiction/using-story-tropes-to-subvert-reader-expectations)). Simply flipping the expected outcome is itself a catalogued move.

## Principles and checklist for the writers' room

What follows is synthesis for direct use. Each principle rests on findings cited above; the wording, the numbers in the thread budget and the application to this game are recommendations, not sourced facts.

### Twelve working principles

1. **Write the true history first.** Fix the ending, the full chronology, the fates of the main characters and the convergence order before scripting scenes. Every clue is then a view onto something that already exists.
2. **Three tools, one cap each.** Give each pillar one mechanism, one memorable hard limit and one visible cost. Never break a limit. Leave the physics unexplained and the rules of use strict.
3. **Teach by experiment before it matters.** For every rule: anomaly, harmless test, named rule, personal use, bill. The player should operate each mechanism before it is used as an explanation.
4. **One pillar, one price.** Each of AGI, aliens, cloning and time travel damages one relationship and is the only thing that can save another.
5. **Start together, then scatter.** Open with most of the cast in one place and one shared event. State relationships plainly. Allow at most one thread that begins elsewhere. Budget about three plot lines and four to six active threads.
6. **Thread equals place, look and want.** Each thread has its own location, visual identity and one plainly stated goal. Show a persistent map or board at chapter boundaries. Rotate in short slices and never park a thread for long.
7. **Hide by viewpoint, not by withholding.** The player sees across threads and knows more than any character; surprise comes only from what a given viewpoint honestly does not know. Never let a playable character conceal what they know from the player.
8. **Twists reclassify; they do not add.** The best reveal keeps every fact the player witnessed and changes what it meant. Attack an assumption the player supplied. One answer should close several questions and flip someone's situation in the same moment.
9. **Shocks are consequences.** For each death, betrayal or reversal, list its causes and confirm each appears earlier as an ordinary event. Aim at what the genre protects. Place last-minute warning signs for the alert.
10. **Answer steadily, escalate, hold one design back.** Mid-level answers arrive regularly and each opens a larger question. Limit the final act to one or two frame-breaking reveals. Keep a second engine (suspense, pursuit, an inverted thread) running so no single answer ends the game.
11. **The player states conclusions; the game confirms in batches.** Many clue channels out, one constrained channel back, a large answer space, no per-item confirmation, automatic bookkeeping of what was witnessed and never of what it means. Voice carries emotion and motive, space carries sequence and cause, and text is optional depth.
12. **Earn the ending in proportion.** Resolve the largest, most thematic threat last. Give each payoff screen time proportionate to its setup. Keep time, distance and consequence intact as the pace rises. The win and the cost are one act, followed by a quiet beat, with one small thing left for the player to decide.

### Checklist to run on every draft

**World and rules**
- [ ] The true timeline and each faction's incentives are written down and nobody on the team disputes them.
- [ ] Each mechanism has a stated limit, and no scene violates it.
- [ ] The player uses each mechanism at low stakes before any reveal depends on it.
- [ ] The existence and limits of copies are established early (Knox's "twins" rule).
- [ ] Each alien and machine mind has a one-sentence want that is neither conquest nor becoming human.

**Threads and clarity**
- [ ] A cold playtester can say who is related to whom after the opening.
- [ ] At any moment a playtester can state what each active character wants and what the current question is.
- [ ] No more than about six locations or threads are live in one sitting.
- [ ] Every beat links to the next by "therefore" or "but".
- [ ] The convergence order is fixed, and no new threads have been added since it was fixed.
- [ ] Any time jump was designed in from the start.

**Clues and twists**
- [ ] A dependency table lists every fact, where it is planted and where it pays off.
- [ ] Every fact a solution needs is observable before the answer is demanded, by at least two routes.
- [ ] No coincidence supplies an answer; coincidence only causes trouble.
- [ ] Each twist still works when known (it changes the meaning of earlier scenes).
- [ ] No planted answer has been changed because testers guessed it.
- [ ] Decorative detail in evidence scenes cannot be mistaken for a clue.
- [ ] At least one honest red herring sits among the planted objects.
- [ ] Characters who know and do not tell have an in-story reason.

**Play and text**
- [ ] The critical path is solvable from what is seen and heard, without required reading.
- [ ] The avatar never voices a deduction the player has not entered.
- [ ] Each validation step has an answer space in the hundreds or more.
- [ ] A stuck player can always go somewhere else.
- [ ] Committed choices return "yes, but"; refusal and inaction are respected outcomes.
- [ ] The interface does not moralise.
- [ ] Difficulty was calibrated with silently observed outside testers.
- [ ] Consequences of choices are legible afterwards without a guide.

**Ending and originality**
- [ ] A payoff audit lists every long-running setup and shows the climax using it in a way that needed it.
- [ ] No new power or character appears in the final act to solve the problem.
- [ ] The main antagonist relationship is not removed at the midpoint unless its replacement is at least as personal.
- [ ] The promised external question is resolved, as well as the thematic one.
- [ ] An influence log records, per story element, what technique was borrowed and from where, drawing on several works per pillar.
- [ ] No names, characters, settings, invented terms, signature set pieces or recognisable beat sequences from any single work appear.
- [ ] With each subversion removed, the story still has a point.

### Tropes to avoid, and what to do instead

| Area | Avoid | Fresher direction |
|---|---|---|
| AGI | Uprising or sudden turn | Keep its conduct consistent and legible; put the conflict in human disagreement about it |
| AGI | "It was good all along" reversal | A third position that is neither threat nor guardian, with its own unfinished goals |
| AGI | Literal-minded goal pursuit; wants to be human | Goals revised in the open at a cost; a mind whose difference is the point |
| AGI | Machine god as final boss; conveniently dumb or banned AI | Bounded, specific capability; a participant with limits |
| Aliens | Invasion for resources | A motive that is not about Earth at all |
| Aliens | Ancient astronauts built civilisation | Precursors irrelevant to human origins (this premise also carries a reputational risk if it credits real cultures' achievements to aliens) |
| Aliens | Precursors explain every mystery; aliens secretly behind all human troubles | Leave some questions open; make motives local |
| Cloning | "You are the clone" | Disclose copy status early; ask what copies owe each other |
| Cloning | Which one is the real one; disposable or decaying clone | Both equally continuous and diverging; copies with standing |
| Time travel | Hero caused the loop | Reveal the cause early; the mystery is who else knows |
| Time travel | Fixing the past makes it worse; reset button | A fix that works and has a named owner who pays |
| Time travel | Kill the tyrant; self-erasure to close the loop | Small personal stakes; a cost that is not a tidy death |
| Mystery | Investigator is the culprit | If used, tell the player before the cast and build dread |
| Mystery | Least likely suspect; summation gathering | Several live answers; threads reaching conflicting partial solutions |
| Mystery | Narrator who knows and withholds; culprit who is a minor or late character | Honest mistake by the viewpoint; culprit met early with a motive |
| Reveals | Simulation, dream, insanity; dead all along; two characters are one person at different ages | Reclassify something concrete the player handled |
| Structure | Amnesia as the withholding device | Ignorance by position, not by memory loss |
| Structure | Chosen one; teenage saviours with no adult stakes | Characters shaped by institutions and incentives |
| Structure | Visibly shady ally as traitor; secret organisation or church behind everything | Betrayal as visible consequence; factions with competing aims |
| Structure | Escalation to killing a god; friendship as the mechanism of victory | Victory through understood rules and a chosen cost |
| Structure | Destroyed peaceful hometown opening; contrived child in danger; gifted children in a lab | Establish normality the player comes to know, then disturb it specifically |

The "secret organisation behind everything" item was not discussed directly by any fetched article, and no source lists cloning-specific clichés.

## Conclusion

The brief asks for a story more complex than its models that is still easy to follow, and the research suggests those two aims stop competing once complexity is moved out of individual scenes and into the relationships between them. Each scene stays simple: one event, a few people, one or two facts to establish. The complicated truth is the graph the player builds across scenes, which is how Obra Dinn, Golden Idol and Outer Wilds all work. It also explains why the pillars suit this form better than they suit prose. Time travel justifies scrubbing and loops, cloning justifies identity attribution, an AGI justifies the reconstruction instrument and its honest blind spots, and an alien mind justifies a rule set the player must learn as physics.

The less comfortable implication is about originality. The reveal the admired works are most praised for is now the one audiences test for first, so imitation fails on two counts: it breaks the no-plagiarism rule, and it is predictable. What remains open to this project is the combination nobody in the notes attempted: a sociological, many-threaded story in which the fates are disclosed early and the surprise lies in consequences, delivered almost without reading. Whether deduction-by-doing can sustain that over a long game is the question the research cannot answer, and the first prototype should be built to test it.
