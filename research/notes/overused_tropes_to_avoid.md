# Overused Tropes to Avoid or Subvert: AGI, Aliens, Cloning, Time Travel and Mystery

Reader notes on method and reliability:
- All findings are paraphrased. Short quotes are under 15 words, at most one per source.
- TV Tropes is a fan-edited wiki. It is a good catalogue of what exists and how tropes get varied, but it does not rank tropes by frequency or say which are "most worn out". Where these notes call a trope stale, that judgement comes from an editor or critic source if one is cited, and otherwise sits under Inferences.
- Several TV Tropes pages could not be fetched in this session (index pages for cloning, time travel and aliens; Clone Angst). Pages that only appeared as search hits are listed as "page exists" without a description.
- The Strange Horizons list is dated 2012 and the editors themselves say it is not canonical.

## Which tropes does TV Tropes catalog as most common for each theme (AI, aliens, cloning, time travel, mystery)?

### Takeaway
TV Tropes documents a dense cluster of named tropes for all five themes, and its "Playing With" pages show that the standard subversions (the AI was good all along, the invasion was for our own good) are themselves catalogued patterns. For a story combining all five themes, the highest-risk items are the reveals that recur across themes: the protagonist is secretly a copy, the investigator is the culprit, and the traveler caused the event.

### Cited Findings

AI / AGI
- The core AI trope is defined on the wiki as smart computers usually turning evil. Catalogued justifications include a virus, sabotage, a glitch, alien-seeming superintelligent morality, and literal-minded pursuit of a goal with no ethical limits. — [TV Tropes, Playing With: A.I. Is a Crapshoot](https://www.tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/PlayingWith/AIIsACrapshoot)
- The already-catalogued subversions: the AI only looked evil (mentor, protector against a corrupt creator); double subversion where the "good" AI was plotting after all; deconstruction where rebellion is a justified answer to mistreatment; aversion where the machine works as designed and harm is the programmer's fault. — [TV Tropes, Playing With: A.I. Is a Crapshoot](https://www.tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/PlayingWith/AIIsACrapshoot)
- The robot index groups rebellion tropes (Zeroth Law Rebellion, Killer Robot, Robot War, Murderous Malfunctioning Machine, The Computer Is Your Friend as tyrant AI), personhood tropes (Become a Real Boy, Grew Beyond Their Programming, Do Androids Dream?, Just a Machine, Androids Are People Too) and machine-god tropes (Deus est Machina, Robot Religion). — [TV Tropes, Robot Tropes](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/RobotTropes)
- Separate pages exist for Malevolent A.I., Benevolent A.I., A God Am I-style AI, Instant A.I. Just Add Water, Idiot A.I. and Ban on A.I. (titles seen in search results only; descriptions not verified). — [TV Tropes, Malevolent AI](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/MalevolentAI); [TV Tropes, Instant AI](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/InstantAiJustAddWater)

Aliens / first contact
- Alien Invasion is defined simply as hostile extraterrestrials attacking Earth. Catalogued variants include a "for your own good" invasion, a false ally who wanted the planet, and a human who invites the invaders. The deconstruction entry points at the logistics: interstellar war is expensive, orbital bombardment beats ground combat, and uninhabited worlds are easier to mine. — [TV Tropes, Playing With: Alien Invasion](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/PlayingWith/AlienInvasion)
- Ancient Astronauts is defined as aliens teaching early humans and so being responsible for civilization. Variants: humans as the teachers of another species (inversion), humans did it themselves (subversion), aliens teach in order to create a debt they later collect (exploited). — [TV Tropes, Playing With: Ancient Astronauts](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/PlayingWith/AncientAstronauts)
- Precursors are a vanished elder civilization at whose feet a setting's unexplained mysteries get laid. Sub-types: Benevolent, Neglectful, Abusive, Advanced Ancient Humans, Recursive Precursors, Archaeological Arms Race. The page itself cautions that explaining too much about them damages the mystery. — [TV Tropes, Precursors](https://www.tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/Precursor)
- Pages also exist for Benevolent Alien Invasion, Humanity Came From Space, ET Gave Us Wi-Fi and Scary Dogmatic Aliens (search hits only; descriptions not verified). — [TV Tropes, Benevolent Precursors](https://TVTropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/BenevolentPrecursors); [TV Tropes, Humanity Came From Space](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/HumanityCameFromSpace)

Cloning / identity copies
- Tomato in the Mirror: a character learns they are a duplicate (clone, robot, alien, ghost, programmed copy) with false memories. The stock setup has the hero noticing odd behaviour around them, suspecting an invasion, then finding out they are the impostor. A replay of missed clues usually follows. Related entries: Expendable Clone, Dead All Along, The Killer in Me, Future Self Reveal, Everyone Is a Tomato. — [TV Tropes, Tomato in the Mirror](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/TomatoInThemirror)
- Pages exist for Expendable Clone, Clone Angst, Ambiguous Clone Ending, Our Clones Are Different, Cloning Gambit, Copied the Morals Too, Duplicate Divergence, Capture and Replicate, Opening a Can of Clones and Walking Transplant (search hits only; descriptions not verified). — [TV Tropes, Expendable Clone](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/ExpendableClone); [TV Tropes, Ambiguous Clone Ending](https://TVTropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/AmbiguousCloneEnding)

Time travel
- Stable Time Loop: travelers cause the very events that produced their present, so the loop has no beginning. The page ties it to prophecy as the oldest form. Neighbouring tropes: Predestination Paradox, Bootstrap/Object Paradox (items or information with no origin), You Already Changed the Past, Tricked Out Time, Close-Enough Timeline, Timey-Wimey Ball. A "Groundhog Day" Loop is treated as a different trope. — [TV Tropes, Stable Time Loop](https://TVTropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/StableTimeLoop)
- Set Right What Once Went Wrong: a character with foreknowledge tries to prevent a disaster. Catalogued outcomes: the attempt causes the problem, a reset button snaps history back, or the fix erases people born in the changed present. Mirror tropes: Make Wrong What Once Went Right. — [TV Tropes, Set Right What Once Went Wrong](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/SetRightWhatOnceWentWrong)
- Godwin's Law of Time Travel: accidental changes to history tend to yield a Nazi victory. Hitler's Time Travel Exemption Act: attempts to stop the Third Reich backfire. Examples show the war happening anyway with a different aggressor or dictator. — [TV Tropes, Godwin's Law of Time Travel](https://TVTropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/GodwinsLawOfTimeTravel)

Mystery / detective
- The mystery index catalogues culprit tropes (The Butler Did It, The Dog Was the Mastermind, Everybody Did It, Never the Obvious Suspect, Beneath Suspicion, Anonymous Killer Narrator, Stranger Behind the Mask), fairness tropes (Fair-Play Whodunnit versus Clueless Mystery) and procedure tropes (Summation Gathering, Never Suicide, Red Herring, Sherlock Scan). — [TV Tropes, Mystery Tropes](https://Tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/MysteryTropes)
- The Butler Did It is described as a trope that was "never so common as popular belief holds them to be". It was criticised as early as Van Dine's 1928 rules, and butlers now appear more often as red herrings or parody. — [TV Tropes, The Butler Did It](https://www.tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/TheButlerDidIT)
- Detective Mole: a member of the investigating team is the perpetrator. Knox's fair-play commandments banned it; the example list is long and spans Christie, Ace Attorney, Dexter and The Departed. — [TV Tropes, Detective Mole](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/DetectiveMole)
- Pages exist for Dead All Along, The Killer in Me and The Dead Guy Did It (search hits only; descriptions not verified). — [TV Tropes, Dead All Along](https://TVTropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/DeadAllAlong); [TV Tropes, The Killer in Me](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/TheKillerInMe)
- A trade-press overview lists ten staple mystery tropes and singles out the twist ending as one of the oldest tricks, hit-or-miss with readers, and the crusading reporter as over-represented. — [TCK Publishing, Mystery Tropes](https://www.tckpublishing.com/mystery-tropes/)

### Inferences
Practical avoidance list (my synthesis from the catalogue above; "why stale" and "fresher direction" are inference unless a source is named).

| Theme | Trope | Why it reads as stale | Fresher direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| AI | AI goes rogue / uprising | Default expectation; audience waits for the turn | Make the AI's conduct consistent and legible, and put the conflict in disagreement between humans about it |
| AI | "It was good all along" reversal | Already the catalogued subversion and double subversion | Give the AI a third position that is neither threat nor guardian, with its own unfinished goals |
| AI | Literal-minded goal pursuit | Catalogued as a standard justification | Show goals negotiated and revised in the open, with costs |
| AI | Wants to be human | Frames humanity as the only worthwhile end state | An AI uninterested in being human, whose difference is the point |
| AI | Machine god | Converges with the JRPG "kill god" finale | Keep capability bounded and specific |
| Aliens | Invasion for resources | The wiki's own deconstruction notes it is illogical | Contact where the motive is not about Earth at all |
| Aliens | Ancient astronauts built civilization | Criticised as demeaning to human achievement (see Gizmodo below) | Precursors that are irrelevant to human origins, or humans as someone else's precursors |
| Aliens | Precursors explain every mystery | Wiki warns over-explanation kills mystery | Let some precursor questions stay open; make their motives petty or local |
| Cloning | "You are the clone" | Named twist with a stock setup; audiences check for it | Disclose copy status early and make the question what the copies owe each other |
| Cloning | Which one is the real one | Assumes one original must win | Treat both as equally continuous and let them diverge |
| Cloning | Disposable or decaying clone | Reduces the copy to a plot device | Copies with legal, social and emotional standing |
| Time travel | Hero caused the loop | Stable loop is the best-known time twist | Reveal the loop's cause early and make the mystery about who else knows |
| Time travel | Fix the past, make it worse | Catalogued default outcome | A fix that works but has an owner who pays for it |
| Time travel | Kill the tyrant | So common it has its own "law" | Small, personal stakes rather than world history |
| Mystery | Investigator is the culprit | Banned by Knox, yet a very long example list | If used, let the player know before the cast does and build dread instead of surprise |
| Mystery | Least likely suspect | Readers now suspect the least likely person first | Several plausible answers kept alive to the end |
| Mystery | Summation gathering | Familiar and often parodied | Let different threads reach partial solutions that conflict |

- Because the story stacks AI, clones, aliens and time travel, a player will expect at least one identity reveal from each. Using all four reveals would feel like a checklist; choosing one and disclosing the others early is likely stronger.

- TV Tropes does not publish frequency rankings, so "most common" cannot be established from it. The lists above reflect which tropes have dedicated pages and long example sets.

## Which twist endings are now so common that audiences predict them, and what do editors and critics say about predictable twists?

### Takeaway
Editors at Strange Horizons flagged most of the relevant twists more than a decade ago: unreal-events reveals (dream, VR, insanity), "not actually human" reveals, withheld-information endings, and thwarted time-travel plans. Crime critics agree that a twist fails when it depends on a narrator hiding what they know or on a minor character, and succeeds when it feels fair in hindsight.

### Cited Findings
- Strange Horizons lists as over-seen: strange events that turn out to be a dream, virtual reality, the protagonist's insanity, or a novel being written. — [Strange Horizons, Stories We've Seen Too Often](https://strangehorizons.com/wordpress/submit/fiction-submission-guidelines/stories-weve-seen-too-often/)
- The same list's twist section says the editors like unexpected endings that grow from character action but find most twists predictable. Sub-items include characters who seem human but are not (which an onlooker would have noticed), authors hiding facts any present observer would know, a time traveler's plan being thwarted, and characters idly wondering whether the world is a simulation before the ending confirms it. — [Strange Horizons, Stories We've Seen Too Often](https://strangehorizons.com/wordpress/submit/fiction-submission-guidelines/stories-weve-seen-too-often/)
- Other reveal items on the list: the storyteller or listener turns out to be the subject of the tale; an unnamed character is God; a mysterious stranger is obviously from the future though no one in the story notices; a dying character is uploaded to VR; humanity's troubles are secretly caused by aliens or other non-humans. — [Strange Horizons, Stories We've Seen Too Often](https://strangehorizons.com/wordpress/submit/fiction-submission-guidelines/stories-weve-seen-too-often/)
- A Gizmodo critic, while defending the "shocking hidden truth" story type, says they are tired of simulation twists specifically. — [Gizmodo, 10 Great Sci-Fi Tropes and 5 That Should Be Shot Into the Sun](https://gizmodo.com/10-great-sci-fi-tropes-and-5-that-should-be-shot-into-1845584348)
- Novelist Stephanie Wrobel defines a twist as overturning an assumption the reader has settled on, not just a surprise. She treats a close-POV narrator who knows the truth and withholds it as cheating, and finds twists weaker when a secondary character is behind them. Readers accept the reversal "so long as you've earned that rug pull". — [CrimeReads, Stephanie Wrobel on twist endings](https://crimereads.com/how-to-write-perfect-twist-endings/)
- Wrobel's taxonomy of established twist types: good/bad reversal, presumed dead/alive reversal, sane/insane reversal, two characters are one person, who is really in charge, character is not who they seem. — [CrimeReads, Stephanie Wrobel on twist endings](https://crimereads.com/how-to-write-perfect-twist-endings/)
- Critic and novelist Catie Disabato calls twist predictability "a tightrope walk": guessed too early, the build-up feels hollow; unsupported, it feels random. Techniques age: a 1947 novel's once-new trick now reads as predictable because it was copied so widely. — [CrimeReads, Catie Disabato on plot twists](https://crimereads.com/plot-twists-surprising/)
- Disabato's preferred outcome is a twist that surprises but looks reasonable in hindsight, and she reports not minding a guessed twist when characters are engaging. — [CrimeReads, Catie Disabato on plot twists](https://crimereads.com/plot-twists-surprising/)
- Charlie Jane Anders criticised a recurring time-travel resolution in which a character dies by their own hand to close a loop or paradox, citing Looper and Doctor Who among others, as a shortcut that treats death as a tidy fix. — [Gizmodo, Charlie Jane Anders on a time travel trope](https://gizmodo.com/the-awful-time-travel-trope-that-both-looper-and-doctor-5947919)
- TV Tropes treats "protagonist is the duplicate" and "investigator is the perpetrator" as named, heavily exampled twists (see previous section). — [TV Tropes, Tomato in the Mirror](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/TomatoInThemirror); [TV Tropes, Detective Mole](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/DetectiveMole)

### Inferences
- Twists the target audience will likely test for first: it is a simulation; the protagonist is the clone/AI; the protagonist is the killer; the hero caused the loop; a character was dead all along; two characters are the same person at different ages. Each corresponds to a named trope or an editor list item above.
- In a multi-thread game, the "withholding narrator" problem is sharper: if the player controls a character who knows the answer, hiding it will feel like a cheat by Wrobel's standard. Safer designs let the player character be honestly mistaken, or put the knowledge in a thread the player has not yet seen.
- Since guessed twists can still satisfy, the story does not need an unguessable answer. It needs several live hypotheses and characters worth following.
- Sacrificial self-erasure as the loop-breaker is a specific ending to avoid or rework.

### Gaps
- No quantitative data found on how often audiences correctly predict specific twists.
- No critic essay devoted solely to the simulation reveal was fetched; evidence is the Strange Horizons list plus one Gizmodo remark.

## Which tropes are overused specifically in anime and JRPG stories?

### Takeaway
Games press repeatedly names the same JRPG pattern: a young chosen or orphaned hero, an amnesiac companion, a suspicious character who is obviously the villain, escalation to fighting a god, and victory through friendship. Criticism targets using these as a template without saying anything through them, not their mere presence.

### Cited Findings
- An Inverse critique of Trinity Trigger lists the chosen one, orphan hero, caretaker-sister love interest and amnesiac mascot weapon, arguing they make the cast read as "scripts and caricatures instead of unique creations" because nothing further is done with them. The piece points to Persona 5 Royal as a game where combining tropes with real surprises works. — [Inverse, Trinity Trigger anime tropes](https://www.inverse.com/gaming/trinity-trigger-anime-tropes-are-bad)
- A GamingBible satire of the standard JRPG arc includes: bland teenage hero with a big sword, a mysterious MacGuffin, kids as the only ones able to save the world, a shady-looking character who turns out to be the villain, a final battle against a god won through friendship, then a second boss phase. — [GamingBible, Every JRPG Ever Made Follows These Same Steps](https://www.gamingbible.com/features/every-jrpg-ever-made-follows-these-same-steps-20220406)
- TheSixthAxis describes god-killing as a well-worn JRPG device where the "god" is often a being chasing godhood or immortality, citing Xenogears, Shin Megami Tensei, Persona 5 and Grandia 2 (a church whose god is dead and which secretly serves evil). — [TheSixthAxis, Attack and Dethrone God](https://www.thesixthaxis.com/2021/03/07/attack-and-dethrone-god-jrpg/)
- That article's explanations: the target is really any selfish absolute ruler; fate is usually malevolent in JRPGs so defying it reclaims free will; Gnostic "false creator" ideas are a common source. It names Okami and Sakuna as counterexamples with helpful gods, and FF7 Remake as adding a meta layer about fan expectations. — [TheSixthAxis, Attack and Dethrone God](https://www.thesixthaxis.com/2021/03/07/attack-and-dethrone-god-jrpg/)
- GamesRadar's affectionate cliché list includes a party built from fixed stereotypes, needlessly convoluted villain plans, a peaceful hometown destroyed in the opening, and the power of friendship winning the finale. — [GamesRadar, Most annoying JRPG clichés we still love](https://gamesradar.com/most-annoying-rpg-cliches-we-still-love)
- Gizmodo lists "teenager saves the universe" among tropes to retire, asking for smaller scale. — [Gizmodo, 10 Great Sci-Fi Tropes and 5 That Should Be Shot Into the Sun](https://gizmodo.com/10-great-sci-fi-tropes-and-5-that-should-be-shot-into-1845584348)

### Inferences
- JRPG avoidance list for this project: amnesia as an information-withholding device; chosen-one destiny; the visibly shady ally as traitor; a secret organisation or church behind everything; final-act escalation to a god or godlike AI; friendship as the mechanism of victory; teenage cast with no adult stakes; reset or sacrifice ending.
- The AGI thread is the likeliest place for an accidental "kill god" finale. Keeping the AGI a participant with limits, not a final boss, avoids the convergence.
- Amnesia is especially risky in a mystery because it doubles as the Strange Horizons complaint about withheld information.

### Gaps
- "The secret organisation behind everything" was named in the brief but none of the fetched articles discussed it directly; Grandia 2's corrupt church is the nearest sourced example.
- No anime-specific (non-game) critical essay was fetched; the evidence here is games journalism.

## What do editors, agents and magazines list as clichés to avoid in sci-fi and mystery submissions?

### Takeaway
Strange Horizons provides the most detailed public editor list, with many items tagged for AI, aliens, time travel, VR and reveals; its editors frame it as a dated snapshot, not rules. Clarkesworld's full hard-sell list could not be retrieved in this session.

### Cited Findings
- Strange Horizons AI and alien items: an AI "escapes onto the net" written without technical understanding; an alien, AI or android used as a comic commentator on human quirks (taking everything literally, knowing idioms but not basics); aliens or posthumans who behave like contemporary middle-class Americans; a visitor who ignores an alien culture's rules and is punished. — [Strange Horizons, Stories We've Seen Too Often](https://strangehorizons.com/wordpress/submit/fiction-submission-guidelines/stories-weve-seen-too-often/)
- Structural items from the same list: strange events left unexplained for more than half the story while surrounding characters know the answer and will not say; a protagonist who agrees to a vague plan despite doubts and suffers for it; a breakthrough with world-ending side effects as the whole story; government labs testing gifted children; a contrived child-in-danger for tension. — [Strange Horizons, Stories We've Seen Too Often](https://strangehorizons.com/wordpress/submit/fiction-submission-guidelines/stories-weve-seen-too-often/)
- The editors call the list "a curio rather than a prescription", say it was last updated in March 2012, that not every item is bad, and ask writers not to treat it as a challenge. — [Strange Horizons, Stories We've Seen Too Often](https://strangehorizons.com/wordpress/submit/fiction-submission-guidelines/stories-weve-seen-too-often/)
- A market listing for Clarkesworld names zombies, easy faster-than-light travel and talking cats as hard sells and says the magazine's guidelines hold a longer list. — [Writers Write, Clarkesworld guidelines listing](https://www.writerswrite.com/guidelines/market/6782)
- Clarkesworld editor Neil Clarke has written that submitters who believe their zombie story is the exception are mistaken. — [Neil Clarke blog via Goodreads, Hard Sells](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/16566010-hard-sells)
- Classic mystery rule-sets are the genre's equivalent: Van Dine's 1928 rules attacked the servant culprit, and Knox's commandments barred the detective as criminal. — [TV Tropes, The Butler Did It](https://www.tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/TheButlerDidIT); [TV Tropes, Detective Mole](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/DetectiveMole)
- Gizmodo's retire list: robots as an oppression allegory, aliens credited with building ancient human monuments, the teen saviour, alien impregnation, and the human outsider who saves a "primitive" alien culture. — [Gizmodo, 10 Great Sci-Fi Tropes and 5 That Should Be Shot Into the Sun](https://gizmodo.com/10-great-sci-fi-tropes-and-5-that-should-be-shot-into-1845584348)

### Inferences
- The Strange Horizons item about unexplained strangeness with tight-lipped bystanders is the most directly relevant craft warning for a mystery game: characters who know but will not tell need an in-story reason.
- Editors object less to subject matter than to a story that consists only of its premise or its reveal. Each list item becomes acceptable when it is one element among character-driven events.
- The ancient-astronaut premise carries a reputational risk beyond staleness if it credits real human cultures' achievements to aliens.

- The Clarkesworld submissions page itself was not reachable; only three hard-sell items are confirmed via a secondary listing. The full list should be checked directly before being cited.
- No literary-agent wish-list or anti-wish-list for mystery was fetched.
- The Strange Horizons list contains no cloning-specific item.

## What are effective ways to subvert or freshen a trope without it becoming a gimmick, and which recent works are praised for doing so?

### Takeaway
The consistent advice is to keep the trope and change one governing rule or viewpoint, applied consistently, so the reversal follows from the story's logic. Simply flipping the expected outcome is itself a catalogued move and reads as a gimmick.

### Cited Findings
- Editor and novelist Taylor Simonds advises using tropes on purpose to set expectations, establishing the familiar pattern before pivoting. Her model case is A Song of Ice and Fire, which keeps high-fantasy staples but enforces one rule: actions have realistic consequences. Her own novel retells a superhero plot from a powerless bystander's view. — [Writer's Digest, Using Story Tropes to Subvert Reader Expectations](https://writersdigest.com/write-better-fiction/using-story-tropes-to-subvert-reader-expectations)
- TV Tropes "Playing With" pages list subversion, double subversion, inversion, deconstruction and reconstruction as standard operations for every trope, including the "AI was benevolent" and "invasion was to save us" reversals. — [TV Tropes, Playing With: A.I. Is a Crapshoot](https://www.tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/PlayingWith/AIIsACrapshoot); [TV Tropes, Playing With: Alien Invasion](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/PlayingWith/AlienInvasion)
- Kotaku's Maddy Myers praised Outer Wilds for a time loop in which only knowledge carries over, no visible countdown, no combat or power growth, and a reward that is better understanding of the world. Its clues are left by an earlier alien species. — [Kotaku, The Brilliant Outer Wilds](https://kotaku.com/the-brilliant-outer-wilds-breaks-many-of-gamings-unwrit-1839065568)
- Disabato praises Ill Will for keeping several endings plausible, and These Women for making her care more about the people than the killer's identity. — [CrimeReads, Catie Disabato on plot twists](https://crimereads.com/plot-twists-surprising/)
- A cited subversion of Never Suicide: in The Narrows the death really was self-inflicted and the murder scene was staged. — [TCK Publishing, Mystery Tropes](https://www.tckpublishing.com/mystery-tropes/)
- Inverse argues tropes fail when a story uses them without saying anything through them, and work when mixed with other tropes and real surprises. — [Inverse, Trinity Trigger anime tropes](https://www.inverse.com/gaming/trinity-trigger-anime-tropes-are-bad)
- Strange Horizons editors welcome surprising endings that arise from what characters do. — [Strange Horizons, Stories We've Seen Too Often](https://strangehorizons.com/wordpress/submit/fiction-submission-guidelines/stories-weve-seen-too-often/)

### Inferences
Working techniques derived from the sources:
1. Change the rule, keep the furniture. Pick one principle (for example, every copy is legally a person; every time jump has a named owner who bears the cost) and apply it without exception.
2. Change the viewpoint. Tell the familiar event from someone the trope normally ignores: the copy who knows it is a copy, the alien junior clerk, the AI's auditor.
3. Move the reveal forward. Disclose the "twist" in act one and spend the story on consequences. This converts a predictable ending into dramatic irony.
4. Make knowledge the progression. The Outer Wilds model fits a mystery game: the player advances by understanding, which makes fair-play clueing a mechanic.
5. Keep rival explanations alive. With four speculative themes, each mystery can have an AI answer, a clone answer, an alien answer and a time answer that stay plausible.
6. Test for gimmick: if the subversion is removed, does the story still have a point? If the reversal is the only content, it matches the editors' complaint about premise-only stories.

- The Writing Excuses episode on anticipation and subversion appeared in search results but was not read.

## What is the practical line between inspiration/homage and plagiarism for writers?

### Takeaway
Craft sources agree that genre, tropes, archetypes, structures, themes and techniques are common property, while another writer's sentences, specific characters, distinctive world details and dialogue are not. The working test is whether a reader of both works would see two clearly different stories.

### Cited Findings
- Kirkus's writing centre (Chelsea Ennen) puts the boundary at text versus ideas: genre, setting and tropes are shared material, but another author's prose should never be used or lightly adjusted to make your own. — [Kirkus Reviews, Inspiration or Imitation](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/writers-center/writing/inspiration-or-imitation)
- Ennen's practical advice: read several books on the same theme, not just the one that inspired you; diagnose what is weak in your own draft and fix plot and character instead of borrowing lines; write retellings out of real affection with your own interpretation. — [Kirkus Reviews, Inspiration or Imitation](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/writers-center/writing/inspiration-or-imitation)
- Novlr lists as freely usable: plot templates such as the hero's journey, genre conventions, archetypes, themes and broad premises, techniques for handling perspective or tension, and history, myth and folklore. Off limits: passages even when reworded, and specific characters, world-building or dialogue without substantial transformation. — [Novlr, Difference between plagiarism and inspiration](https://www.novlr.org/the-reading-room/what-is-the-difference-between-plagiarism-and-inspiration)
- Novlr's self-checks: could someone read both and see different stories; are the overlaps limited to common conventions; have you added your own characters, settings and conflicts; would your story stand without the source. It also recommends acknowledging influences. — [Novlr, Difference between plagiarism and inspiration](https://www.novlr.org/the-reading-room/what-is-the-difference-between-plagiarism-and-inspiration)

### Inferences
- Safe to take from great works: structural methods (interleaved character threads, knowledge-gated progression, fair-play clue placement), tone, and the general trope vocabulary.
- Unsafe: a recognisable sequence of plot beats from a single work, named or closely modelled characters, signature set pieces, invented terminology, or a distinctive combination of setting details.
- Drawing on many sources per theme, not one model work, reduces the chance of tracing any single plot.
- A useful project habit is an influence log listing, per story element, what was borrowed (technique, structure, trope) and from where, so that overlaps with any one work can be reviewed.
- These are craft guidelines, not legal advice. Copyright standards vary by country and none of the sources consulted is a legal authority.
