# Worldbuilding and "Iceberg" Lore (Halo as central case study)

Notes on sourcing: every bullet under "Cited Findings" was read from the linked page during this research session. Anything drawn from general knowledge and not confirmed on a fetched page is placed under "Gaps" and labelled as unverified. Direct quotes are kept short and are marked with quotation marks.

## 1. Halo case study: what the deep lore is, how much was visible at first, how Bungie hinted at more, and where later entries stumbled

### Takeaway
Halo's first game shipped with almost none of its deep history explained: a ring, a parasite, an absent builder race, a religious alien alliance and one odd title ("Reclaimer"). The 100,000-year backstory (Precursors, Forerunners, ancient humanity, the Flood's true origin, the Didact and Librarian) was added in layers over twelve years through optional terminals and novels. It worked while the games stayed self-contained and each answer pointed at a further question; it stumbled when Halo 4 and 5 made the campaign plot depend on that side material, and when answers shrank the mystery instead of extending it.

### Cited Findings

**The deep lore (in-universe chronology)**
- The Precursors seeded the galaxy with life, could take any form, and are the nearest thing to gods in Halo canon. They held the Mantle of Responsibility, a duty of guardianship over lesser species, and intended to pass it on. — [Windows Central](https://www.windowscentral.com/halo-timeline-precursors-and-forerunners-betrayal)
- When the Precursors judged the Forerunners arrogant and favoured ancient humanity instead, the Forerunners attacked and nearly exterminated their creators. — [Windows Central](https://www.windowscentral.com/halo-timeline-precursors-and-forerunners-betrayal)
- Surviving Precursors reduced themselves to a dust meant to let them return later; it degraded over millions of years and became the first form of the Flood. So the Flood is the revenge of the murdered creator race, not a random parasite. — [Windows Central](https://www.windowscentral.com/halo-timeline-precursors-and-forerunners-betrayal)
- Ancient humans ran an interstellar empire, met the Flood first (through contaminated domestic animals), found the last Precursor (the Primordial) and brought it to their capital Charum Hakkor; its answers about the Flood drove the questioning scientists to suicide. — [Windows Central](https://www.windowscentral.com/halo-timeline-precursors-and-forerunners-betrayal)
- Humans fleeing the Flood pushed into Forerunner space; the Forerunners read it as invasion, won the war, stripped humanity of technology and exiled the survivors to Earth as hunter-gatherers. The Didact, the Forerunner military commander, questioned the Primordial and kept what it said secret. Most Forerunners then dismissed the Flood as a human hoax. — [Windows Central](https://www.windowscentral.com/halo-timeline-precursors-and-forerunners-betrayal)
- About 100,000 years before the games the Flood returned; the Forerunners fired the Halo array to kill all sentient life and starve the parasite, then reseeded the galaxy (humanity included) from specimens stored on the Ark. — [Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2012/11/06/the-abbreviated-fiction-of-halo.aspx)
- The Didact believed Forerunners alone were fit to guide the galaxy; the Librarian, his wife, believed humanity had a special destiny and worked to preserve it. — [Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2012/11/06/the-abbreviated-fiction-of-halo.aspx)
- The Covenant is a multi-species alliance whose faith holds that the Forerunners became gods by lighting the "Sacred Rings" and that believers can follow them on the Great Journey. The religion rests on misreadings: the Mantle was taken as a right to rule instead of a duty of care, and a terminal on the Ark uses "Great Journey" for the Forerunners' post-firing exile. — [Halopedia, Covenant religion](https://www.halopedia.org/Covenant_religion)
- The truth leaks in-story through heretics: the monitor 343 Guilty Spark tells an Elite what the rings really do, which starts a heretic uprising; the Prophet of Truth's order to purge the Elites causes the Great Schism and an Elite-human alliance. — [Halopedia, Covenant religion](https://www.halopedia.org/Covenant_religion)
- Human "smart" AIs are made by destructively scanning a human brain, last roughly seven years, then accumulate so many internal links that they degrade (rampancy) and effectively think themselves to death. Cortana was made from a flash-cloned copy of Dr Halsey's brain, an illegal act; Halsey's traits surface during Cortana's rampancy in Halo 4. — [Halopedia, Smart AI](https://www.halopedia.org/Smart_artificial_intelligence)
- The SPARTAN-II programme: Halsey abducted gifted children and raised them as augmented soldiers, first to put down colonial rebels and only later to fight the Covenant. The hero's name is a first name plus a candidate number (John-117). These facts come from the novel The Fall of Reach, not from the first game. — [Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2012/11/06/the-abbreviated-fiction-of-halo.aspx)

**How much was visible, and when each layer arrived**
- Halo: Combat Evolved (2001) left its ancient setting unexplained: players did not learn who built the ring, what the parasite really was, or why the hero is addressed as "Reclaimer". — [SUPERJUMP](https://www.superjumpmagazine.com/diminishing-halos-mythology/)
- Bungie's original private answer was that humans and Forerunners were the same species, which is why humanity's existence offended the Covenant. It was only hinted at: the "Reclaimer" title and humans' knack for Forerunner machines. A cut Halo 2 level had the Arbiter find a human skeleton in a Forerunner tomb. — [Halopedia, Human-Forerunner connection](https://www.halopedia.org/Human-Forerunner_connection)
- Halo 3's campaign kept that idea in Guilty Spark's final line, which Jaime Griesemer later described as a reveal that "just points at another mystery." — [Halopedia, Human-Forerunner connection](https://www.halopedia.org/Human-Forerunner_connection)
- Halo 3 (2007) added seven hidden terminals (plus an eighth secret one). They are optional, scattered across three late levels, presented as recovered message logs between the Librarian and the Didact, and show more material on higher difficulty (the rogue AI Mendicant Bias content is mostly Legendary-only). — [Halopedia, Halo 3 terminals](https://halopedia.org/Terminal/Halo_3)
- Frank O'Connor wrote the terminals with Damian Isla and others. — [VICE oral history](https://vice.com/en/article/the-complete-untold-history-of-halo-an-oral-history); [Halopedia, Human-Forerunner connection](https://www.halopedia.org/Human-Forerunner_connection)
- Greg Bear's Forerunner Saga (Cryptum 2011, Primordium 2012, Silentium 2013) set the Forerunner era in full: Forerunners and humans as separate rival species, the ancient human empire, the Primordial, the Didact split into an original and an imprinted copy. It was written alongside Halo 4 and traded ideas with it. — [Halopedia, The Forerunner Saga](https://halopedia.org/The_Forerunner_Saga)
- Supplementary novels carry most of the rest: The Fall of Reach (Spartan origins, Halsey), First Strike (between games 1 and 2), Ghosts of Onyx (Spartan-IIIs), Contact Harvest and The Cole Protocol (early war). — [Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2012/11/06/the-abbreviated-fiction-of-halo.aspx)

**How Bungie's writers actually worked**
- The project began as a multiplayer prototype with no story planned; the universe accreted from many contributors. Joseph Staten described his late-development job as "duct tape" writing, bridging over cut levels. — [VICE oral history](https://vice.com/en/article/the-complete-untold-history-of-halo-an-oral-history)
- Roughly 25 missions were planned and ten shipped; campaign brainstorming only began in December 2000, under a year before release. A level explaining the ring was cut and compressed into a cutscene. — [Halopedia, Development of Halo: CE](https://www.halopedia.org/Development_of_Halo:_Combat_Evolved)
- Staten argued the Covenant needed a power structure and a shared religious motive to explain why many species would guard the ring together. — [VICE oral history](https://vice.com/en/article/the-complete-untold-history-of-halo-an-oral-history)
- Jason Jones designed the Flood for gameplay first and wanted its arrival to reverse the player's idea of who the enemy was; the fiction came after and went through several discarded origins (Covenant bioweapon, a native rite-of-passage disease). — [VICE oral history](https://vice.com/en/article/the-complete-untold-history-of-halo-an-oral-history); [Halopedia, Development of Halo: CE](https://www.halopedia.org/Development_of_Halo:_Combat_Evolved)
- Cortana descends from Durandal, the AI of Bungie's earlier Marathon; an early storyline already had her going power-mad. — [Halopedia, Development of Halo: CE](https://www.halopedia.org/Development_of_Halo:_Combat_Evolved)
- Staten's 2010 GDC Online advice: do not start with plot. Build a place, locations and characters that can host many plots; scripts were written late and heavily rewritten. Game writing is building worlds, most of it for internal readers; huge canons are unwieldy, so give players simple, bounded "pockets" to explore. — [Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/gdc-online-bungie-s-staten-on-building-worlds-not-words)
- Staten's caution from the same talk: the first game's narrow focus meant nobody planned ahead, which fed Halo 2's overreach and its cut final act and cliffhanger. — [Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/gdc-online-bungie-s-staten-on-building-worlds-not-words)
- Halo 2's planned third act (including a fight at the Ark) was cut for production reasons; Marty O'Donnell and Marcus Lehto both called the result jarring or disjointed. — [VICE oral history](https://vice.com/en/article/the-complete-untold-history-of-halo-an-oral-history)
- O'Donnell's distinction: the Flood reveal in game 1 was a real twist; the Arbiter in Halo 2 was a mechanic, not a twist. — [VICE oral history](https://vice.com/en/article/the-complete-untold-history-of-halo-an-oral-history)
- Frank O'Connor observed that opening a series with a grand prophecy makes escalation hard, which is why ODST and Reach returned to ground-level stakes. Paul Bertone called ODST's audio side-story a way of letting players fill gaps. — [VICE oral history](https://vice.com/en/article/the-complete-untold-history-of-halo-an-oral-history)
- Art direction carried lore: Marcus Lehto says the ring was meant to feel vast, dark, sinister and ancient, a tomb for something terrible, placed deliberately at the galaxy's edge with the Milky Way visible in the sky; Forerunner metal should look hundreds of thousands of years old, not newly built. — [VGC, Aug 2026](https://www.videogameschronicle.com/news/out-of-scale-no-mystery-damn-ugly-halos-original-art-director-shares-his-thoughts-on-campaign-evolveds-architecture/)
- Covenant visual language came from sea creatures and shells, with colour coding to signal rank and threat. — [VICE oral history](https://vice.com/en/article/the-complete-untold-history-of-halo-an-oral-history)

**The lore bible**
- The Halo Story Bible began around 2000-2001 (Bungie plus Microsoft writers and novelist Eric Nylund); it reached version 5.0 in late 2001. In 2008-2010 Microsoft paid an outside firm about eighteen months to compile a roughly 2,000-page update; it later became an internal wiki. Greg Bear was handed a watermarked extract. — [Halopedia, Halo Bible](https://www.halopedia.org/Halo_Bible)
- Working rule: bible material that has not been published is not canon and may change. — [Halopedia, Halo Bible](https://www.halopedia.org/Halo_Bible)

**What critics and fans say works**
- The original's gaps were the attraction: an "imagination sandbox" players filled with theories. Cortana functioned as an in-fiction theorist, reasoning from clues as a fan would, so the player was oriented without being told answers. The hero felt like a small piece in a much older game. — [SUPERJUMP](https://www.superjumpmagazine.com/diminishing-halos-mythology/)
- Staten himself held that the draw was play (being a powerful actor in a rich simulation), with the hero deliberately faceless; story was support, not the main course. — [Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/gdc-online-bungie-s-staten-on-building-worlds-not-words)

**Where it stumbled**
- Halo 3 shipped with two contradictory answers to the human-Forerunner question because the campaign and terminal teams worked separately and nobody caught it. 343 later patched this by declaring the terminals edited by a rogue AI, an in-fiction unreliable-narrator fix. — [Halopedia, Human-Forerunner connection](https://www.halopedia.org/Human-Forerunner_connection)
- Halo 4 leaned on novels for the Didact and Halsey; a Kotaku critic argued an instalment must stand alone and that a vague galaxy-scale threat engages less than personal stakes. — [Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/the-problem-with-the-post-bungie-halo-campaigns-1744683465)
- Halo 4's villain backstory sat in the novels and in terminal videos that played in an external app; the Librarian, introduced in side material, arrives in the campaign as an unexplained exposition source. One critic called this "lore overdosing". The same critic praised the Chief-Cortana rampancy thread as the game's emotional core. — [The Controller Crusher (Medium)](https://medium.com/the-controller-crusher/reclaiming-some-sense-a-halo-reclaimer-trilogy-deep-dive-part-1-halo-4-4b9302c27486)
- Explaining the builders made them more human and less strange, and Halo 4 turned the Chief from a pawn into a predestined key piece; official answers were usually weaker than what fans had imagined. — [SUPERJUMP](https://www.superjumpmagazine.com/diminishing-halos-mythology/)
- Halo 5 was criticised as a first act with no contained story, a villain (the Warden) with no clear purpose, protagonists who do not change the outcome, and a Cortana turn whose logic did not hold. — [GamesRadar](https://gamesradar.com/why-halo-5s-story-fails-completely); reception of the Cortana turn was split, with Ars Technica and Kotaku calling it unearned — [Wikipedia, Cortana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cortana_(Halo))
- Halo 4's rampancy arc, by contrast, was widely praised. It is a payoff of a rule (seven-year lifespan) seeded in the 2001 novel. — [Wikipedia, Cortana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cortana_(Halo)); [Halopedia, Smart AI](https://www.halopedia.org/Smart_artificial_intelligence)

### Inferences
- Halo's iceberg was largely built after the tip. The first game had a handful of strong unexplained nouns and a private one-line answer; the 100,000-year history was reverse-engineered to fit those nouns. A writer does not need the whole iceberg on day one, but does need a few load-bearing secrets that later material can honour.
- The best Halo lore has the shape "the thing you fought is the consequence of an ancient crime": Flood = wronged creators; Covenant war = a lie protecting a religion; humanity's weakness = a punishment. Each present-day faction is a downstream effect of deep history. This is directly reusable for an AGI/aliens/cloning/time-travel mystery.
- The pattern that held up: main path self-sufficient, deeper layer optional and gated by effort (hidden terminals, harder difficulty). The pattern that failed: main path needs the optional layer.
- Rampancy shows the value of a hard rule with a clock. A stated limit (seven years) seeded early became the strongest emotional beat eleven years later.
- A guide character who speculates aloud (Cortana) is a cheap way to deliver hypotheses rather than facts, keeping mystery intact while preventing confusion.

### Gaps
- Not confirmed on a fetched page: the Fall of Reach detail that abducted SPARTAN-II children were swapped for short-lived flash clones so families would not notice. This is widely reported lore but I could not source it here; verify on Halopedia's "Flash clone" or SPARTAN-II pages before relying on it.
- Not confirmed: the specific Contact Harvest reveal in which a Forerunner AI tells the Prophets that humans are the Forerunners' chosen heirs (the "reclamation/reclaimer" mistranslation), and the Prophets hide it to keep power. The Halopedia page fetched supports only "the religion rests on misreadings" and that Contact Harvest contains Reclaimer and Mendicant Bias material.
- I found little first-hand material from Jason Jones on story intent beyond the Flood-as-reversal point. A Rolling Stone interview with Staten on influences failed to load. Influence claims (Ringworld, Banks's Culture, Aliens) are only partly sourced: the Halopedia development page confirms Ron Cobb's Alien/Aliens design influence and VICE confirms the Aliens model for Sgt Johnson.
- The GamesRadar Halo 5 piece did not discuss the misleading "Hunt the Truth" marketing or reliance on comics/novels for the squad members; those common complaints are unsourced here.
- No Eurogamer, Polygon or Ars Technica retrospective was successfully read; critic material comes from Kotaku, GamesRadar, SUPERJUMP, Medium and VGC.

## 2. The iceberg principle: Hemingway, Tolkien, Harrison, and unexplained references

### Takeaway
Depth is an effect produced on the page by a few specific techniques: casual, incomplete references made as if everyone already knows; multiple sources that disagree; and real substance behind at least some of it. Hemingway's condition still applies: what you leave out must be something you know, or the reader feels a hole instead of a depth.

### Cited Findings
- Hemingway set out the idea in Death in the Afternoon (1932): a writer who truly knows a thing can omit it and the reader will still feel it, whereas omitting from ignorance leaves gaps. About one-eighth of the iceberg shows. — [Wikipedia, Iceberg theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iceberg_theory)
- Example: in "Hills Like White Elephants" the central subject is never named yet is unmistakable from the tension; in "Big Two-Hearted River" the war is never mentioned. Critics read the method as making readers complete the connections with their own feeling. — [Wikipedia, Iceberg theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iceberg_theory)
- Scholars identify four factors behind Tolkien's "impression of depth": (1) a truly large background (maps, genealogies, a legendarium); (2) passing, incomplete mentions that characters take for granted; (3) multiple accounts with gaps and contradictions, like real records; (4) multiple styles and "hands", including a framing manuscript and untranslated languages. — [Wikipedia, Impression of depth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impression_of_depth_in_The_Lord_of_the_Rings)
- Examples of factor 2: a character recalling ancient armies, another mentioning legendary jewels, a gift horn taken from a named dragon's hoard that is never explained. — [Wikipedia, Impression of depth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impression_of_depth_in_The_Lord_of_the_Rings)
- Tolkien kept contradictions on purpose (two characters each described as the oldest being); scholars argue apparent inconsistencies make readers assume a real, messy history. — [Wikipedia, Impression of depth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impression_of_depth_in_The_Lord_of_the_Rings)
- In a 1963 letter Tolkien credited part of the book's pull to glimpses of a large history behind it, like a far-off city seen through mist, and worried the effect would fail in the older legends themselves unless still further unreachable vistas were opened. — [Wikipedia, Impression of depth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impression_of_depth_in_The_Lord_of_the_Rings)
- Tom Shippey holds that depth is what most separates Tolkien from imitators. Gergely Nagy shows how naming older monsters and heroes during the Shelob fight enlarges a small scene by linking it to ancient feuds. — [Wikipedia, Impression of depth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impression_of_depth_in_The_Lord_of_the_Rings)
- Tolkien admired the same effect in Beowulf, whose digressions into older tales create the illusion of a past stretching into dark antiquity and light the main story by contrast. — [Wikipedia, Impression of depth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impression_of_depth_in_The_Lord_of_the_Rings)
- The effect can be faked and filled in later: Le Guin referred in Earthsea to older tales she had not yet written; Rowling used all four factors on a smaller scale. — [Wikipedia, Impression of depth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impression_of_depth_in_The_Lord_of_the_Rings)
- George R.R. Martin called Middle-earth an iceberg with most of its mass hidden and said his own world began as a thin sheet by comparison; he invented king lists for appendices and the history firmed up as characters mentioned events and readers asked about them. — [Flavorwire](https://www.flavorwire.com/484941/my-editors-head-exploded-george-r-r-martin-on-a-world-of-ice-and-fire-and-what-makes-him-different-from-tolkien-at-92y)
- M. John Harrison (blog post, 27 January 2007) argued that every moment of SF should be "the triumph of writing over worldbuilding." His claims: exhaustive survey is impossible and would give an archive, not a book; economy beats exhaustiveness; words can only imply a world, never encode one; worldbuilding makes readers dependent on one fixed version instead of letting them "read into existence" the text. He targets worldbuilding as an ideology, and concedes its techniques are present in all fiction. — [Harrison post, archived at gwern.net](https://gwern.net/doc/fiction/fantasy/2007-01-27-mjohnharrison-veryafraid-criticismofsffworldbuildingandtolkiensubcreationtheory.html)
- N.K. Jemisin questions the "show only 10%" rule: it frames hidden work as something dangerous, and like any rule it can be broken with skill. — [Jemisin, worldbuilding webinar PDF](https://nkjemisin.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/WDWebinar.pdf)

### Inferences
- Tolkien's letter and the SUPERJUMP critique of Halo describe the same law: a vista explained stops being a vista. Each answer has to open a further unreached distance, or the world visibly shrinks. Griesemer's phrase about a reveal that points to another mystery is the working version.
- Harrison and Tolkien are less opposed than they seem for game writers. Harrison's point (the audience builds the world from implication) is the mechanism by which Tolkien's four factors work. The practical conflict is only over how much hidden material must really exist.
- Le Guin, Martin and early Bungie show a "seed now, write later" method is viable, but Hemingway's caveat applies to anything the plot stands on. For a mystery, the solution and its causal chain must be fully known; ornamental references can be hollow.
- Factor 3 (contradictory sources) is nearly free in a game with terminals, logs and multiple factions, and it suits a mystery about AGI, clones and time travel, where records can be forged, copied or from another timeline.

### Gaps
- I did not read Tolkien's letters directly; the 1963 letter is reported via Wikipedia's summary. The "languages first, stories to give them a home" account of his method is only indirectly supported here (Martin's description of Tolkien as a linguist who cared more about languages and histories than plots).
- Tolkien's fuller statement of the dilemma (telling the untold tales destroys their magic) was only partly covered by the page read.

## 3. How acclaimed worldbuilders work

### Takeaway
The strongest worlds are derived, not listed: pick a small number of premises (an environment, a scarce resource, a founding event) and reason outward to culture, politics and conflict. Game studios add a second principle: put every piece of lore in the mouth of someone inside the world, and deliver it through objects and places.

### Cited Findings

**Frank Herbert (Dune)**
- Started from a theme (suspicion of messiahs and hero worship), then spent years reading religion, psychology, history, linguistics, economics. A 1957 reporting job on Oregon dune-control gave him the desert planet and the ecologist figure. — [Tim O'Reilly, Frank Herbert, ch. 3](https://www.oreilly.com/tim/herbert/ch03.html)
- Built an interlocking ecology: water scarcity rules life; sandworms make the spice; water kills worms, so the planet's two treasures exclude each other. Nothing can change without changing everything. Feudal politics, a manipulative religious order and the spice economy reinforce one another. — [Tim O'Reilly, Frank Herbert, ch. 3](https://www.oreilly.com/tim/herbert/ch03.html)
- Conveyed mostly by implication: many terms go unexplained, the desert language borrows from Arabic to suggest age, and an appendix poses as a later scholarly monograph. — [Tim O'Reilly, Frank Herbert, ch. 3](https://www.oreilly.com/tim/herbert/ch03.html)

**Iain M. Banks (the Culture)**
- In a 1994 essay written outside the novels he reasons from material conditions: space habitats must be self-sufficient, so central control fails; post-scarcity makes work voluntary; conscious Minds are partners. Law is replaced by manners; the worst penalty is a drone that follows a murderer for life. Naming conventions encode origin and chosen identity. He openly admits some cosmology is invented for impressiveness. — [Banks, "A Few Notes on the Culture"](https://theculture.adactio.com/)

**N.K. Jemisin**
- Works macro to micro: planet, continents, climate, ecology; then people. Pick about three sociological traits, at most one speculative element, and one unusual material ("Element X") that should create new problems instead of solving old ones. Religion, family, technology and conflict are then derived. — [Jemisin, worldbuilding webinar PDF](https://nkjemisin.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/WDWebinar.pdf)
- Her "immersion pyramid": the stranger the world, the less you should explain and the more context must carry; familiar worlds can afford frequent explanation but risk infodumps. Readers accept a learning curve until it becomes too steep. — [Jemisin, worldbuilding webinar PDF](https://nkjemisin.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/WDWebinar.pdf)

**Brandon Sanderson**
- The world serves the story; cohesion beats quantity. Go deep on a few elements that touch character and plot. He files notes in a world guide under setting, character and plot. Mistborn grew from one question (what if the dark lord had won?) followed through society and culture. — [Sanderson lecture notes #8, 2025](https://www.brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/worldbuilding-tools-lecture-2025)
- He holds that no one can build a whole world; even Tolkien's roughly twenty years left gaps, and trying is a trap for new writers. — [Popverse](https://www.thepopverse.com/literary-brandon-sanderson-cosmere-worldbuilding-j-r-r-tolkien-lord-of-the-rings/)
- Three laws: (1) you can resolve conflict with magic only as far as the audience understands it; (2) limits matter more than powers; (3) deepen what exists before adding new things. — [Sanderson FAQ](https://faq.brandonsanderson.com/knowledge-base/what-are-sandersons-laws-of-magic/)

**George R.R. Martin**
- Story and character first, history accreted; fans and two fact-checking co-authors catch inconsistencies. — [Flavorwire](https://www.flavorwire.com/484941/my-editors-head-exploded-george-r-r-martin-on-a-world-of-ice-and-fire-and-what-makes-him-different-from-tolkien-at-92y)
- For Elden Ring he wrote the mythos and the history of the demigods before the game's events; FromSoftware then designed the ruined, corrupted versions players meet. — [PlayStation Blog, Miyazaki interview](https://blog.playstation.com/2022/01/28/an-interview-with-fromsoftwares-hidetaka-miyazki/)

**Ursula K. Le Guin**
- In Steering the Craft she names the "expository lump": information delivered as a disguised lecture. Her remedy is to break it small and build the story out of it. — [Helen Lowe, summarising Le Guin](https://helenlowe.info/blog/2022/02/14/on-worldbuilding-beware-the-expository-lump/)
- She seeded Earthsea with references to legends she only wrote later. — [Wikipedia, Impression of depth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impression_of_depth_in_The_Lord_of_the_Rings)

**Hidetaka Miyazaki / FromSoftware**
- Directed artists with a few abstract "image words", organised the world around a handful of themes (gods and knights; chaos and fire; death and decay; ancient dragons), and insisted on dignity and refinement in designs. Many backstories were deliberately left where some players would never find them. — [Dark Souls Design Works interview (Fextralife transcription)](https://darksouls.wiki.fextralife.com/Dark+Souls+1+-+Design+Works+Interview)
- Visual design carries history: scorched knight armour implies what happened to its wearers; worn stairs warn of danger; a creature's body shape tells of its greed. — [Dark Souls Design Works interview](https://darksouls.wiki.fextralife.com/Dark+Souls+1+-+Design+Works+Interview)
- The fragmentary style is linked to his childhood reading of books he could not fully understand, filling gaps by imagination (he described that upbringing to the Guardian in 2015). — [Den of Geek](https://www.denofgeek.com/?p=781317)
- Elden Ring keeps the fragmented method and sense of depth while being more direct about human drama; a faded-gold art style signals a golden age already gone. — [PlayStation Blog](https://blog.playstation.com/2022/01/28/an-interview-with-fromsoftwares-hidetaka-miyazki/)
- Bloodborne: story is uncovered, not told. Item text gives history and motive; streets and locked districts show what the Church did; NPCs voice rival factional views. Its structure turns from gothic beast hunt to cosmic horror. Mechanics (blood as healing, ammunition and risk) carry theme. Weakness: many players miss most of it. — [GDC Game Narrative Review, Bloodborne](https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc2023/GNR/GDCGameNarrativeReview_Bloodborne_CanonCoker.pdf)

**Bethesda (The Elder Scrolls)**
- After Daggerfall the writers adopted a rule that all published lore is written by someone living in the world, so every account is biased and partial and no authorial voice settles disputes. Six mutually exclusive endings of one game were all made canon by an in-fiction event. Michael Kirkbride's working rule was that boring ideas were treated as wrong. — [PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/the-battle-to-control-whats-fact-and-fiction-in-the-elder-scrolls-lore)
- Costs: dense for newcomers, running canon disputes, and fan anger when an in-world book dismissed an earlier description as a scribe's error. — [PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/the-battle-to-control-whats-fact-and-fiction-in-the-elder-scrolls-lore)

**BioWare (Mass Effect codex)**
- Chris Hepler treated codex, planet and weapon text as the believability layer: real planetary science, real research behind weapons, refusal to introduce explanations no earlier technology supported, and entries for unvisitable planets as pure flavour. He wrote for expert readers who would catch errors, kept overlapping entries consistent, and left future plot room. — [Chris Hepler](https://christopherhepler.com/mass-effect-codex-galaxy-map-and-item-descriptions/)

**Bungie (Destiny Grimoire)**
- Destiny 1's lore cards were earned in play but readable only on a website or app; many players never read them and the game left core concepts unexplained. For Destiny 2 world lead Steve Cotton said the lore would move into the game through missions, characters and scannable objects. — [PC Gamer](https://pcgamer.com/destiny-2-will-not-use-grimoire-cards-to-tell-its-story)
- A commentator's lesson: essential story belongs in play; optional outside lore can enrich but must not be required. — [Windows Central](https://www.windowscentral.com/no-grimoire-destiny-2-pros-and-cons)

**Team Cherry (Hollow Knight)**
- They did not design for every player to find everything; assuming someone will find each secret makes a personal discovery feel special. Any breakable wall might hide a joke item or a whole area with a boss. Players find secrets far faster than developers expect. — [Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/the-makers-of-hollow-knight-are-fine-with-players-missi-1828721254)

### Inferences
- Herbert, Banks and Jemisin share a method useful for an SF mystery: choose constraints, then derive. For this project the equivalents might be: what does cloning do to inheritance and identity law; what does a time-travel rule do to record-keeping and religion; what does an AGI want that humans cannot supply. Derived detail feels inevitable; listed detail feels arbitrary.
- Jemisin's "Element X creates problems" and Herbert's mutually exclusive water/spice are the same trick: the world's key resource should contain a built-in contradiction that generates plot.
- The Elder Scrolls rule (no omniscient lore, only in-world authors) is the cheapest way to get Tolkien's factors 3 and 4, and it gives writers a retcon tool for free. Halo reached for the same tool after the fact with its "edited terminals" fix.
- Martin plus FromSoftware is a clean division of labour: one party writes the golden-age history in full; another shows only its wreckage. The player sees consequences and infers causes.

### Gaps
- No primary Le Guin essay was read; her views are via a secondary blog and Wikipedia.
- Miyazaki's own words on item descriptions specifically were not found on a fetched page; the Den of Geek piece relies partly on a fan's reading of the 2015 Guardian interview, which I did not read directly.
- Elder Scrolls "Dragon Breaks" by that name, Destiny's Books of Sorrow, and Hollow Knight's lore-writing process (as opposed to secret-hiding) were not covered by the sources read.
- I could not find a source for Sanderson's "hollow iceberg" phrasing (the idea that you build the tip and only enough beneath to imply the rest). The 2025 lecture notes and the Popverse report support the underlying claim (full worlds are impossible; build what plot and character need; let readers sense vastness) but not the term. Check the lecture video itself.

## 4. What makes lore compelling instead of homework

### Takeaway
Lore pulls when it is discovered through play, argued over by in-world voices, causally tied to the present danger, and left with a further horizon. It becomes homework when it is required reading, delivered outside the experience, or when the official answer is smaller than the question.

### Cited Findings
- Pull versus push: story embedded in a scene for the player to infer (a body, fallen rock, a shaft of light) invests players more than story told at them, partly because each player reads the clues through their own experience. Players must be able to miss things for finding them to mean anything. — [Nieman Storyboard on Smith and Worch's GDC 2010 talk](https://niemanstoryboard.org/2011/01/14/harvey-smith-on-environmental-storytelling-and-embedding-narrative/)
- The player as archaeologist: Souls games drip hints through items, places and loading text without closing the case, and theory-building becomes a reason to keep playing. Series that tried to connect every dot (Resident Evil, Metal Gear Solid are the critic's examples) got worse. — [VideoGamer](https://www.videogamer.com/features/lore-and-order-how-bloodborne-and-dark-souls-get-storytelling-right)
- Contradictory in-world sources keep conversation alive; a fixed authorial voice ends it. — [PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/the-battle-to-control-whats-fact-and-fiction-in-the-elder-scrolls-lore)
- Unanswered questions as fuel: Halo's early gaps produced fan theorising, and fan theory videos still partly restore the mystery. — [SUPERJUMP](https://www.superjumpmagazine.com/diminishing-halos-mythology/)
- History with present consequences: the Flood as the remnant of murdered creators; humanity's primitive state as a sentence imposed after a lost war. — [Windows Central](https://www.windowscentral.com/halo-timeline-precursors-and-forerunners-betrayal)
- A founding misunderstanding: the Covenant mistakes a weapon for a path to godhood and a duty for an entitlement; the truth, once out, splits the alliance. — [Halopedia, Covenant religion](https://www.halopedia.org/Covenant_religion)
- Factions with reasons: Staten insisted a multi-species alliance needed a structure and a shared belief to explain its behaviour. — [VICE oral history](https://vice.com/en/article/the-complete-untold-history-of-halo-an-oral-history)
- Deep time and ruin: Tolkien's world is full of ruins, relics and half-remembered songs; Elden Ring's look signals a golden age that has passed; Lehto wanted Halo's ring to read as a tomb. — [Wikipedia, Impression of depth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impression_of_depth_in_The_Lord_of_the_Rings); [PlayStation Blog](https://blog.playstation.com/2022/01/28/an-interview-with-fromsoftwares-hidetaka-miyazki/); [VGC](https://www.videogameschronicle.com/news/out-of-scale-no-mystery-damn-ugly-halos-original-art-director-shares-his-thoughts-on-campaign-evolveds-architecture/)
- Rules with costs: limits generate tension and stop powers becoming shortcuts. — [Sanderson FAQ](https://faq.brandonsanderson.com/knowledge-base/what-are-sandersons-laws-of-magic/)
- Personal stakes beat cosmic ones: audiences shrug at a destroyed planet they never knew and care about one character's fight. — [Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/the-problem-with-the-post-bungie-halo-campaigns-1744683465)
- Homework test: Destiny 1 players often did not understand what they were doing until they read a card afterwards, outside the game. — [Windows Central](https://www.windowscentral.com/no-grimoire-destiny-2-pros-and-cons)

### Inferences
- A workable checklist for each lore item: Does it explain something the player can see or fight right now? Is it voiced by someone with a motive? Does it contradict or complicate another source? Does it leave one further question? If none apply, cut it.
- "Founding lie" is the highest-yield device in this list for a mystery game, because uncovering it is both the lore payoff and the plot twist, and it gives every faction a rational reason to behave as it does before the reveal.
- Mysteries come in two classes (see next section's Anders point): background texture that may stay unexplained forever, and central questions the story has promised to answer. Mixing them up produces either cheated players or shrunken worlds.

### Gaps
- No quantitative data (telemetry on how many players read terminals or item text) was found. Claims that players skip lore are qualitative.

## 5. Hard versus soft worldbuilding, and rules that support fair mystery twists

### Takeaway
Anything the plot uses to solve a problem or spring a twist must run on rules the audience already holds; anything meant only to produce awe can stay vague, provided it causes trouble instead of fixing it. A mystery story can therefore be hard at its core (time travel, cloning, AI limits) and soft at its edges (aliens, deep past).

### Cited Findings
- Sanderson's first law places systems on a continuum. Soft (Tolkien, Martin): limits are vague, wonder is high, so magic should create problems or atmosphere, not solutions. Hard (Asimov's robot laws): explicit rules let characters be clever, and rules drive twists and climaxes. Hard does not need a scientific explanation, only clarity about what can be done. — [Sanderson's First Law essay (archived)](https://www.gwern.net/docs/www/www.brandonsanderson.com/c1641e4b726a642d6c2b096240eeb25a38d9c58e.html)
- Rowling is his mixed case: rules are firm within each book where the climax needs them, looser across the series. His own systems are mostly hard with hidden rules revealed gradually, because he prefers discovery to mysticism. — [Sanderson's First Law essay (archived)](https://www.gwern.net/docs/www/www.brandonsanderson.com/c1641e4b726a642d6c2b096240eeb25a38d9c58e.html)
- Solving a conflict with a power the audience was not shown feels contrived; the practical test for hard systems is to ask how characters could solve the problem with what they already know and have. — [Sanderson's First Law essay (archived)](https://www.gwern.net/docs/www/www.brandonsanderson.com/c1641e4b726a642d6c2b096240eeb25a38d9c58e.html)
- A different use of the same words: "hard" worldbuilding as planned in advance (consistent, suits complex settings, risk of rigidity) and "soft" as improvised (efficient, surprising, risk of painting into a corner and of a world that exists only around the protagonists). Most writers blend, and serial works need a stable base. — [World Anvil](https://www.worldanvil.com/academy/hard-versus-soft-worldbuilding)
- Charlie Jane Anders (Gizmodo, 26 Feb 2010): the midichlorian backlash was not about explanation as such; the new explanation contradicted the clear earlier definition of the Force. And a distinction: Star Wars never posed the Force as a puzzle, while Lost made the island its central question, so Lost owed an answer consistent with what it had set up. — [Gizmodo](https://gizmodo.com/the-real-problem-with-midichlorians-5478314)
- Internal logic breaches are noticed even in deliberately obscure games: a reviewer faults Bloodborne where dream realms persist after their hosts die. — [GDC Game Narrative Review, Bloodborne](https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc2023/GNR/GDCGameNarrativeReview_Bloodborne_CanonCoker.pdf)
- Halo example of a hard rule paying off: the seven-year AI lifespan stated in the novels drives Halo 4's best-received storyline. — [Halopedia, Smart AI](https://www.halopedia.org/Smart_artificial_intelligence); [Wikipedia, Cortana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cortana_(Halo))

### Inferences
- For a mystery with time travel, cloning and AGI: state each mechanism's limits and costs early and in play (one clear demonstration each), then build twists only from combinations of those shown rules. A fair twist is one a player could have derived.
- Keep the aliens and the deepest past soft. They supply scale and dread and should complicate matters, never rescue anyone.
- Sanderson's "hidden rules revealed gradually" is the bridge between hard systems and mystery: the rule existed and left evidence before it was named. This differs from inventing a rule at the reveal.
- Anders's distinction gives a planning step: list every open question and tag it "promised" (must be answered, consistently) or "texture" (may stay open). Do not let marketing or early scenes promote texture into a promise by accident.

### Gaps
- I did not find a dedicated source on fair-play conventions of detective fiction (e.g. Knox's rules) applied to speculative settings; another researcher may cover that.
- The "hard/soft worldbuilding" framing popularised in video essays about Studio Ghibli was not read; the World Anvil piece uses the terms for planning style, which is a different axis from Sanderson's. Writers should say which meaning they intend.

## 6. Delivering lore with little reading

### Takeaway
The lowest-text channels are place, object, sound and behaviour: scale and age of architecture, what the sky shows, what enemies look like and say, what items physically are. Text, when used, should be short, optional, found in the world, and written by someone inside it.

### Cited Findings
- Environmental storytelling principles (Smith and Worch, GDC 2010): each prop should mean something alone and as part of a larger picture; settings should draw on the game's main premise; a scene with no particular meaning is a wasted chance to say something about the world; meaning forms in the gaps, as between comic panels. — [Nieman Storyboard](https://niemanstoryboard.org/2011/01/14/harvey-smith-on-environmental-storytelling-and-embedding-narrative/)
- Space shapes the player's sense of role: traces of violence forecast conflict; a maze makes the player feel like a test animal. — [Nieman Storyboard](https://niemanstoryboard.org/2011/01/14/harvey-smith-on-environmental-storytelling-and-embedding-narrative/)
- Architecture and skybox as lore in Halo: scale, darkness, weathered ancient metal, and the home galaxy hanging small in the sky tell the player the ring is old, remote and dangerous before a word is spoken. — [VGC](https://www.videogameschronicle.com/news/out-of-scale-no-mystery-damn-ugly-halos-original-art-director-shares-his-thoughts-on-campaign-evolveds-architecture/)
- Faction design as lore: Covenant shapes from marine life, colour signalling rank; Forerunner style developed while building a specific level. — [VICE oral history](https://vice.com/en/article/the-complete-untold-history-of-halo-an-oral-history); [Halopedia, Development of Halo: CE](https://www.halopedia.org/Development_of_Halo:_Combat_Evolved)
- Enemy and equipment design as history in Dark Souls: burnt armour, a body warped by appetite, deliberately jerky animation suggesting a human forcing a transformation. Art direction started from mood words such as sadness or cold. — [Dark Souls Design Works interview](https://darksouls.wiki.fextralife.com/Dark+Souls+1+-+Design+Works+Interview)
- Audio: Halo used many randomised line variants for marines; ODST carried a parallel story as found audio; Halo 3 terminals play reversed speech; Elden Ring's choir sings invented syllables, so even the music withholds meaning. — [VICE oral history](https://vice.com/en/article/the-complete-untold-history-of-halo-an-oral-history); [Halopedia, Halo 3 terminals](https://halopedia.org/Terminal/Halo_3); [Den of Geek](https://www.denofgeek.com/?p=781317)
- Optional terminals done well: few in number (seven), hidden off the main path, framed as recovered correspondence with translation brackets, deeper content gated behind difficulty. — [Halopedia, Halo 3 terminals](https://halopedia.org/Terminal/Halo_3)
- Mechanics as lore: Bloodborne's healing, ammunition and risk systems all run on blood, stating the theme through play. — [GDC Game Narrative Review, Bloodborne](https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc2023/GNR/GDCGameNarrativeReview_Bloodborne_CanonCoker.pdf)
- Short reference text can be flavour with no gameplay cost: codex and planet entries for places never visited. — [Chris Hepler](https://christopherhepler.com/mass-effect-codex-galaxy-map-and-item-descriptions/)
- Sensory cues let the audience infer rules without a lecture (the look of spice, the taste of burned metal). — [Sanderson lecture notes #8](https://www.brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/worldbuilding-tools-lecture-2025)
- Things seen and never explained: Tolkien's untranslated runes and the unexplained hoard-horn; Herbert's undefined terms. — [Wikipedia, Impression of depth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impression_of_depth_in_The_Lord_of_the_Rings); [Tim O'Reilly, Frank Herbert, ch. 3](https://www.oreilly.com/tim/herbert/ch03.html)
- The contrast case: text-heavy games that halt play for reading, and expository cutscenes, spell the story out instead of making discovery part of play. — [VideoGamer](https://www.videogamer.com/features/lore-and-order-how-bloodborne-and-dark-souls-get-storytelling-right)

### Inferences
- A useful budget rule drawn from these cases: every lore fact should first be attempted as (1) a place, (2) an object or enemy, (3) a sound or overheard line, and only then (4) a short in-world text. A fact that survives to (4) should fit in a few sentences and have an author with a bias.
- Gating depth by effort (hidden location, higher difficulty, second playthrough) lets the same game serve players who want none of the lore and players who want all of it.
- A speculating companion (Cortana) and factional NPC barks deliver interpretation through voice at zero reading cost.

### Gaps
- The Smith/Worch talk was read via a journalist's summary, not the slides or video.
- No source was found here on Hollow Knight's or Bloodborne's specific use of skyboxes or background art.

## 7. Common worldbuilding failures

### Takeaway
The recurring failures are structural, not stylistic: story that needs side material, answers that contradict or shrink what came before, parallel teams canonising different truths, and worlds built so long that nothing ships or so thin that they exist only around the hero.

### Cited Findings
- Lore dumps: Le Guin's "expository lump". Genre fiction is most at risk because readers lack background, and researchers are tempted to show all their work. Test: if the story works without the detail, drop it. — [Helen Lowe on Le Guin](https://helenlowe.info/blog/2022/02/14/on-worldbuilding-beware-the-expository-lump/)
- Even Bungie had one: Staten nicknamed a character "Captain Exposition" for an explanatory cutscene that replaced a cut level. — [Halopedia, Development of Halo: CE](https://www.halopedia.org/Development_of_Halo:_Combat_Evolved)
- Worldbuilder's disease: Sanderson's term for building so long that writing stalls. Cures: keep the world in service of the story, set deadlines, iterate. — [Sanderson lecture notes #8](https://www.brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/worldbuilding-tools-lecture-2025)
- The opposite failure: a world that reaches only as far as the protagonists, so actions have no wider consequence; and improvised worlds that corner the writer into convenient fixes. — [World Anvil](https://www.worldanvil.com/academy/hard-versus-soft-worldbuilding)
- Plot hidden in side material: Halo 4's villain and a key ally are explained in novels and external videos; Destiny 1's core concepts sat on a website. — [The Controller Crusher (Medium)](https://medium.com/the-controller-crusher/reclaiming-some-sense-a-halo-reclaimer-trilogy-deep-dive-part-1-halo-4-4b9302c27486); [Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/the-problem-with-the-post-bungie-halo-campaigns-1744683465); [Windows Central](https://www.windowscentral.com/no-grimoire-destiny-2-pros-and-cons)
- Over-explaining: answers treated as fixed canon were usually less interesting than fan theories, and commercial pressure to mine a franchise leaves audiences knowing too much. — [SUPERJUMP](https://www.superjumpmagazine.com/diminishing-halos-mythology/)
- Bad explanation, as distinct from explanation: an answer that contradicts the established definition draws backlash; withholding a promised answer is no substitute for a good one. — [Gizmodo](https://gizmodo.com/the-real-problem-with-midichlorians-5478314)
- Retcons of character: recasting an established mentor as villain and a beloved companion as would-be dictator retroactively taints earlier heroism. — [Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/the-problem-with-the-post-bungie-halo-campaigns-1744683465)
- Retcon by fiat: calling an earlier description a transcription error angered Elder Scrolls fans. — [PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/the-battle-to-control-whats-fact-and-fiction-in-the-elder-scrolls-lore)
- Team silos: Halo 3's campaign and terminals shipped incompatible origins because separate teams did not cross-check. — [Halopedia, Human-Forerunner connection](https://www.halopedia.org/Human-Forerunner_connection)
- Escalation trap: starting with prophecy-scale stakes leaves nowhere to build. — [VICE oral history](https://vice.com/en/article/the-complete-untold-history-of-halo-an-oral-history)
- Unfinished arcs: an instalment that only sets up later ones, and a cliffhanger without enough resolution of its own, both drew lasting criticism. — [GamesRadar](https://gamesradar.com/why-halo-5s-story-fails-completely); [VICE oral history](https://vice.com/en/article/the-complete-untold-history-of-halo-an-oral-history)
- Too hidden: with fully optional, non-linear lore many players miss most of the story. — [GDC Game Narrative Review, Bloodborne](https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc2023/GNR/GDCGameNarrativeReview_Bloodborne_CanonCoker.pdf)
- Harrison's deeper objection: exhaustive worldbuilding treats a story as an instruction manual and the reader as a passive recipient. — [Harrison post, archived](https://gwern.net/doc/fiction/fantasy/2007-01-27-mjohnharrison-veryafraid-criticismofsffworldbuildingandtolkiensubcreationtheory.html)

### Inferences
- The single most relevant failure for an interwoven multi-thread mystery is the Halo 4 one: each thread's plot must be followable from what is on screen in that thread. Cross-thread and optional material should add meaning, never supply a missing premise.
- Two retcon grades emerge: an in-fiction unreliable source (accepted, even enjoyed) versus an out-of-fiction correction that contradicts what players saw (resented). Design sources to be fallible from the start so later corrections are in-world events.
- The Bloodborne weakness sets the lower bound: a spine of plot must be unmissable even when the depth is optional.

### Gaps
- No source read here documents backlash to a fully explained mystery in a mystery game specifically; the examples are from film, TV and action games.
- The Prometheus/space-jockey case was found in search results but not read.

## 8. Practical methods: how much to build, building outward, timelines, factions, and the lore bible

### Takeaway
Build the load-bearing causes completely, derive the rest outward from the conflict and a few premises, and write down only what has been shown as canon. Keep one owned document, treat unpublished material as provisional, and route every team's lore through the same check.

### Cited Findings
- Place before plot: build setting, locations and characters that can host many stories; write scripts late and expect rewrites. — [Game Developer on Staten](https://gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/gdc-online-bungie-s-staten-on-building-worlds-not-words)
- Leave deliberate "white space" in the universe for other teams and later stories. — [VICE oral history](https://vice.com/en/article/the-complete-untold-history-of-halo-an-oral-history)
- Give players and collaborators bounded pockets with simple rules instead of the whole canon. — [Game Developer on Staten](https://gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/gdc-online-bungie-s-staten-on-building-worlds-not-words)
- Writers must make the whole team feel ownership of story and write within the game's real strengths and limits; most of the team never reads the script. — [Game Developer on Staten](https://gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/gdc-online-bungie-s-staten-on-building-worlds-not-words)
- How much to build: only what touches character and plot, a few elements in depth. — [Sanderson lecture notes #8](https://www.brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/worldbuilding-tools-lecture-2025); [Popverse](https://www.thepopverse.com/literary-brandon-sanderson-cosmere-worldbuilding-j-r-r-tolkien-lord-of-the-rings/)
- Ratio example from a working novelist: several whole histories read to produce four or five sentences on the page. — [Helen Lowe](https://helenlowe.info/blog/2022/02/14/on-worldbuilding-beware-the-expository-lump/)
- Derivation order: environment, then three social traits, one speculative element, one problem-making resource, then everything else follows. — [Jemisin webinar PDF](https://nkjemisin.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/WDWebinar.pdf)
- Start from one question and follow consequences through society (Mistborn); start from a theme and build an interlocked system (Dune). — [Sanderson lecture notes #8](https://www.brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/worldbuilding-tools-lecture-2025); [Tim O'Reilly, Frank Herbert, ch. 3](https://www.oreilly.com/tim/herbert/ch03.html)
- Timelines by accretion: Martin began with names and dates for appendices and thickened them as the story referred back. — [Flavorwire](https://www.flavorwire.com/484941/my-editors-head-exploded-george-r-r-martin-on-a-world-of-ice-and-fire-and-what-makes-him-different-from-tolkien-at-92y)
- Gameplay-first, fiction-after can work: the Flood was a combat design before it had a history, and went through three origins. — [Halopedia, Development of Halo: CE](https://www.halopedia.org/Development_of_Halo:_Combat_Evolved)
- Faction design: give each a power structure and a belief that explains its conduct; give each a readable visual vocabulary. — [VICE oral history](https://vice.com/en/article/the-complete-untold-history-of-halo-an-oral-history)
- Factions should voice views of one another, so the player triangulates. — [GDC Game Narrative Review, Bloodborne](https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc2023/GNR/GDCGameNarrativeReview_Bloodborne_CanonCoker.pdf)
- Lore bible practice (Halo): named stewards; versioned document; unpublished entries are apocrypha; measurable facts taken from shipped game assets are kept; when sources clash, reconcile and if needed write new connective story. — [Halopedia, Halo Bible](https://www.halopedia.org/Halo_Bible)
- Consistency practice (Mass Effect): never introduce a technology no earlier entry supports; keep overlapping entries aligned; leave room for future plots; assume expert readers will check. — [Chris Hepler](https://christopherhepler.com/mass-effect-codex-galaxy-map-and-item-descriptions/)
- External checkers help: Martin relies on two fan-scholars to catch errors. — [Flavorwire](https://www.flavorwire.com/484941/my-editors-head-exploded-george-r-r-martin-on-a-world-of-ice-and-fire-and-what-makes-him-different-from-tolkien-at-92y)
- Planning ahead matters for series: no forward plan after game one fed the sequel's troubles. — [Game Developer on Staten](https://gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/gdc-online-bungie-s-staten-on-building-worlds-not-words)

### Inferences
Suggested working procedure for the mystery project, assembled from the above (my synthesis, not a sourced method):
1. Write the hidden truth first, as a causal chain: the ancient or founding event, who lied about it and why, and how that produced each present-day faction and danger. This is the part Hemingway says must be known.
2. Set three or four hard rules with costs and clocks (for example an AI lifespan, a cloning defect, a time-travel constraint). Each must be demonstrated in play before any twist uses it.
3. Derive factions from the lie: for each, what it believes, what it wants, what it would lose if the truth came out, and how it looks and sounds.
4. Keep a two-column timeline: what happened, and what each faction believes happened. Differences between the columns are the game's mysteries.
5. Tag every open question "promised" or "texture". Promised questions get answers that are consistent and that each open one further horizon. Texture stays open.
6. For each character thread, confirm the plot is followable from that thread alone. Cross-thread echoes and optional finds add meaning only.
7. Assign each lore fact a delivery channel in the order place, object or enemy, sound, short in-world text; give every text an in-world author and bias.
8. Lore bible rules: one owner; shown-in-game is canon, the rest is provisional; every team's content checked against it; sources written as fallible so corrections can happen in-fiction.
9. Leave white space on purpose: named events, places and people that are referred to and never shown.

- The Halo record suggests a rough proportion for a first game: a handful of proper nouns and one visible reversal on the surface, a one-paragraph private answer beneath, and room to grow. Depth was perceived long before it was written.

### Gaps
- No source read gives a tested numerical ratio of built to shown material; "one-eighth" and "10%" are metaphors.
- I found no public template of an actual studio lore bible; the Halo account describes its history and governance, not its page layout.
- Tools and formats for tracking multi-timeline continuity (relevant to time travel) were not covered by any source read.
