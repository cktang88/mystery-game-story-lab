# Game of Thrones / A Song of Ice and Fire: multi-thread structure

Scope note: every cited finding below comes from a page that was actually fetched in this pass. Several intended sources could not be fetched (A Wiki of Ice and Fire POV pages, English Wikipedia's A Feast for Crows page, both Rolling Stone interviews, the PNAS copy on PubMed Central). Where that left a hole, it is listed under Gaps rather than filled from memory. Items under Inferences are my reasoning, not sourced fact.

## 1. How many POV characters and parallel storylines run, and how are they introduced?

### Takeaway
The books begin with nine viewpoint characters, nearly all in one castle, and grow to 31 by book five; the whole thing hangs on only three large plot lines. Martin says he copied the "start small and together, then scatter" shape from Tolkien on purpose.

### Cited Findings
- The series has three interwoven plots: a dynastic war among families for Westeros, the rising supernatural threat from the far north, and an exiled king's daughter trying to return from Essos to claim the throne. — [Wikiwand: A Song of Ice and Fire](https://wikiwand.com/en/A_Song_of_Ice_and_Fire)
- The viewpoint cast grows from nine characters in book one to 31 by book five. — [Wikiwand: A Song of Ice and Fire](https://wikiwand.com/en/A_Song_of_Ice_and_Fire); the same nine-to-31 figure was put to Martin by the NPR host in 2019, and Martin replied that he has not counted and manages them with growing difficulty — [NPR transcript, 2019](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/771377681)
- A network-science study of the five books counts differently: 24 POV characters, of whom 14 are "major" (eight or more chapters). — [Gessey-Jones et al., arXiv copy of the PNAS paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.01783.pdf). This differs from the 31 figure above; the two sources do not explain the difference (see Inferences).
- The same study covers 343 chapters and identifies 2,007 named characters, 1,806 of whom interact with at least one other. Characters per chapter run from 7 in the first chapter up to 89 in one book-four chapter, settling around 35 after book one. — [arXiv/PNAS study](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.01783.pdf)
- Martin says he took his narrative shape from The Lord of the Rings, which opens small in the Shire, widens, and then splits the Fellowship. In his own story nearly everyone begins at Winterfell (Daenerys is the exception) and is then spread across the world. — [Winter is Coming, relaying IGN coverage of Martin's remarks, May 2019](https://winteriscoming.net/2019/05/14/george-r-r-martin-on-what-he-borrowed-from-j-r-r-tolkien)
- The same article notes that in the books the characters mostly remain apart, while on TV most had come back together by the end. — [Winter is Coming](https://winteriscoming.net/2019/05/14/george-r-r-martin-on-what-he-borrowed-from-j-r-r-tolkien)
- The TV pilot had to introduce, in one episode: the White Walkers, the Wall and Night's Watch, the Stark family and Jon Snow's place in it, Theon, Jon Arryn's death, the Lannisters, Viserys and Daenerys in exile, Khal Drogo, Jorah, and Westeros itself. Winterfell serves as the hub, with the king's trip north bringing the second family to the first. — [The Ringer, pilot retrospective](https://www.theringer.com/game-of-thrones/2021/4/16/22386842/game-of-thrones-pilot-retrospective-anniversary)
- The project grew far past its plan: Martin first thought it might be a short story or novella, sold it as a trilogy, and was at work on the sixth book of that "trilogy" by 2013. — [Wheeler Centre](https://www.wheelercentre.com/news-stories/2013/architects-gardeners-and-game-of-thrones)

### Inferences
- The 31 vs 24 gap is probably a counting-rule difference (for example whether one-off prologue and epilogue narrators are counted), but no fetched source states this.
- "Three plot lines, nine viewpoints, one starting location" is the real on-ramp. The audience meets most of the cast as one household, learns their relationships to each other in one place, and only then has to follow them separately. Each later thread is "what happened to that person I already know", not a new story.
- Daenerys is the one thread that starts apart. It is tolerable because there is exactly one such outlier at the start, not several.
- The honest ratio is useful for a game: 2,007 named characters but only about 14 heavy viewpoints. Depth of world and number of threads the audience must actively track are different numbers.

### Gaps
- Exact POV count per book (books two to four) and chapter counts per character: the A Wiki of Ice and Fire POV pages failed to load and Wikiwand's table was not returned. Only the endpoints (9 and 31) are sourced.
- Number of parallel storylines per TV season or per episode: no fetched source gives a count. The nearest sourced proxy is the title sequence's cap of six locations per episode (section 2).

## 2. What techniques keep many threads clear?

### Takeaway
Clarity comes from filtering a huge world through a small number of viewpoints, tying each thread to a place, and literally showing the audience a map before every episode. The showrunners learned the hard way that basic relationships must be stated outright: their first pilot failed because viewers did not grasp that two leads were siblings.

### Cited Findings
- Each book chapter is told in limited third person from one character's subjective viewpoint, rotating among characters; no viewpoint character is guaranteed to survive. — [Wikiwand: A Song of Ice and Fire](https://wikiwand.com/en/A_Song_of_Ice_and_Fire)
- The 14 major POV characters each have on average about 154 connections (standard deviation 47), close to Dunbar's number of roughly 150, the commonly cited limit on a person's stable social ties. The authors conclude the 2,000-character narrative stays within readers' cognitive limits because it is experienced through these few perspectives. — [arXiv/PNAS study](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.01783.pdf)
- Across all characters the average number of connections is only about 16. — [arXiv/PNAS study](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.01783.pdf)
- Importance rankings in the network are dominated by POV characters; Jon Snow, Jaime, Tyrion, Catelyn and Arya have the most connections. Daenerys ranks low on measures of bridging and closeness because she is geographically separated from the rest for most of five books. — [arXiv/PNAS study](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.01783.pdf)
- The cumulative character count rises almost linearly across the books, which the authors read as a stable structure rather than sudden floods of new names. — [arXiv/PNAS study](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.01783.pdf)
- The opening-credits map exists because, at pilot stage, the team feared the geography would confuse viewers. A producer wanted an equivalent of the map printed at the front of a fantasy novel. — [Art of the Title](https://artofthetitle.com/title/game-of-thrones); [Wikipedia: Game of Thrones title sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_of_Thrones_title_sequence)
- The first attempt was five short map shots cut into the pilot each time the story changed location. They oriented viewers but broke the flow of the episode, so the idea was moved into the title sequence. — [Art of the Title](https://artofthetitle.com/title/game-of-thrones)
- The title sequence shows the places that episode visits, capped at six for time. Four anchors always appear: King's Landing, Winterfell, the Wall, and wherever Daenerys currently is. — [Wikipedia: Game of Thrones title sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_of_Thrones_title_sequence)
- The map also carries plot state: the sigil over Winterfell changes when the castle changes hands, and smoke appears after it burns. — [Wikipedia: Game of Thrones title sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_of_Thrones_title_sequence)
- The unaired first pilot failed with viewers who had not read the books. They did not register that Jaime and Cersei were brother and sister, so the episode's key moments fell flat. About 90 percent was reshot. — [TIME](https://time.com/4206627/game-of-thrones-creators-talk-original-disaster-pilot); [The Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/game-of-thrones/2021/4/16/22386842/game-of-thrones-pilot-retrospective-anniversary)
- The fix included a newly invented Jaime and Cersei scene in the capital. It shows the second main location early, hints that the pair share a secret, and makes their relationship unmissable, which the episode's cliffhanger depends on. — [The Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/game-of-thrones/2021/4/16/22386842/game-of-thrones-pilot-retrospective-anniversary)
- The Ringer points out that a novel can stop for a paragraph of background but television cannot, so explanation had to be folded into scenes. — [The Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/game-of-thrones/2021/4/16/22386842/game-of-thrones-pilot-retrospective-anniversary)
- A student critic observes that most episodes give each plotline only a short slice, with only a few episodes devoted to one or two threads. — [The Crimson White](https://thecrimsonwhite.com/40609/culture/game-of-thrones-and-the-art-of-fantasy-story-scale/)
- Early seasons made distance felt: the royal party's journey to Winterfell takes weeks, and Tyrion's trip from the Eyrie to the capital spans several episodes. — [SlashFilm, pacing discussion](https://www.slashfilm.com/552431/game-of-thrones-pacing)

### Inferences
- The four always-shown map anchors amount to a statement of the show's stable thread set: capital intrigue, the Stark home, the northern frontier, and the exile. Everything else is a temporary branch off one of those four. A game could adopt the same rule: a small fixed set of "home" threads the player can always locate, plus short-lived side branches.
- Thread equals place. Because each thread sits in its own location with its own look, a single establishing image tells the audience which story they are in before anyone speaks.
- The pilot lesson generalises: a twist can only land if the audience holds the plain facts it overturns. Test with people who do not know the story, and check that they can state who is related to whom.
- The map shots cut into the episode failed while the same map in the credits succeeded. Orientation aids work best at the boundary (start of a chapter or session), not as interruptions.
- The low average connection count (16) against the POV figure (154) suggests most characters belong to only one thread. Few characters are shared across threads, which keeps each thread's cast learnable.

### Gaps
- No fetched source discusses "previously on" recaps, costume or colour identity per faction, or the descriptive chapter titles of books four and five. I could not source these.
- No fetched source states the "each character has a simple readable want" idea in those words. The nearest sourced claim is Tufekci's point (section 6) that characters' choices are legible because the pressures on them are shown.

## 3. How do threads cross and converge, and how does information asymmetry create tension?

### Takeaway
Characters are built up separately, so that when two threads finally touch the audience already knows both sides; meanwhile the audience sees the whole board while each character sees only a corner of it. The books also reorder events so that danger feels constant even though deaths actually come in bursts.

### Cited Findings
- The show's key moments tend to be the points where characters from different threads meet. In "Battle of the Bastards" Jon, Sansa and Ramsay had each been developed in separate arcs first, so their collision carried weight and rippled out to characters elsewhere. — [The Crimson White](https://thecrimsonwhite.com/40609/culture/game-of-thrones-and-the-art-of-fantasy-story-scale/)
- The same piece says viewers hold the bigger picture and grasp the meaning of moves that characters cannot, calling this "dramatic irony on an epic scale". — [The Crimson White](https://thecrimsonwhite.com/40609/culture/game-of-thrones-and-the-art-of-fantasy-story-scale/)
- In chapter order, the gaps between significant deaths fit a memoryless (geometric) distribution: how long it has been since the last death tells the reader nothing about when the next will come. — [arXiv/PNAS study](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.01783.pdf)
- In the story's own calendar the same deaths are not random. They cluster, with long safe stretches, following a pattern similar to ordinary human activity. — [arXiv/PNAS study](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.01783.pdf)
- The authors' reading is that telling events out of calendar order, through interleaved viewpoint chapters, is what turns realistic clustered events into an experience that feels unpredictable. — [arXiv/PNAS study](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.01783.pdf)
- The Red Wedding shows up in the network data as a visible jump in one of the survivor-network measures in book three. — [arXiv/PNAS study](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.01783.pdf)
- Early-season pacing spent several episodes placing pieces before a payoff; critics describe tension coming from the wait itself. — [SlashFilm, pacing discussion](https://www.slashfilm.com/552431/game-of-thrones-pacing)
- Martin wants readers to fear for characters and to understand that rescue may not arrive. — [NPR, 2011](https://www.npr.org/2011/07/17/137789188/author-george-r-r-martin-playing-for-keeps)

### Inferences
- Limited viewpoint plus rotation gives two kinds of asymmetry at once. The audience knows more than any one character (they have seen the other threads), yet less than the author (each chapter hides what its narrator does not know or will not think about). Tension comes from the first; fair surprise comes from the second.
- The PNAS finding is directly usable: the designer can keep events logically clustered in the world's timeline and still make them feel unpredictable through the order in which threads are shown.
- Convergence works as a reward. The longer two well-known threads have run apart, the more a meeting pays off, provided both sides were developed first.

### Gaps
- No fetched source gives a scene-by-scene account of specific dramatic-irony setups (for example the audience knowing who pushed Bran while Catelyn suspects the wrong man). I could not source a detailed treatment.

## 4. How were the big shocks seeded so they were surprising yet fair? What has Martin said?

### Takeaway
Each shock overturned a genre expectation (the hero lives, the son avenges the father) while following strictly from causes the audience had already watched pile up. Martin says outright that he targets the predictable next step, and he describes himself as a "gardener" who knows the destination but discovers the route.

### Cited Findings
**Martin on killing characters and unpredictability**
- Martin says audiences assumed Ned was safe because he looked like the hero, and that the expected sequel would be the son avenging the father, so he killed Robb too. — [TVLine, citing Martin's March 2019 Entertainment Weekly interview](https://www.tvline.com/2212242/why-ned-stark-game-of-thrones-death-meant-robb-had-die-george-rr-martin/)
- His stated principle: "I like my fiction to be unpredictable." — [TVLine](https://www.tvline.com/2212242/why-ned-stark-game-of-thrones-death-meant-robb-had-die-george-rr-martin/)
- He called the Red Wedding "the hardest scene I ever had to write", skipped the chapter, finished the rest of the book, and came back to it last. He also wants each death to carry meaning and be felt as a loss. — [CBS News / 60 Minutes](https://www.cbsnews.com/amp/news/game-of-thrones-the-killing-scenes-george-rr-martin-and-the-red-wedding-60-minutes-2019-04-14)
- In 2011 he described a war story in which nobody dies as "a cheap easy way out". — [NPR, 2011](https://www.npr.org/2011/07/17/137789188/author-george-r-r-martin-playing-for-keeps)

**Martin on gardeners and architects**
- Architects plan every detail before writing; gardeners plant a seed and find out what grows. Martin calls himself "much more a gardener than an architect" and admits the method is inefficient and leads to dead ends. — [NPR transcript, 2019](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/771377681)
- He adds that all writers mix both, and that he does know the ending and the fates of the main characters even though much of the story emerges during writing. — [Wheeler Centre](https://www.wheelercentre.com/news-stories/2013/architects-gardeners-and-game-of-thrones)

**Ned Stark's execution**
- One critic argues Ned's death and the Red Wedding satisfied because warnings were already on the table, citing Littlefinger's advice to Ned and Tywin's letter-writing. — [Sirlin.net](https://sirlin.net/posts/game-of-thrones-what-went-wrong)
- The same critic says Ned's death taught the audience that a hero's usual narrative protection does not apply in this world. — [Sirlin.net](https://sirlin.net/posts/game-of-thrones-what-went-wrong)
- Tufekci argues the death was survivable for the story because institutions and outside pressures carried the plot, not any one person: "The story moved on; many characters did not." — [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/observations/the-real-reason-fans-hate-the-last-season-of-game-of-thrones/)

**The Red Wedding: a chain of visible causes** (all from [Screen Rant](https://screenrant.com/game-thrones-red-wedding-clues/2/))
- Season 1: Robb buys passage across a river by promising to marry a Frey daughter; the host is shown as bitter toward the Starks.
- Season 2: Robb falls for another woman, and his mother frees a key prisoner, which angers an important ally and splits the army.
- Season 3: an ally tells Robb he lost the war on his wedding day; Robb executes that ally and loses nearly half his army; needing troops, he goes back to the Freys, who demand a replacement wedding.
- In the episode itself: the plan is described as working only if the host cooperates; Robb's wolf is penned outside; the doors are shut after the ceremony; a senior ally refuses drink; the hosts' observance of guest right makes the Starks feel safe.

**Jon Snow's parentage**
- The fan theory that Jon is the child of Lyanna Stark and Rhaegar Targaryen ran for about twenty years, from the first book in 1996 to the season 6 finale in 2016. — [The Hollywood Reporter](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/live-feed/game-thrones-finale-jon-snow-mother-twist-means-906436)
- Planted clues include the dying Lyanna's repeated "Promise me, Ned", one of the most picked-over lines in the books, and Ned's season 1 promise to tell Jon about his mother next time they meet, a meeting his execution prevents. — [The Hollywood Reporter](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/live-feed/game-thrones-finale-jon-snow-mother-twist-means-906436)
- The reveal itself is done without stating it: Lyanna whispers that Robert would kill the child, and the show cuts from the baby's face to adult Jon. — [The Hollywood Reporter](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/live-feed/game-thrones-finale-jon-snow-mother-twist-means-906436)
- Supporting clues are scattered through other characters' mouths: a Dornish prince says Rhaegar left his wife for another woman; an old knight recalls Rhaegar as someone who disliked killing; the realm's best swordsman was posted to guard Lyanna's tower instead of fighting in the war. — [TIME](https://time.com/4900151/who-is-jon-snows-father/)
- The showrunners worked out who Jon's mother was from the first five books alone. — [TIME](https://time.com/4900151/who-is-jon-snows-father/)
- Season 7 added documentary proof (a record of an annulment and secret remarriage) and a vision of the wedding. — [TIME](https://time.com/4900151/who-is-jon-snows-father/)

### Inferences
- Three distinct recipes for a fair shock appear here:
  1. Consequence shock (Ned, Red Wedding). Every cause is shown on screen as an ordinary plot event. The surprise is only that the story lets the consequence land on someone the genre normally protects.
  2. Long-fuse mystery (Jon's parentage). A question is raised in chapter one, a false answer is supplied by a trusted character, and true clues are split across unrelated threads and unreliable or offhand speakers. No single thread holds enough to solve it.
  3. Withheld-by-viewpoint (also Jon's parentage). The one character who knows the truth is a viewpoint character who is killed before he tells.
- The Red Wedding clue list shows that the final warning signs can be placed minutes before the event (the penned wolf, the shut doors). Late clues do not spoil a twist; they turn surprise into dread for the alert and into "I should have seen it" for everyone else.
- A solvable mystery is a feature, not a failure. The parentage answer was guessed by fans for twenty years and by the showrunners themselves, and the reveal still landed. For a mystery game this argues for making the central secret deducible by a careful player.
- Martin's "gardener who knows the destination" stance fits a game well: fix the ending and the fates of main characters, leave the middle flexible.

### Gaps
- Both Rolling Stone interviews failed to load, so Martin's longer remarks there on Ned and the Red Wedding are known here only through TVLine's brief summary.
- I could not fetch the original Entertainment Weekly Red Wedding oral history; the Ned/Robb reasoning is relayed by TVLine.
- Book-specific Red Wedding foreshadowing (prophetic visions and dreams) is not covered by the fetched Screen Rant piece, which deals with the show only.
- The often-told story of Martin quizzing the showrunners about Jon's mother before approving the adaptation was not in any fetched page beyond TIME's note that they guessed correctly.

## 5. What structural problems appeared as threads multiplied, and what does that teach about limits?

### Takeaway
Once many scattered threads all had to arrive at one place, ordering their arrivals became so hard that Martin rewrote for years, and the oversized manuscript had to be cut in two by geography. The pain point was convergence, not divergence.

### Cited Findings
- Martin says the "Meereenese knot" was about everyone reaching Daenerys: many characters were travelling to her city, and he had to settle who got there, in what order, and what had already happened when each arrived. — [Westeros.org "So Spake Martin" archive, July 2011](https://www.westeros.org/Citadel/SSM/Month/2011/07)
- Realistic travel made it worse: he wanted the voyages to be hard and dangerous, so arrivals could not just be scheduled. — [Westeros.org SSM](https://www.westeros.org/Citadel/SSM/Month/2011/07)
- He wrote multiple versions, and one Daenerys chapter, almost ten years old, was redone with many different endings. — [Westeros.org SSM](https://www.westeros.org/Citadel/SSM/Month/2011/07)
- A specific blocker was showing the aftermath of a major event. He tried it through one outside viewpoint who could only guess, then through another; both failed. The fix was to add a new viewpoint character already on the spot (Barristan Selmy), which he says "cleared away some of the brush", though it was not his first choice. — [Westeros.org SSM](https://www.westeros.org/Citadel/SSM/Month/2011/07)
- He had planned a five-year jump in the story so the child characters could age, then abandoned it; both the plan and its reversal disturbed the chronology. — [Westeros.org SSM](https://www.westeros.org/Citadel/SSM/Month/2011/07)
- Book four became too large and was unfinished, so Martin and his publishers split it. The cut was made by location, not by time, producing two books that run simultaneously with different casts: A Feast for Crows mainly in southern Westeros, A Dance with Dragons in the North, at the Wall and in Essos. — [Wikiwand: A Feast for Crows](https://origin-production.wikiwand.com/en/A_Feast_for_Crows)
- The second half was advertised for the following year and arrived six years later, in 2011. — [Wikiwand: A Feast for Crows](https://origin-production.wikiwand.com/en/A_Feast_for_Crows)
- About 500 pages were first moved from book four into book five, but so much was rewritten that only a couple of hundred survived. — [Westeros.org SSM](https://www.westeros.org/Citadel/SSM/Month/2011/07)
- The six-year wait angered and alienated many readers. — [NPR, 2011](https://www.npr.org/2011/07/17/137789188/author-george-r-r-martin-playing-for-keeps)
- The Barristan fix shows in the network data: he ranks second among all characters for bridging between groups, just behind Jon Snow. — [arXiv/PNAS study](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.01783.pdf)
- By 2019 Martin said he was handling the viewpoint cast with increasing difficulty. — [NPR transcript, 2019](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/771377681)

### Inferences
- The cost of threads is not linear. Nine threads that start together are easy. Thirty-one threads that must reconverge create an ordering problem, because each arrival changes the situation the next arrival finds.
- The geographic split meant readers went a whole book without some favourite characters. Rotation is a promise to the audience that every thread will come back soon; splitting by place broke that promise.
- Martin's fix (add another viewpoint) solved a local problem by increasing the thread count again. This is the gardener's trap: each patch adds growth.
- For a game: plan the convergence point and arrival order before the threads are scattered. This is the one place where being an architect matters most.
- A time jump is hard to add once threads are moving at different speeds. If the story needs one, design it in from the start.

### Gaps
- Who proposed the geographic split, the list of POV characters in each half, and critical reception of book four: not in the fetched pages (English Wikipedia's page was blocked; Wikiwand's summary lacked these).
- No fetched source gives Martin stating a maximum workable number of viewpoints.

## 6. What do critics say made seasons 1-4 work and seasons 7-8 fail, structurally?

### Takeaway
Early seasons moved slowly, respected distance and consequence, and made every character's choice follow from visible pressures. Late seasons kept the big plot destinations but dropped the groundwork: travel became instant, long-built threats and skills paid off cheaply or not at all, and characters bent to reach required endpoints.

### Cited Findings
**What worked**
- Tufekci's thesis: the early show told a "sociological" story, in which institutions, incentives and events shape what people do, so viewers could understand even the villains and no single character had to carry the plot. — [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/observations/the-real-reason-fans-hate-the-last-season-of-game-of-thrones/)
- That design is what made major deaths affordable; she compares The Wire, which also killed leads and built seasons around institutions. — [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/observations/the-real-reason-fans-hate-the-last-season-of-game-of-thrones/)
- The looming threat from the north originally worked as pressure that sharpened rivalries inside the human camps. — [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/observations/the-real-reason-fans-hate-the-last-season-of-game-of-thrones/)
- Early seasons took several episodes to set pieces before paying off, and travel time conveyed the size of the world. — [SlashFilm, pacing discussion](https://www.slashfilm.com/552431/game-of-thrones-pacing)

**What failed**
- Late seasons shifted to "psychological" storytelling centred on individual heroes. Tufekci suspects the showrunners kept Martin's plot points but not the method that would have made them believable. — [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/observations/the-real-reason-fans-hate-the-last-season-of-game-of-thrones/)
- Daenerys's turn to mass killing read as sudden and was explained through inherited madness, not through pressures the audience had watched build. — [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/observations/the-real-reason-fans-hate-the-last-season-of-game-of-thrones/)
- Another critic agrees the turn itself was a defensible destination, foreshadowed since season 2, and that the failure was the trigger: an enemy surrendering does not explain it, and a scene or two could have. — [Sirlin.net](https://sirlin.net/posts/game-of-thrones-what-went-wrong)
- Setups that went unused or were contradicted at the climax: Arya's assassin training, a character's repeated resurrections, the dragons, Bran's visions. The supernatural villain, built up as a patient planner with hidden motives, died abruptly with no plan revealed. — [Sirlin.net](https://sirlin.net/posts/game-of-thrones-what-went-wrong)
- Killing that villain early removed the pressure that had been driving the human conflicts. — [No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/sociological-versus-psychological)
- In season 7 characters crossed the continent within single episodes with no sense of elapsed time; examples include an army seeming to teleport and a warning that is never sent because the plot needs a character to miss it. — [SlashFilm, pacing discussion](https://www.slashfilm.com/552431/game-of-thrones-pacing)
- A writer on the show admitted they chose to "just get on with it" in season 7; another conceded they sometimes cheated on time and could have added a "weeks later" card but did not. — [Collider](https://collider.com/game-of-thrones-season-7-pacing-criticism/)
- Collider links the speed-up to the decision to end the series after season 8. — [Collider](https://collider.com/game-of-thrones-season-7-pacing-criticism/)
- Final-season critique: offscreen travel and a vague timeline made characters feel like "pawns on a game board"; a longer season or two would have let threads resolve. — [SlashFilm, final season](https://slashfilm.com/game-of-thrones-final-season-problems)
- Character choices bent to the plot: Jaime's reversal toward Brienne and return to Cersei felt forced, and Arya abandons a years-long mission after one last-minute conversation. — [SlashFilm, final season](https://slashfilm.com/game-of-thrones-final-season-problems)
- Threads simply dropped: several supporting characters vanish unresolved. — [SlashFilm, final season](https://slashfilm.com/game-of-thrones-final-season-problems)
- A dissenting view: one SlashFilm writer preferred the faster pace, reading it as the story narrowing from slow burn to endgame, and found reunions emotionally strong even where logic slipped. — [SlashFilm, pacing discussion](https://www.slashfilm.com/552431/game-of-thrones-pacing)

### Inferences
- Critics mostly object to missing connective tissue, not to the endings chosen. The same event can be a fair shock or a cheat depending on whether its causes were shown.
- Convergence naturally speeds a story up, since fewer locations means less rotation. The show's error was to let convergence also erase the world's rules (distance, time, consequence) that had made earlier events credible.
- Removing a story's background pressure (the common threat) before the personal conflicts are resolved leaves those conflicts without a cause. Resolve the pressure last, or replace it.
- A rule a game can use: every long-running setup is a debt. If a skill, prophecy or threat has been built up for hours, the climax must use it in a way that could not have happened without it.

## 7. Practical lessons: how many threads, how often to rotate, when to converge

### Takeaway
The sourced numbers point to a small core: three plot lines, about nine starting viewpoints, roughly 14 heavy viewpoints at most, four permanent "home" locations and no more than six locations in any one sitting. Scatter from a shared start, rotate in short slices, and plan the convergence order early.

### Cited Findings
- Three plot lines underlie the entire series. — [Wikiwand: A Song of Ice and Fire](https://wikiwand.com/en/A_Song_of_Ice_and_Fire)
- Nine viewpoints at the start; 31 by book five, at which point the author reports growing difficulty. — [Wikiwand: A Song of Ice and Fire](https://wikiwand.com/en/A_Song_of_Ice_and_Fire); [NPR transcript, 2019](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/771377681)
- Fourteen viewpoints carry the bulk of the chapters, each with a social circle near the human cognitive limit of about 150. — [arXiv/PNAS study](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.01783.pdf)
- The TV title sequence shows at most six locations per episode and keeps four permanent anchors. — [Wikipedia: Game of Thrones title sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_of_Thrones_title_sequence)
- Most episodes give each thread only a brief segment; single-thread episodes are rare. — [The Crimson White](https://thecrimsonwhite.com/40609/culture/game-of-thrones-and-the-art-of-fantasy-story-scale/)
- Nearly all characters begin in one place and are then scattered. — [Winter is Coming](https://winteriscoming.net/2019/05/14/george-r-r-martin-on-what-he-borrowed-from-j-r-r-tolkien)
- Convergence on a single place was the hardest problem in the books, solved only with years of rewrites and an extra viewpoint. — [Westeros.org SSM](https://www.westeros.org/Citadel/SSM/Month/2011/07)
- Splitting threads into separate volumes by location led to a six-year gap before half the cast returned. — [Wikiwand: A Feast for Crows](https://origin-production.wikiwand.com/en/A_Feast_for_Crows)
- Interleaving threads out of strict calendar order makes realistically clustered events feel unpredictable. — [arXiv/PNAS study](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.01783.pdf)
- Screenwriting lessons drawn from the Tufekci essay: let outside forces shape behaviour, build a story that does not rest on one character, and ground every major change in earlier evidence. — [No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/sociological-versus-psychological)
- Viewers must be told basic relationships plainly or later reveals fail. — [TIME](https://time.com/4206627/game-of-thrones-creators-talk-original-disaster-pilot)

### Inferences
These are design recommendations derived from the findings above, not sourced claims.
- Thread budget: aim for 3 underlying plot lines, 4 to 6 character threads active at once, and treat roughly 9 as a comfortable ceiling for a story that wants to stay clear. The series itself became hard for its own author to manage somewhere between 14 and 31.
- Introduction: open with most of the cast in one location and one shared event. Let the player learn names, relationships and wants there. Then split them. Allow at most one thread that starts elsewhere.
- Identity: one place, one look, one plainly stated goal per thread. Keep a persistent map or board that shows where every thread currently stands, shown at chapter boundaries.
- Rotation: short slices, no thread absent for long. Never park half the cast for an extended stretch.
- Asymmetry: let the player see across threads so they know what individual characters do not; hide information only through what a given viewpoint character does not know.
- Shocks: for each planned shock, list its causes and confirm each one appears on screen beforehand as an ordinary event. Aim the shock at an expectation the genre creates. Place a few last-minute warning signs for attentive players.
- Central mystery: raise the question at the very start, supply a plausible false answer from a trusted voice, and scatter true clues across different threads so no single thread solves it. It should be deducible.
- Convergence: decide early where threads meet and in what order. Converge in pairs first, then in larger groups. Keep the world's rules (time, distance, consequence) intact as the pace rises.
- Payoff audit: before the ending, list every long-running setup and confirm the climax uses it.
- Process: know the ending and the fates of the main characters from the outset; leave the middle free to grow, but refuse new threads once the convergence plan is fixed.
