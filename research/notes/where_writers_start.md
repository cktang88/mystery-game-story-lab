# Where Professional Storytellers Start a Story, and How the Starting Point Shapes the Work

Scope note: every claim below is tied to a fetched page. Several pages could not be reached (Paris Review interviews with Ishiguro, Bradbury and Le Guin were blocked by the fetch allowlist or failed; a Boing Boing Ted Chiang interview failed). Where a well-known claim could not be confirmed from a fetched source, it is listed under Gaps rather than stated as fact. Direct quotes are kept under 15 words and limited to one per source.

## 1. Character-first: who starts with character, and how do they derive plot from want, need and flaw?

### Takeaway
Character-first writers (King, Bradbury, Urasawa, Lisa Cron's method, Pixar's rules, Anthony Horowitz for mysteries) usually do not start from a bare character. They start from a character under pressure: a person dropped into a situation (King), a person with a belief that is about to be tested (Cron, Pixar #6), or, for crime, a person with a motive (Horowitz). Plot is then whatever that pressure forces the character to do. The result tends to be emotionally strong and surprising scene by scene, but it is weak at long-range setups and payoffs unless an ending or structure is added later.

### Cited Findings
**Stephen King (situation plus character)**
- King starts from a situation, puts characters in it and watches them try to get out. He does not plot ahead and holds that plotting and real spontaneity do not mix. His "what if" examples: *'Salem's Lot* (what if vampires invaded a small New England town?) and *Cujo* (what if a mother and son were trapped in a stalled car by a rabid dog?). — [The Write Practice, summarizing *On Writing*](https://thewritepractice.com/stephen-king-situation/)
- King treats stories as found objects. The writer's job is to watch what happens and write it down, and he describes plot as untrustworthy, something best kept "under house arrest." — [The Write Practice](https://thewritepractice.com/stephen-king-situation/)
- In King's method the situation (a "what if") comes first and character second. He is really a premise-plus-character writer, not a pure character writer. — inference from the same source.

**Ray Bradbury**
- Bradbury wrote long lists of nouns ("the lake," "the crowd," and so on) and called them provocations that brought up his better material. He would pick a noun, write freely about it, and a story formed once a character appeared. — [The Marginalian, on *Zen in the Art of Writing*](https://www.themarginalian.org/2013/10/18/ray-bradbury-on-lists/)
- His characters did the work if he "gave them their heads," meaning he let their fantasies and fears drive the action. — [The Marginalian](https://www.themarginalian.org/2013/10/18/ray-bradbury-on-lists/)
- Bradbury's real entry point was a remembered image or object (a noun), with character second and plot emerging last. — inference from the same source.

**Lisa Cron (*Story Genius*)**
- Cron's method starts with how the character must change. The protagonist begins with a worldview that "no longer serves them," rooted in a wound, and this is the misbelief. Second, design a plot whose events specifically challenge that belief. Third, build every scene so it pressures the misbelief, until a final reckoning forces the character to drop it. — [Turning to Story, summary of the Story Genius method](https://turningtostory.substack.com/p/the-story-genius-method)

**Pixar story artists (Emma Coats's "22 rules")**
- Rule #6: find what the character is good at or comfortable with, then throw the opposite at them. Rule #13: give characters strong opinions, because passive characters lose the audience. Rule #21: ask what would make *you* act that way, rather than writing "cool." Rule #4 is the story spine ("Once upon a time... every day... one day... because of that... until finally"). Rule #7: settle the ending before the middle. Rule #3: you only learn what the story is about at the end, so rewrite then. — [Open Culture, Pixar's 22 rules](https://www.openculture.com/2013/03/pixars_22_rules_of_good_storytelling.html)
- So even Pixar's character-first rules pair character with an early ending decision (#7) and a theme found in revision (#3).

**Naoki Urasawa (*Monster*, *20th Century Boys*, *Pluto*)**
- Urasawa does not plan every detail. He pictures a "movie trailer" for the overall arc and starts once he is excited enough. He says detailed advance planning would make a 5 to 7 year serial impossible, and that once he starts, the story tells him where it wants to go. — [J-Generation, Japan House LA interview 2019](https://j-generation.com/2019/01/naoki-urasawa-interview-monster-20th-century-boys-2019/)
- In *20th Century Boys* he drew a baby in an early scene without knowing it was Kanna, and was as surprised as readers when the link clicked. If a story stops surprising him, he says he cannot continue it. His craft focus is faces and expressions, which change in every situation. — [J-Generation](https://j-generation.com/2019/01/naoki-urasawa-interview-monster-20th-century-boys-2019/)

**Anthony Horowitz (mystery writer, character and motive first)**
- Horowitz's top tip for mysteries is to start with character and motive ("it all starts with the motive"), and only then build clues, suspense and misdirection. — [PBS Masterpiece](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/masterpiece/specialfeatures/how-to-write-a-mystery-anthony-horowitzs-1-tip)

**Gen Urobuchi**
- Urobuchi writes each character as an individual so that opposing ideologies come out convincingly, and holds that good and evil must be on even ground so either side could win. — [Anime News Network interview, 2014](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/interview/2014-09-17/gen-urobuchi/.78542)

**Kotaro Uchikoshi (story first, then characters)**
- In a Virtue's Last Reward postmortem, Uchikoshi said he builds the story first and then extracts the characters it needs to work. He called that unusual among Japanese creators. — [Game Developer, "Storytelling Secrets of Virtue's Last Reward"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-storytelling-secrets-of-i-virtue-s-last-reward-i-)
- He balances casts in opposing pairs (cold and kind, assertive and passive). He also deliberately includes a character who looks plainly villainous, so the real antagonist stays hidden. — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-storytelling-secrets-of-i-virtue-s-last-reward-i-)

### Inferences
- How plot comes out of character in practice: want or misbelief, then events built to attack that belief (Cron, Pixar #6), then the character's choices, then consequences ("because of that," Pixar #4). The flaw generates incidents, and the need is the change the ending must pay off.
- Discovery writers of long serials (King, Urasawa) say surprise keeps them going, and they accept that some setups only make sense afterwards (Urasawa's baby). For a mystery game whose clues must be fair on replay, discovery alone is risky. It works only if a later revision pass re-seeds the clues.
- For mysteries specifically, "character first" means the culprit's motive first (Horowitz). The murderer's want is the hidden plot, and the investigator's story is how it gets uncovered.

### Gaps
- I could not fetch King's own text for the "fossils" metaphor (stories as fossils to be dug up) or his *Misery* example. The Write Practice summary only confirms "found objects." King's phrasing needs verifying against *On Writing* itself.
- The Paris Review Bradbury interview (Art of Fiction No. 203) was blocked, so I could not source his remark that plot is the footprints characters leave.
- No primary source found for Hajime Isayama's character-creation process. Only his ending-first statements were sourced (see section 5).

## 2. World/setting-first: Tolkien, Herbert, Jemisin, Le Guin, FromSoftware, Bungie. How do they avoid a world without a story?

### Takeaway
World-first writers succeed when the world is built around a tension that produces conflict: Tolkien's languages needed a history in which they could change and die; Herbert's ecology (water scarcity) was fused with a political-religious theme (the danger of messiahs); Jemisin's broken planet is built to dramatize oppression; Bungie's Halo is a "place" designed to host many plots. The common safeguard is that the world exists to make a theme or a playable conflict possible, and plot is attached later through characters who have a stake in that tension.

### Cited Findings
**J.R.R. Tolkien**
- Tolkien wrote that inventing languages was the foundation, and that the stories were made more to give the languages a world than the reverse. — [Wikipedia, "Languages constructed by Tolkien"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Languages_constructed_by_Tolkien). Caveat: the page marks this quote "[T 5]" and does not explicitly tie it to a letter. It lists Letter #165 (to Houghton Mifflin, 30 June 1955) among its references, but the match is unconfirmed.

**Frank Herbert (*Dune*)**
- Herbert's earliest concept was theme: messianic upheavals and the danger of hero-worship. The ecology came from a 1957 assignment in Florence, Oregon, about planting grasses to stop moving dunes. That led to the thought of a planet that was entirely desert. — [Tim O'Reilly, *Frank Herbert*, ch. 3](https://www.oreilly.com/tim/herbert/ch03.html)
- An early version centered on an ecological hero whose reclamation would take centuries, which Herbert saw covered only one dynamic. He then added Paul as an immediate messiah, the feudal Atreides, the Bene Gesserit and the breeding program. Herbert explained the merge: "I don't think any story should have only one thread." — [O'Reilly](https://www.oreilly.com/tim/herbert/ch03.html)
- Herbert spent years researching religion, psychoanalysis, linguistics, economics and politics. He kept folders of scene and character notes and drew on Kalahari water-lore, Apache tactics and Bedouin culture for the Fremen. He held that ideas are cheap and their development is what counts. — [O'Reilly](https://www.oreilly.com/tim/herbert/ch03.html)

**N.K. Jemisin (*The Broken Earth*)**
- Jemisin writes short "proof of concept" stories to test a potential novel's world, then revises the world based on reader reactions. In the test story "Stone Hunger" the magic (orogeny) worked like a sense of taste. She rejected that because she wanted magic to be a science gone wrong, though "the rest of the world was fine." — [Writer's Digest interview](https://writersdigest.com/be-inspired/the-wd-interview-n-k-jemisin)
- For *The Fifth Season* she outlined three plotlines, knew from the start that all three viewpoints were one person (Essun), and named the theme outright: she wanted to explore oppression, including slavery, closeted queerness and disability. She wrote most of Essun's thread first to "lodge her" in her head, planted hints across all three threads, and removed the most obvious ones in revision with beta readers' help. — [Writing Excuses 19.46, interview on structure](https://writingexcuses.com/19-46-an-interview-on-structure-with-n-k-jemisin/)
- She found the voice (part second person, present tense) through test chapters, by instinct. — [Writer's Digest](https://writersdigest.com/be-inspired/the-wd-interview-n-k-jemisin)

**Ursula K. Le Guin**
- In her introduction to *The Left Hand of Darkness*, Le Guin calls science fiction at its best a thought experiment ("let's say" a premise holds, then see what follows). She says the androgynous Gethenians predict nothing. They describe a psychological reality that already exists. Her key line: "Science fiction is not predictive; it is descriptive." — [GitHub gist reproducing the introduction](https://gist.github.com/andreparames/4424894) (the page does not name its author, but the content matches Le Guin's introduction); corroborated by [Bowdoin course blog](https://courses.bowdoin.edu/italian-2500-spring-2020-ajmuscat/world-science-fiction/le-guin-introduction-to-the-left-hand-of-darkness/)
- Le Guin's "world" is therefore an apparatus for a question. The setting is how the thought experiment gets run. (See also section 3.)

**Bungie / Halo (Joseph Staten, GDC Online)**
- Staten's lesson from Halo was that place should come before plot. Early scripts "got blown apart," so the team concentrated on building blocks (interesting places and characters) and wrote cinematic and mission scripts late. His advice was to resist starting with plot and to build an enduring place that allows many plots. The audience, he said, wants "your worlds, not your words." — [Game Developer, "Bungie's Staten on building worlds, not words"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/pc/gdc-online-bungie-s-staten-on-building-worlds-not-words)
- Staten warned that huge canons are unwieldy. He recommended "safe pockets" with simple rules, clear boundaries and internal freedom. He also stressed that gameplay comes first and that writers must work within the limits of design and tech, blaming Halo 2's slump on design and technical over-reach. — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/pc/gdc-online-bungie-s-staten-on-building-worlds-not-words)

**Hidetaka Miyazaki / FromSoftware**
- Secondhand: Miyazaki grew up poor, read library books he only half understood, and filled the gaps with imagination. Commentators argue his games leave gaps deliberately for players to fill. The article draws on a 2015 Guardian interview plus a Reddit researcher's analysis. — [Den of Geek](https://www.denofgeek.com/?p=781317)

**Hayao Miyazaki (setting and image-led)**
- See section 6. His image boards set a film's world and mood before the story is fixed.

### Inferences
- How world-first writers avoid a static world:
  - Put a conflict into the world itself: scarcity (Dune's water), oppression (Jemisin's orogenes), an unstable hypothesis (Le Guin).
  - Fuse at least two threads early: Herbert merged ecology with the messiah theme.
  - Give the world one character with a personal stake: Jemisin's Essun.
  - Prototype the world with small stories before committing: Jemisin's proof-of-concept stories, Staten's building blocks.
- Staten's "safe pockets" maps well onto iceberg lore. Keep a large hidden bible, but expose it in bounded, internally consistent pockets the game can actually deliver.
- World-first carries a particular risk for games: lore that cannot be shown through play. Staten's insistence on gameplay first and writing within tech limits is the corrective.

### Gaps
- I could not confirm the exact Tolkien letter or recipient for the "stories made to provide a world for the languages" line.
- I found no fetched primary interview in which Hidetaka Miyazaki describes building lore before story, or his process for item-description storytelling. The Den of Geek piece is secondhand (via Reddit). A primary Miyazaki interview on lore-first design should be sought separately.
- I did not reach Le Guin's Paris Review interview (blocked) to source how she finds characters. Her essays on character-first creation (e.g., "Science Fiction and Mrs. Brown") were not fetched.

## 3. Theme- or question-first: Le Guin, Ted Chiang, Yoko Taro, Urobuchi, Truby, Egri. How do they avoid preachiness?

### Takeaway
Question-first writers frame the theme as an open question or experiment, not an answer: Le Guin's "let's say," Dick's "what is reality?", Chiang's scientific idea, Uchikoshi's central theme. Theorists who start from a thesis (Egri's premise, Truby's moral argument) avoid sermonizing by carrying the argument through action and structure rather than dialogue. In Egri's framing, the thesis must be one the author takes a side on. In Truby's, the hero's moral decision must come late enough to keep real jeopardy.

### Cited Findings
**Ted Chiang**
- Chiang says a scientific idea is usually the initial impetus for a story, but not enough on its own. He does not begin drafting until he knows the ending ("I don't begin drafting until I know the ending"), meaning both the plot's conclusion and the protagonist's arc. Sometimes the trigger is a sentence that arrives in his head. — [Electric Literature interview](https://electricliterature.com/ted-chiang-interview/)

**Philip K. Dick**
- Dick said his fiction came from two linked questions: what is reality, and what makes a human authentic. Counterfeit realities produce counterfeit humans and vice versa. Every novel meant building a universe sturdy enough not to fall apart "two days later." — [The Marginalian on "How to Build a Universe..."](https://www.themarginalian.org/2013/09/06/how-to-build-a-universe-philip-k-dick/)

**Ursula K. Le Guin** — the thought-experiment framing is in section 2. Le Guin explicitly denies prophecy and prescription, which is itself an anti-preachiness stance: the story describes rather than urges.

**Lajos Egri (*The Art of Dramatic Writing*)**
- Egri defines the premise as what the play sets out to prove, e.g. "Great love defies even death" for *Romeo and Juliet*. A good premise contains character, conflict and conclusion (e.g. "Frugality leads to waste"), working as a thumbnail synopsis. — [Writers Write summary of Egri](https://www.writerswrite.com/fiction/egri/)
- Egri allows starting from a character, an incident or a thought, as long as the premise is found before the play is finished. Bad premises are vague ("love defies all"), emotions without direction ("jealousy"), passive, multiple, or switched mid-play. The author must take sides. — [Writers Write](https://www.writerswrite.com/fiction/egri/)

**John Truby (*The Anatomy of Story*)**
- Truby blames preachiness on too much moral argument and not enough plot. Plot's surprises give the moral sequence its force, and structure and action, not dialogue, should carry it. — [Medium summary of *Anatomy of Story*, pp. 122–126](https://medium.com/@pirangy/balancing-moral-argument-with-plot-the-verdict-and-the-iliad-john-truby-the-anatomy-of-story-p-fa5426906a71)
- His moral argument sequence runs: initial values and moral weakness, moral need, immoral actions the hero justifies, moral self-revelation and decision, thematic revelation. Delaying the self-revelation keeps the audience unsure whether the hero will act rightly in time. *The Verdict* weakens itself by deciding too early. *The Iliad* runs the fall-and-rise twice. — [Medium summary](https://medium.com/@pirangy/balancing-moral-argument-with-plot-the-verdict-and-the-iliad-john-truby-the-anatomy-of-story-p-fa5426906a71)

**Kazuo Ishiguro (theme first, then a framework)**
- *Never Let Me Go* grew from about 15 years of fragments about "students" in wrecked English farmhouses, with a strange fate over them he could not name. A radio discussion of biotechnology made the framework fall into place. He set it in 1970s–80s England as an alternative history rather than the future, so it would not be read as prophecy. His underlying aim was a simple, fundamental story about the sadness of the human condition. — [Penguin Random House, "A Conversation with Kazuo Ishiguro"](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/85609/never-let-me-go-by-kazuo-ishiguro/9781400078776/)

**Yoko Taro (emotion first)** — see section 5. His starting unit is a target emotion (sadness at a death), which works like a theme expressed as a feeling rather than an argument.

**Gen Urobuchi**
- For *Psycho-Pass*, Urobuchi said he wanted to convey "fear" within the world, and he linked Makishima's sense of not belonging to that theme. His first priority is simply to make something interesting. — [Anime News Network 2014](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/interview/2014-09-17/gen-urobuchi/.78542)

**Kotaro Uchikoshi (theme first in Zero Time Dilemma)**
- *Zero Time Dilemma* "started off with a central theme." Uchikoshi then chose the speculative-fiction and philosophy elements that best conveyed it. — [Game Developer, "No Escape: Zero Time Dilemma and the narrative design of Kotaro Uchikoshi"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/no-escape-i-zero-time-dilemma-i-and-the-narrative-design-of-kotaro-uchikoshi)
- In Virtue's Last Reward, the Prisoner's Dilemma and Schrödinger's cat are hinted at, not explained, because over-explaining would cost the story credibility. The concepts add flavor rather than lecture. — [Game Developer, VLR](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-storytelling-secrets-of-i-virtue-s-last-reward-i-)

**M. Night Shyamalan (theme as a one-line core)**
- Shyamalan describes his films by their core meaning before their twists: *The Sixth Sense* is about a man looking for redemption, and *Unbreakable* asks what your potential is. He outlines for months, and *The Sixth Sense* sold on its tenth draft. — [Creative Screenwriting](https://www.creativescreenwriting.com/cswcms/m-night-shyamalan-on-screenwriting/)

### Inferences
- Anti-preachiness techniques with sources:
  1. Phrase the theme as a question or experiment (Le Guin, Dick).
  2. Hint at science or philosophy and do not explain it (Uchikoshi).
  3. Let structure and action argue, and delay the moral decision (Truby).
  4. Give both sides real force (Urobuchi's even ground; Egri's "take sides" means a clear stance, not a strawman).
  5. Set the speculative frame in an alternative past or present rather than a prophesied future (Ishiguro, Le Guin).
- Question-first writers still need an ending before drafting (Chiang), or the question never resolves dramatically.

### Gaps
- I did not fetch Truby's own definition of the "designing principle" or his advice to start from premise. Only the moral-argument material was sourced.
- I found no fetched source for Yoko Taro on theme or anti-preachiness. He has said elsewhere that he dislikes pushing a message, but this was not verified here.
- Le Guin's Paris Review interview and her other craft essays were not reached.

## 4. Premise / high-concept / "what if" first: Philip K. Dick, Crichton, Brooker, high-concept screenwriting

### Takeaway
"What if" starts produce strong hooks and clear stakes, but their writers then have to work to add human depth. Brooker searches the premise for its worst plausible outcome. Crichton revives "exhausted" genres through deep research and openly writes characters from the outside, like a camera. King, Herbert and Ishiguro all show that a premise usually becomes a story only once it fuses with a character in a situation or with a theme.

### Cited Findings
- **Charlie Brooker / Black Mirror:**
  - Episodes begin with Brooker and producer Annabel Jones discussing an issue (parenting, an observation about life). A "what if" emerges, and they bat it around looking for the worst possible outcome.
  - Brooker knows an idea works when he "can't shut up" about it. He then writes a short treatment, takes notes and drafts.
  - Characters were sometimes reshaped late by casting: Jodie Foster on "Arkangel," and Andrea Riseborough's role swap on "Crocodile."
  - Source: [TBI Vision, "Writer's room: Charlie Brooker on designing a Black Mirror"](https://tbivision.com/?p=823705)
- **Michael Crichton:** Crichton is drawn to genres that seem exhausted (he cites the Victorian adventure novel and time travel) and tries to revive them. He sees characters from outside, as if watching a film: "I'm looking at a movie." He rewrites heavily and rewrote *Timeline* several times. His editor noted that *The Andromeda Strain* drew its strength from a documentary feel rather than characterization. — [Publishers Weekly, "The High Concept of Michael Crichton" (1999)](https://publishersweekly.com/pw/print/19991101/32624-the-high-concept-of-michael-crichton.html)
- **Philip K. Dick:** his premises are tied to his core questions about reality and authenticity, so the premise always serves a deeper question. — [The Marginalian](https://www.themarginalian.org/2013/09/06/how-to-build-a-universe-philip-k-dick/)
- **Stephen King:** "what if" situations as starting points, e.g. *'Salem's Lot* and *Cujo*. — [The Write Practice](https://thewritepractice.com/stephen-king-situation/)
- **Frank Herbert:** "What if I had an entire planet that was desert?" was a premise, but on its own it produced a one-dynamic story until he merged in the messiah theme. — [O'Reilly](https://www.oreilly.com/tim/herbert/ch03.html)
- **Ishiguro:** the biotech premise was the framework that unlocked years of character fragments, not the starting point. — [Penguin Random House](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/85609/never-let-me-go-by-kazuo-ishiguro/9781400078776/)
- **Egri** would classify a bare premise ("what if X") as an incident, not a premise. It needs a character, a conflict and a conclusion to prove. — [Writers Write](https://www.writerswrite.com/fiction/egri/)

### Inferences
- A "what if" is most productive when pushed toward its worst consequence (Brooker) and when it meets an existing emotional or thematic seed (Ishiguro, Herbert).
- Premise-first tends to produce thin, camera-eye characters (Crichton's self-description). Writers who use it need a deliberate second pass for want, need and flaw.
- For a game with several sci-fi conceits (AGI, aliens, cloning, time travel), each conceit is a separate "what if." Herbert's lesson is that the story became strong only when separate premises were fused under one theme.

### Gaps
- I did not source formal high-concept screenwriting doctrine (e.g., Blake Snyder's *Save the Cat* logline test). Not fetched.
- No direct Crichton quote about Jurassic Park's origin (a long-gestating screenplay idea) was verified.

## 5. Ending-first / twist-first: mystery writers, Uchikoshi, Rowling, Shyamalan, Nolan, Dark's creators, Isayama, Yoko Taro, Poe. How do they build backward?

### Takeaway
Ending-first has the deepest pedigree for mysteries. Poe argued plots must be worked out to the denouement before writing, so every incident points toward it. In practice, though, most writers fix the endpoint plus a few anchor beats and stay flexible in between. Rowling, Dark's creators and Lindelof all show plans that held at the anchors and shifted in the middle. Isayama shows the cost of a fixed ending held too rigidly. Yoko Taro's "backwards scriptwriting" is the most concrete backward-building method found: fix the climactic emotional scene, then distribute the details that make it hurt across the earlier timeline.

### Cited Findings
**Edgar Allan Poe**
- In "The Philosophy of Composition," Poe argues that any plot worth the name must be worked out to its denouement before writing begins. Keeping the outcome in view lets incidents and tone build toward it and gives the plot consequence and causation. He criticizes writers who start from a theme or striking incidents and patch the gaps as they go. He begins from the effect he wants on the reader and derives everything in "The Raven," refrain and setting included, from that intended ending. He cites Dickens's claim that Godwin wrote *Caleb Williams* backwards. — [Poe, "The Philosophy of Composition," Lit2Go](https://etc.usf.edu/lit2go/147/the-works-of-edgar-allan-poe/5371/the-philosophy-of-composition)

**Yoko Taro (NieR, GDC 2014)**
- "Backwards scriptwriting":
  - Put the emotional core at the center of the timeline. In NieR this was "a girl dies."
  - Refine what makes it sad: she is young, cannot speak, is kind, and it is her wedding day.
  - Spread those details through the earlier timeline so players bond with her before the tragedy. A death shown at the start would carry no attachment.
- "Photo thinking": imagine a specific emotional scene visually (a bride dying in someone's arms), then work backward and plant subtle hints such as an overcast sky. Avoid meaningless settings that distract from the scene.
- He developed both methods through trial and error after finding script guides unhelpful.
- Source: [Game Informer, "Making Weird Games for Weird People," GDC 2014](https://gameinformer.com/b/news/archive/2014/03/20/making-weird-games-for-weird-people.aspx)

**Hajime Isayama (Attack on Titan)**
- Isayama told the *New York Times* (Nov 2023) that the story of a victim who becomes the aggressor was "something I had in mind right from the get-go," and that he began with the ending in mind. He also said he felt tied to what he had envisioned when young, that manga became a restrictive art form for him, and that with total freedom he might have changed the ending. An earlier, bleaker ending resembling *The Mist* was reportedly considered. — [SlashFilm, summarizing the NYT interview and others](https://slashfilm.com/1439560/attack-on-titan-creator-planned-ending-start)

**J.K. Rowling**
- Rowling sketched the seven books in advance. Mr. Weasley was "due to die in Book 5" but was spared, and Lupin and Tonks died in Book 7 instead. Hagrid carrying Harry out of the forest had been planned for a long time. She knew Snape's nature from the beginning and always knew Fred would be the twin to die.
- After her mother's death, death became the central theme of the series, and her casual early handling of Harry's parents' deaths changed. She revised the long-planned last line ("only those whom he loved could see the lightning scar") to "All was well."
- Source: [Accio Quote, Dateline NBC (Meredith Vieira), July 2007](https://accio-quote.org/articles/2007/0729-dateline-vieira.html)

**Dark (Baran bo Odar and Jantje Friese)**
- Some endings were set early: saving Regina and the final dinner scene were things they always wanted. Never answering whether God exists ("Woller's eye") was also always the plan. Three seasons matched the show's triquetra and "threes" motif.
- The middle moved. The parallel world was originally planned for season two, and they "shifted the puzzle pieces around." Friese said the main challenge was deciding when to reveal what, and that some of it was developed as they went.
- Foreshadowing was planted for the ending, e.g. a three-worlds image in a book Elisabeth reads in season two.
- Source: [The Hollywood Reporter, final-season interview](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/live-feed/dark-final-season-explained-baran-bo-odar-jantje-friese-interview-1302171)

**Christopher Nolan**
- Nolan plans structure first through extensive diagrams, then writes his nonlinear scripts straight through from page one, in the order the audience will experience them. He tried writing *Following* chronologically and re-editing it into structure, and never did that again, because the needed rewriting was enormous. — [TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/christopher-nolan-writing-nonlinear-chronological-order-scripts/)

**M. Night Shyamalan** (a counterexample to "twist first")
- Shyamalan says he does not start from the twist. He starts from what the film means ("a man looking for redemption"), and the extra layer usually emerges as he writes, often in the middle. He outlines for months and went through ten drafts of *The Sixth Sense*. — [Creative Screenwriting](https://www.creativescreenwriting.com/cswcms/m-night-shyamalan-on-screenwriting/)

**Kotaro Uchikoshi** (mixed, and partly discovered)
- In Virtue's Last Reward he built the branching flow in Excel, prototyped outcomes, and rewrote upstream when a scenario failed. Changing Round One forced rework of everything in Round Two that depended on it. — [Game Developer, VLR](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-storytelling-secrets-of-i-virtue-s-last-reward-i-)
- *Zero Time Dilemma* began from a central theme and a mandate to resolve VLR's open mysteries without contradicting earlier setups. With the fragment structure he warned that changing one fragment affects everything around it. He also lets characters go where they want, so the structure often departed from his plan. — [Game Developer, ZTD](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/no-escape-i-zero-time-dilemma-i-and-the-narrative-design-of-kotaro-uchikoshi)
- For *Ever17*, he did *not* plan the main twist in advance. He reached the trick from another angle, doubted it would work, then re-examined the character settings and found they fit as if planned. — [Noisy Pixel interview](https://noisypixel.net/kotaro-uchikoshi-interview-infinity-zero-escape-ai-series/)
- He started the Infinity series partly from emotion: the pent-up gloom of adolescence. Character traits such as Tsugumi's immortality and You being a clone began as random ideas. — [Noisy Pixel](https://noisypixel.net/kotaro-uchikoshi-interview-infinity-zero-escape-ai-series/)
- On endings, he wants players to imagine what happens afterwards: whatever players think is the ending should be their ending. — [Anime News Network 2018](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/feature/2018-09-18/interview-zero-escape-director-kotaro-uchikoshi/.136019)

**Pixar rule #7:** settle the ending before the middle, because endings are hardest. — [Open Culture](https://www.openculture.com/2013/03/pixars_22_rules_of_good_storytelling.html)

**Ted Chiang:** no drafting until the ending and the protagonist's arc are known. — [Electric Literature](https://electricliterature.com/ted-chiang-interview/)

### Inferences
- What successful backward-builders actually do:
  1. Fix the endpoint as an effect or emotion (Poe, Yoko Taro), not just as a plot fact.
  2. Fix a few anchor scenes (Rowling's Hagrid scene, Dark's dinner).
  3. Plan the reveal order separately from the event order (Friese's "when do we reveal what"; Nolan's audience-order writing).
  4. Plant hints that only read correctly in hindsight (Dark's three-worlds image, Jemisin's seeded hints, Yoko Taro's overcast sky).
  5. Keep the middle flexible.
- Uchikoshi's *Ever17* story shows twist-discovery is possible. It worked because the character settings were rich enough to absorb a late twist. A useful rule: dense, specific backstory creates "found" twists.
- Isayama's regret warns against tying a long work to an ending chosen before the theme matured. Rowling's revisions show the healthier pattern: fixed theme and anchors, with negotiable casualties and lines.

### Gaps
- I found no primary source for the commonly repeated claim that Rowling wrote the final chapter around 1990 and kept it in a vault. The fetched Dateline transcript does not say this.
- I did not verify Agatha Christie's working method (her notebooks) or other classic mystery novelists beyond Poe and Horowitz.
- No source was fetched on whether Dark's creators started from the family trees or from the time-loop concept.

## 6. Image- or scene-first: C.S. Lewis, Faulkner, Ishiguro, Hayao Miyazaki, Yoko Taro, Crichton

### Takeaway
Image-first writers start from a charged picture with an unexplained question in it: why is a faun carrying parcels in the snow, and why are the girl's drawers muddy? They then ask who is in the picture and what happened around it. The image supplies tone and a central mystery. The structure is often found by retelling (Faulkner's four tellings) or by drawing (Miyazaki's image boards), and the result is strong atmosphere and emotional unity with a plot that is often improvised.

### Cited Findings
- **C.S. Lewis:** in "It All Began with a Picture," Lewis says *The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe* began with the picture of a faun carrying an umbrella and parcels in a snowy wood. He had carried the image since about age 16, and around 40 he decided to try to make a story about it. — [Goodreads author blog quoting Lewis](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/16510811-how-c-s-lewis-got-the-idea-for-the-lion-the-witch-and-the-wardrobe)
- **William Faulkner:** the novel grew from the image of a little girl's muddy drawers as she climbed a tree to look through a parlor window at her grandmother's funeral while her brothers waited below. He told the story through one brother, found it insufficient, then a second brother, then the third, then in his own voice. He felt Caddy was too beautiful and moving to narrate herself, and said the book evolved as he wrote. — [Biblioklept, transcribing a University of Virginia recording](https://biblioklept.org/2014/12/11/william-faulkner-explains-the-genesis-of-the-sound-and-the-fury/)
- **Hayao Miyazaki:** his image boards are loose, fast concept sketches, not continuity storyboards. For *Horus* (1968) the boards were pinned up for the staff and set the film's direction; Miyazaki called them the material that determined the story's direction. A mid-1970s sketch of a rainy bus stop became *My Neighbor Totoro*'s scene, and an early-1980s floating-castle sketch became *Castle in the Sky*. — [Animation Obsessive, "The Image Boards of Hayao Miyazaki"](https://animationobsessive.substack.com/p/the-image-boards-of-hayao-miyazaki)
- Miyazaki's often-quoted admission that the studio never knows where the story will go and keeps working as it develops appears as a headline at [Creative Bloq](https://www.creativebloq.com/art/animation/quote-of-the-day-by-hayao-miyazaki-its-a-dangerous-way-to-make-an-animated-film-and-i-wish-it-could-be-different-we-never-know-where-the-story-will-go-but-we-just-keep-working-on-the-film-as-it-develops) and [SlashFilm](https://www.slashfilm.com/865373/studio-ghiblis-scripting-process-is-practically-nonexistent). Seen in search titles only; the full articles were not fetched.
- **Yoko Taro's "photo thinking"** (section 5) is an image-first method aimed at an ending: imagine the key emotional image, then reverse-engineer hints. — [Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/b/news/archive/2014/03/20/making-weird-games-for-weird-people.aspx)
- **Michael Crichton:** "I see pictures in my head." He works visually, like watching a movie. — [Publishers Weekly](https://publishersweekly.com/pw/print/19991101/32624-the-high-concept-of-michael-crichton.html)
- **Kazuo Ishiguro:** the starting material for *Never Let Me Go* was a recurring scene-cluster (students in wrecked farmhouses under a strange fate), kept for years until a premise explained it. — [Penguin Random House](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/85609/never-let-me-go-by-kazuo-ishiguro/9781400078776/)
- **Bradbury's noun lists** are image-first in miniature. — [The Marginalian](https://www.themarginalian.org/2013/10/18/ray-bradbury-on-lists/)

### Inferences
- The best starting images contain a question: Lewis's faun, Faulkner's muddy drawers, Ishiguro's fated students. That makes image-first naturally suited to mystery. The opening image can literally become the game's first mystery or its final reveal.
- Faulkner's four tellings are an early model of multi-perspective structure: retelling one event through different characters until it is complete. That is directly relevant to interwoven character threads.
- The risk is drift. Miyazaki's improvised process is workable for a 2-hour film with one author but dangerous for a branching mystery that needs fair clues.

### Gaps
- I could not fetch Ishiguro's Paris Review interview (Art of Fiction No. 196), so his general claims about planning, and his remark that he moves a theme to whatever setting suits it, are unverified here.
- I could not verify Miyazaki's "no script" process in his own words. The full Starting Point essays were not fetched.
- The Faulkner source is a UVA audio transcription via a blog, not the Paris Review.

## 7. How do writers move from the starting point to the other elements? (Order of development and weaving)

### Takeaway
Across sources there is a repeating three-step pattern. (1) Start from a generative seed: image, question, world-tension, situation or emotion. (2) Fuse it quickly with a second element of a different kind, so that a premise meets a character or a world meets a theme. (3) Fix the ending and anchor scenes, then design reveals in audience order. Weaving happens when one theme is expressed simultaneously as a world rule, a character flaw and a plot reversal.

### Cited Findings
- **Herbert:** theme (messiahs) plus world (desert ecology), then a second thread to avoid single-dynamic plot, then institutions (Bene Gesserit, breeding program), then years of research and scene and character notes. — [O'Reilly](https://www.oreilly.com/tim/herbert/ch03.html)
- **Jemisin:** world concept tested by a short story, then theme (oppression) and outline (three plotlines), then the central character's thread drafted first, then voice found in test chapters, then hints planted across threads and pruned in revision. — [Writer's Digest](https://writersdigest.com/be-inspired/the-wd-interview-n-k-jemisin); [Writing Excuses](https://writingexcuses.com/19-46-an-interview-on-structure-with-n-k-jemisin/)
- **Cron:** character misbelief first, plot built to attack it second, scenes as pressure third. — [Turning to Story](https://turningtostory.substack.com/p/the-story-genius-method)
- **Pixar:** spine (#4), ending before middle (#7), theme discovered at the end and rewritten in (#3), core essence then build out (#22). — [Open Culture](https://www.openculture.com/2013/03/pixars_22_rules_of_good_storytelling.html)
- **Egri:** any start (character, incident, thought), but find the premise before finishing, then derive what characters must be to prove it. — [Writers Write](https://www.writerswrite.com/fiction/egri/)
- **Ishiguro:** fragments and characters (years), then a premise as framework, then setting chosen to avoid prophecy, then theme as the unifying emotional aim. — [Penguin Random House](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/85609/never-let-me-go-by-kazuo-ishiguro/9781400078776/)
- **Uchikoshi (ZTD):** theme, then the speculative and philosophical devices that convey it, then constraints from prior canon, then fragment structure planned carefully, then characters allowed to bend it. — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/no-escape-i-zero-time-dilemma-i-and-the-narrative-design-of-kotaro-uchikoshi). VLR: story, then characters extracted from it, then a spreadsheet flow, prototyped and rewritten. — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-storytelling-secrets-of-i-virtue-s-last-reward-i-)
- **Nolan:** structure diagrams first, then writing in audience order. — [TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/christopher-nolan-writing-nonlinear-chronological-order-scripts/)
- **Staten:** place, then building blocks (places and characters), then scripts last, shaped by design. — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/pc/gdc-online-bungie-s-staten-on-building-worlds-not-words)
- **Brooker:** issue, then "what if," then the worst outcome, then a treatment, then the script, then cast and collaborator reshaping. — [TBI Vision](https://tbivision.com/?p=823705)
- **Rowling:** a sketched seven-book plan with anchor scenes and a fixed character truth (Snape). Theme deepened mid-process by life events (death) and was then rewoven back into earlier material. — [Accio Quote](https://accio-quote.org/articles/2007/0729-dateline-vieira.html)

### Inferences
- The "second element" is almost always of a different type from the first. A world gets a theme (Herbert), a premise gets a character (King), a character gets a premise (Ishiguro), a theme gets devices (Uchikoshi). That fusion is where a story becomes a story.
- Weaving device: make the theme operate at three levels. Herbert does this with water: an ecology rule, a cultural value and a political lever. Jemisin does it with oppression: a magic system, a social order and the protagonist's wound.
- Revision is where weaving happens. Pixar #3 (find the theme at the end, then rewrite), Jemisin's hint pruning and Rowling's re-centering on death all show that integration is a revision activity, not a first-draft one.

### Gaps
- No single source offers a cross-writer empirical comparison of development order. The pattern above is my synthesis.

## 8. What do teachers and showrunners say about starting points for long-running, multi-thread or mystery stories, and the risks of each?

### Takeaway
For long multi-thread mysteries, practitioners converge on one point: know the endpoint and the reveal schedule, but keep the middle flexible. Lost's creators wanted an end date from the pilot and admit they never balanced new mysteries against answers. Dark planned its endpoint and three-season shape but moved pieces mid-way. Isayama shows that an ending fixed too rigidly can feel like a cage. Urasawa shows that pure discovery can sustain long serials but leaves setups to luck.

### Cited Findings
- **Damon Lindelof (Lost):**
  - He and Carlton Cuse pitched ending after about three seasons, and talk of ending began "as early as the pilot." ABC resisted.
  - Their answer to "why keep watching after mysteries resolve?" was to introduce new mysteries as old ones were answered. Lindelof admits they did not get that balance right.
  - He also warned that character-backstory flashbacks were finite and would start to feel like treading water.
  - They negotiated an end date (six shortened seasons) to allow planning. The Oceanic 6 arc was worked out by mid-season 3.
  - Source: [Collider, Lindelof on Lost's original plan](https://collider.com/damon-lindelof-lost-original-ending-plan)
- **Dark:** planned three-season shape and fixed end beats, with reveal timing as the hardest problem and pieces shifted between seasons. — [Hollywood Reporter](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/live-feed/dark-final-season-explained-baran-bo-odar-jantje-friese-interview-1302171)
- **Isayama:** ending fixed from the start, later felt as a constraint. — [SlashFilm](https://slashfilm.com/1439560/attack-on-titan-creator-planned-ending-start)
- **Urasawa:** advance planning of every detail would make a 5 to 7 year series impossible. He uses a "trailer" vision plus discovery. — [J-Generation](https://j-generation.com/2019/01/naoki-urasawa-interview-monster-20th-century-boys-2019/)
- **Staten (games):** start with place, not plot; huge canons are unwieldy, so use safe pockets; Halo 2's problems stemmed from over-reach. — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/pc/gdc-online-bungie-s-staten-on-building-worlds-not-words)
- **Uchikoshi (branching games):** in interlocking structures, changing one fragment affects everything around it. Over-reliance on twists is easy and the process is "an imperfect art." — [Game Developer, ZTD](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/no-escape-i-zero-time-dilemma-i-and-the-narrative-design-of-kotaro-uchikoshi). In VLR, an upstream change forced downstream rework. — [Game Developer, VLR](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-storytelling-secrets-of-i-virtue-s-last-reward-i-)
- **Herbert:** no story should have only one thread. — [O'Reilly](https://www.oreilly.com/tim/herbert/ch03.html)
- **Poe:** the denouement must be in view before writing. — [Lit2Go](https://etc.usf.edu/lit2go/147/the-works-of-edgar-allan-poe/5371/the-philosophy-of-composition)
- **Horowitz:** mysteries start from motive and character, and the trickery comes after. — [PBS](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/masterpiece/specialfeatures/how-to-write-a-mystery-anthony-horowitzs-1-tip)
- **Hidetaka Miyazaki (secondhand):** intentional gaps for players to fill. — [Den of Geek](https://www.denofgeek.com/?p=781317)

### Inferences — risk table by entry point (synthesized from the above)
| Entry point | Typical strength | Typical risk | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Character | Emotional pull, organic surprise | Setups found by luck; weak clue fairness | King, Urasawa, Cron |
| World/setting | Depth, many possible plots, iceberg lore | Static world; lore the game cannot deliver | Staten (over-reach), Herbert's one-dynamic draft |
| Theme/question | Coherence, meaning | Preachiness; unresolved question | Truby, Egri, Chiang's ending rule |
| Premise/"what if" | Hook, clarity | Thin characters; gimmick | Crichton's camera-eye, Brooker's worst-case search |
| Ending/twist | Fair clues, payoff, hindsight resonance | Rigidity (Isayama); twist without meaning (Shyamalan starts from meaning instead) | Poe, Yoko Taro, Dark, Rowling |
| Image/scene | Tone, a built-in central mystery | Improvised plot; drift | Lewis, Faulkner, Miyazaki |

### Gaps
- I did not fetch writing-teacher material written specifically for long-form TV mystery structure (e.g., showrunner guides on "mystery box" theory such as J.J. Abrams's TED talk), so that perspective is missing.
- No sourced statement from Bungie on how Halo's lore was managed across later games (e.g., the Halo story bible) was fetched.

## 9. Analysis: which entry point or combination suits a very complex mystery game (AGI, aliens, cloning, time travel, multiple interwoven threads, iceberg lore)?

### Takeaway
No single entry point fits. The evidence points to a layered hybrid:
1. **Theme as a question** (Le Guin, Dick, Uchikoshi's ZTD) to unify the many sci-fi conceits.
2. **The hidden "true history" and ending** (Poe, Chiang, Dark, Yoko Taro), fixed early as the iceberg's base: what actually happened across all timelines, and the final emotional effect.
3. **The world as a bounded system of rules and tensions** (Herbert, Jemisin, Staten's safe pockets) that generates the lore.
4. **Characters with motives and misbeliefs** (Horowitz, Cron), derived from and reacting against that hidden history.
5. **One or a few key images or scenes** (Lewis, Faulkner, Yoko Taro's photo thinking) as anchor moments the reveals are built toward.

The reveal order is then designed separately from the event order (Nolan, Friese).

### Cited Findings (supporting the recommendation)
- **Theme or question to unify disparate conceits:**
  - Herbert's desert premise alone gave a single dynamic, and merging it with a theme made the novel. — [O'Reilly](https://www.oreilly.com/tim/herbert/ch03.html)
  - Dick's reality and authenticity questions map directly onto AGI and cloning. — [The Marginalian](https://www.themarginalian.org/2013/09/06/how-to-build-a-universe-philip-k-dick/)
  - Uchikoshi chose ZTD's speculative devices to serve a central theme. — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/no-escape-i-zero-time-dilemma-i-and-the-narrative-design-of-kotaro-uchikoshi)
- **Ending and true history first, for time travel and fair mysteries:**
  - Poe's denouement-first rule. — [Lit2Go](https://etc.usf.edu/lit2go/147/the-works-of-edgar-allan-poe/5371/the-philosophy-of-composition)
  - Chiang will not draft without the ending. — [Electric Literature](https://electricliterature.com/ted-chiang-interview/)
  - Dark fixed its end beats and three-part shape, which matters for a time-loop story. — [Hollywood Reporter](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/live-feed/dark-final-season-explained-baran-bo-odar-jantje-friese-interview-1302171)
  - Lindelof's admitted imbalance shows what happens without a planned answer schedule. — [Collider](https://collider.com/damon-lindelof-lost-original-ending-plan)
- **Interlocking structures punish late changes:** in Uchikoshi's fragment and AB-game structures, upstream changes cascade. — [Game Developer ZTD](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/no-escape-i-zero-time-dilemma-i-and-the-narrative-design-of-kotaro-uchikoshi); [Game Developer VLR](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-storytelling-secrets-of-i-virtue-s-last-reward-i-)
- **But keep room for discovery:**
  - Uchikoshi's *Ever17* twist was found late and fit because the character settings were rich. — [Noisy Pixel](https://noisypixel.net/kotaro-uchikoshi-interview-infinity-zero-escape-ai-series/)
  - Isayama felt caged by a rigid early ending. — [SlashFilm](https://slashfilm.com/1439560/attack-on-titan-creator-planned-ending-start)
  - Rowling changed casualties while keeping anchors. — [Accio Quote](https://accio-quote.org/articles/2007/0729-dateline-vieira.html)
- **Multiple interwoven threads:**
  - Jemisin's three-POV structure was planned around one hidden identity reveal, with hints seeded in all threads and pruned with beta readers. — [Writing Excuses](https://writingexcuses.com/19-46-an-interview-on-structure-with-n-k-jemisin/)
  - Faulkner retold one event through several perspectives. — [Biblioklept](https://biblioklept.org/2014/12/11/william-faulkner-explains-the-genesis-of-the-sound-and-the-fury/)
  - Uchikoshi used opposing character pairs plus a decoy villain. — [Game Developer VLR](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-storytelling-secrets-of-i-virtue-s-last-reward-i-)
- **Iceberg lore that games can deliver:**
  - Staten's place-before-plot rule, safe pockets and warning about over-reach. — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/pc/gdc-online-bungie-s-staten-on-building-worlds-not-words)
  - FromSoftware's deliberate gaps (secondhand). — [Den of Geek](https://www.denofgeek.com/?p=781317)
  - Uchikoshi hints at science rather than explaining it. — [Game Developer VLR](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-storytelling-secrets-of-i-virtue-s-last-reward-i-)
- **Emotional payoff:**
  - Yoko Taro's backwards scriptwriting puts the emotional event at the center and spreads its sadness-making details earlier. — [Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/b/news/archive/2014/03/20/making-weird-games-for-weird-people.aspx)
  - Pixar #7: decide the ending before the middle. — [Open Culture](https://www.openculture.com/2013/03/pixars_22_rules_of_good_storytelling.html)

### Inferences (the recommended workflow, my synthesis)
1. **Seed: a central question, not an answer.** Example shape: "What makes a self continuous: memory, body, or choice?" This lets AGI, cloning, time travel and aliens each act as a different experiment on one question (Le Guin's thought experiment, Dick's authenticity question). Use Egri's test that the author takes a side, but carry the argument through structure (Truby), never speeches.
2. **Write the hidden chronology ("what really happened") and the final emotional image before anything player-facing.** For time travel this is non-negotiable, because a loop must be causally closed (Dark). Pick one or two "photo thinking" images (Yoko Taro) as the emotional climax. Ideally the opening image should contain a question that the ending answers (Lewis, Faulkner).
3. **Build the world as rules plus tensions, in safe pockets.** Each conceit should have a few hard rules and a scarcity or conflict, like Herbert's water. Prototype pockets with small side stories before committing (Jemisin's proof-of-concept stories; Staten's building blocks).
4. **Derive characters from the hidden history.** For each thread, define a motive (Horowitz), a misbelief the plot will attack (Cron), and a secret connection to the true chronology. Pair characters in oppositions and include a decoy (Uchikoshi). Make some threads secretly the same person or entity seen from different times or copies (Jemisin's one-person reveal, which fits cloning and time travel).
5. **Design the reveal schedule separately from event order, and write in player order** (Nolan, Friese). Map the "mystery ledger" so answers and new questions stay balanced, avoiding Lindelof's admitted imbalance.
6. **Lock anchors and keep the middle flexible.** Fix the ending, the core twist and a handful of anchor scenes. Let the connective tissue change (Rowling, Dark). Avoid Isayama-style rigidity by fixing the ending's *meaning* more firmly than its exact events. Note that Shyamalan anchors on meaning, not twist.
7. **Do a revision pass for weaving.** Re-seed clues so they read differently in hindsight, and prune the most obvious ones with test readers or players (Jemisin; Pixar #3).
- **Net answer:** a theme-question seed, fused immediately with a fixed hidden history and ending (ending-first), built out through a rule-bound world (world-second), and populated by motive-driven characters (character-third), anchored on a few vivid images. Pure character-first or pure image-first are poor fits as the *governing* method for this genre, though both are valuable inside the threads. Pure world-first risks lore without drama unless it is tied to the question and the ending.

### Gaps
- The recommendation is a synthesis. No single sourced practitioner prescribes this exact layering for a multi-conceit sci-fi mystery game.
- I found no primary interview with Uchikoshi describing the *AI: The Somnium Files* writing order, and no Kojima or other large sci-fi mystery game writer was researched. Those could test or refine the recommendation.
