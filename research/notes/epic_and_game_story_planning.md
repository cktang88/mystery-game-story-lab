# How Professional Teams Plan Epic, Long-Form and Multi-Thread Stories (TV, Film, Animation, Games, Prose)

Scope note: These notes cover planning pipelines, documents, roles and continuity methods. They are meant to inform planning of an original, highly complex mystery game (AGI, aliens, cloning, time travel, interwoven character threads, deep lore; not point-and-click, not text-heavy). Every claim has an inline source. Anything I could not source is listed under "Gaps". Direct quotes are under 15 words, with at most one per source.

---

## 1. TV writers' rooms: breaking a season, and how serialized mystery shows planned long arcs

### Takeaway
A writers' room works from the top down. First comes a treatment or bible. Then the season arc is set (start point and end point). Then each episode gets a paragraph. Then episodes are "broken" beat by beat on a whiteboard or corkboard into acts. Then come 8 to 12 page outlines, and only then scripts. The serialized mysteries that held together (Dark, Severance, Babylon 5) had a showrunner who already knew the answers, plus a written plan with escape hatches. The ones that frayed (Lost, BSG) lacked an end date or a fixed answer to their central question, and wrote "the plan" into the premise before it existed.

### Cited Findings

**General season-breaking pipeline (who does what, in what order)**
- Z Nation is a documented example of a genre room. The room had seven writers plus an assistant, led by the showrunner. The showrunner was described as both the show's creative head and its general manager. Before the room met, writers read the showrunner's expanded treatment. The first days went to "blue sky" talk about direction, reference stories, and how the show would differ from others in its genre — [Gizmodo, Craig Engler, "Inside the Writers' Room"](https://gizmodo.com/so-you-want-to-make-a-genre-tv-show-part-2-inside-the-writers-room-1635438731)
- In the same room, each character was built out with a backstory, flaws and an individual arc. The writers also looked for thematic threads, for example a physical crossroads that doubles as an emotional one. A big U.S. map on the wall tracked the characters' 3,000-mile journey, which is an example of a spatial continuity aid for the room — [Gizmodo/Engler](https://gizmodo.com/so-you-want-to-make-a-genre-tv-show-part-2-inside-the-writers-room-1635438731)
- Order of documents in that room: treatment, then 13 episodes described in paragraph form and sent to the network for approval. Next, each approved episode was "broken" on a whiteboard into a teaser plus six acts, with beats listed per act. Then came 8 to 12 page outlines, which got producer and network notes before a final version. Then each writer took one outline and wrote the script. The room broke only the first 7 episodes before drafting began, so breaking and writing overlapped — [Gizmodo/Engler](https://gizmodo.com/so-you-want-to-make-a-genre-tv-show-part-2-inside-the-writers-room-1635438731)
- A generic season-breaking guide gives this order: set the core concept and genre, then fix where the season starts and ends and "connect the dots" as the season arc, then add subplots, then write per-episode outlines (each episode self-contained but serving the arc), then balance standalone and serialized episodes, then map each character's start, end and turning points, then plan twists and cliffhangers, then track consistency through regular room meetings — [No Film School, "How to Break a TV Season"](https://nofilmschool.com/how-to-break-a-tv-season)

**Breaking Bad (Vince Gilligan): structured beats, deliberate corners**
- The writers discussed every beat of each episode in a Burbank room, wrote the beats on index cards, and pinned them to a board. The finale was built the same way — [NPR, "Breaking Bad Writers: This Is It; There's No More"](https://www.npr.org/2013/10/03/228813142/breaking-bad-writers-this-is-it-theres-no-more)
- Peter Gould described scripts with a firm structure in which every scene had to be justified, but with gaps left for writers to discover details while drafting. Gilligan's guidance was "Feel your way through it." To find the ending, the room kept asking when Walt would finally see himself clearly. Its answer was that the show ends once he does. They debated every possible ending before choosing — [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2013/10/03/228813142/breaking-bad-writers-this-is-it-theres-no-more)
- Gilligan has said the team wrote itself "into a corner a little bit" with Season 5 flash-forwards (Walt with hair, a $100 tip, an M60 in a car trunk). They began from striking images and only later asked what they meant. He had broad ideas of payoffs but little detail, and he advises new showrunners against working this way. Getting out of the corners cost him months of anxiety. Solving one corner, Walt and Jesse trapped in the RV with Hank outside, took seven people three or four days — [TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/vince-gilligan-relishes-anxiety-painting-himself-corners-breaking-bad-110096/)
- Writer Thomas Schnauz confirms that cliffhangers were sometimes written with no known way out. Jesse was originally going to die at the end of Season 1 — [The Week, "8 revelations from inside the Breaking Bad writers' room"](https://theweek.com/articles/461639/8-revelations-from-inside-breaking-bad-writers-room)

**Dark (Baran bo Odar and Jantje Friese)**
- Friese says many destinations were known in advance, but not where in the narrative to reveal them. She calls "when do we reveal what?" the biggest structural challenge. The parallel world was first slated for Season 2 but was moved when they got there, as they put it, by shifting the puzzle pieces around. Some fixed points were held from early on: the dinner scene, saving Regina, and the decision that Woller's eye would deliberately have no answer — [The Hollywood Reporter, Dark final-season interview](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/live-feed/dark-final-season-explained-baran-bo-odar-jantje-friese-interview-1302171)
- Odar describes visual continuity rules for multiple timelines, for example not reusing a color palette already assigned to another decade. Budget limits shaped how the mirror world was shown: they mirrored the camera image instead of rebuilding sets — [THR](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/live-feed/dark-final-season-explained-baran-bo-odar-jantje-friese-interview-1302171)
- After Season 1, Odar said some paradoxes were deliberately left without answers, comparing them to the chicken-and-egg question — [TheWrap, Dark Season 1 interview](https://www.thewrap.com/dark-showrunners-answer-nagging-questions-season-1-tease-plans-season-2)

**Severance (Dan Erickson)**
- Season 2 writer Mohamad El Masri says he was given a roughly 50-page show bible. The room spent its early months clarifying core questions such as what Lumon wants. Erickson acts as "the answer man" who has the mythology mapped in his head. The room still had to work out how the goats, the refining and the Eagan family's role fit together, on the principle that everything has to connect — [Winter Is Coming, El Masri interview](https://winteriscoming.net/posts/severance-writer-assures-us-that-all-the-mysteries-link-up-and-make-sense-exclusive-01j9c2q931pg)

**Babylon 5 (J. Michael Straczynski): the five-year arc with "trap doors"**
- Straczynski planned a five-season novel for television. A seven-page synopsis, reprinted in the script books, laid out the original plan, in which Sinclair was the lead for the whole run — [Reactor, "Babylon 5 was the ultimate exercise in plotting vs. pantsing"](https://reactormag.com/babylon-5-was-the-ultimate-exercise-in-plotting-vs-pantsing/)
- He built "trap doors": alternative resolutions for each main character that branch off the main story, so the arc could survive an actor leaving. These were used when Michael O'Hare (Sinclair) had to leave after Season 1. Takashima's planned "sleeper personality" plot passed to Talia Winters. When the network appeared to be shutting down, he compressed the Shadow War and Earth Civil War into Season 4. The series finale was shot early and held back. He describes himself as a plotter who still stays open to a stronger path when it appears — [Reactor](https://reactormag.com/babylon-5-was-the-ultimate-exercise-in-plotting-vs-pantsing/)

**Counterexamples: Lost and Battlestar Galactica**
- Lindelof says he and Cuse first pitched ending Lost after about three seasons, with key mysteries answered in Seasons 1 and 2. ABC refused, because networks do not end shows that people are watching. He admits mysteries piled up faster than answers ("we did not get that balance right"). Flashbacks began to feel like treading water by mid-Season 3. They negotiated an end date and settled on six seasons with fewer episodes each, after ABC floated ten — [Collider, Lindelof interview](https://collider.com/damon-lindelof-lost-original-ending-plan)
- Ron Moore admitted at the Austin TV Festival that the Cylons had no plan. The phrase about the Cylons having a plan was added to the title card as a punchy line, and David Eick told him they would figure it out later. Eick later said the plan "kept shifting" and that a clearer one only formed in Season 3 — [/Film](https://www.slashfilm.com/846604/not-even-the-writers-knew-the-cylons-plan-in-battlestar-galactica)

### Inferences
- The professional order of work is: concept or treatment, then bible, then season arc (start and end), then a paragraph per episode, then beat-level breaks on a board, then outlines, then scripts. Each stage is reviewed before the next. For a game, the equivalent stack is world/lore bible, then the "season" (whole-game) arc, then chapter paragraphs, then beat boards, then chapter outlines, then scripts and level docs.
- Breaking Bad's lesson for a mystery is that improvising can work for character drama, but it is costly when a flash-forward or puzzle-box setup has already been promised. Mystery answers must be fixed before setups ship.
- Dark's lesson: fix the answers (destinations) early, and treat the reveal order as the main thing you keep tuning.
- Babylon 5's trap doors map neatly onto game production. For each character thread, keep a fallback resolution ready in case content is cut (scope, budget, VO cast changes).
- BSG's lesson: never put "they have a plan" into marketing or the premise unless the plan exists in writing.

### Gaps
- I could not fetch a primary source showing Dark's physical writers' room wall or family-tree chart. Fan-made character maps exist, but no confirmed in-house document was found. Whether all three seasons were plotted before Season 1 aired is not confirmed in the interviews I fetched.
- I found no fetched source on The Wire's season planning (season-per-institution design, novelist writers). A BuzzFeed piece only covers Simon's habit of hiring novelists and journalists ([BuzzFeed News](https://www.buzzfeednews.com/article/megcramer/how-david-simon-builds-a-writing-team)).
- No fetched source gives a full A/B/C-story or act-break template beyond Z Nation's teaser plus six acts.
- THR's Severance interview could not be fetched (site error).

## 2. Storyboarding and pre-visualization (Pixar, Miyazaki, anime series composition)

### Takeaway
In animation, storyboards are the rewrite stage. A Pixar film is "written" again and again through boards cut into story reels with scratch audio. Reels are screened roughly every three months and critiqued by the Braintrust. Onward used about 97,759 boards. Miyazaki goes further and skips the script: the storyboard is the screenplay, which is risky and openly acknowledged as such. In TV anime, a series composition writer (shirīzu kōsei) owns the season's scope and episode distribution, much like a showrunner's arc plan.

### Cited Findings

**Pixar**
- For Onward, the Head of Story (Kelsey Mann), the director (Dan Scanlon) and the producer started in September 2013. Scene ideas were "carded": written on notecards and arranged on a large bulletin board, then added, removed and reordered. Notes from a small group of writers and story artists drove script rewrites — [/Film, "Creating the Story of Onward"](https://slashfilm.com/making-of-onward-cracking-the-story)
- The pipeline per scene works like this. Artists get the director's description and a script page. They board it (in a room called "The Fish Bowl") and pitch it to the story team, editors, art department, director and producer, using Pitch Doctor software so they can redraw quickly during notes. Writers and the director record scratch dialogue. Editorial cuts the boards together with scratch audio and effects and reviews the cut with the artist. This repeats until a rough cut of the whole film exists. Onward used 97,759 storyboards — [/Film](https://slashfilm.com/making-of-onward-cracking-the-story)
- Screenings happen about every three months per film, covering roughly 35 to 40 scenes each. Each scene is revised and rewatched until the director locks it. Storyboards act as rewrites: a story artist's suggested line went into the script, and boards changed the camera to Ian's point of view in the chasm scene — [Screen Rant, Kelsey Mann on Onward](https://screenrant.com/pixar-onward-storytelling-high-bar-story-artists-kelsey-mann/)
- Braintrust sessions last about two hours. Top creatives give peer feedback on a rough cut or on storyboards pinned around a room, focusing on make-or-break decision moments. Notes are suggestions and the director decides. Any team member can flag a problem. Story overhauls are normal: Toy Story 2 was reworked in about eight months, Ratatouille and Brave changed directors, and Toy Story 4's original outline was dropped — [Fandom, "How Pixar Made Big Group Collaboration Work"](https://www.fandom.com/articles/pixar-collaboration-teams)
- Catmull's reason for the Braintrust is that creators on a long project lose perspective and stop seeing the forest for the trees. A director may assume something is visible on screen when it exists only in their head. Fixes range from clarifying a setup to starting over — [Longreads, excerpt of Creativity, Inc.](https://longreads.com/2014/03/13/the-secret-behind-pixars-storytelling-process/)
- On Incredibles 2, the editor attended every story pitch from first to last and built first cuts with story artists. Production was flexible enough that whole characters and storylines could be added or dropped over a schedule of five years or more — [Frame.io blog](https://blog.frame.io/2018/06/18/making-incredibles-2/)

**Hayao Miyazaki (storyboard-first, no script)**
- Miyazaki often storyboards before any script exists. The story develops as he draws, and production begins while the boards are still changing. He calls this a dangerous way to make an animated film and says it is hard on animators. Spirited Away was planned at about 1,200 shots but needed 1,415. Film length and schedule are hard to predict — [/Film, citing Midnight Eye 2002 interview](https://www.slashfilm.com/865373/studio-ghiblis-scripting-process-is-practically-nonexistent)

**Anime series composition (shirīzu kōsei)**
- The pipeline starts with a production proposal (pitch document with story outline, which often changes later). After greenlight, the series composer plans the series' scope, divides content across episodes and sets the main storylines. Some composers write every episode; others write the key ones and assign the rest. Scriptwriters expand each episode's points into about 20 minutes of action and dialogue. The series director has the final word and oversees scripts with the composer. Literature staff manage deadlines and sit in on script meetings. Scripts then go to episode directors or storyboarders, who may alter them. Design work runs in parallel — [Sakugabooru blog, "The Pre-Production of Anime #2: Scripting"](https://blog.sakugabooru.com/2017/07/05/the-pre-production-of-anime-2-scripting/)

### Inferences
- For a game that is not text-heavy, Pixar's reel loop is the closest model. Represent each story beat visually (boards or animatics with scratch VO) early, cut them together in play order, and screen them on a fixed cadence to a "braintrust" of people outside the project. Story reels reveal clarity problems, such as a reveal that relies on information only the writer knows, months before costly cinematics or levels are built.
- For a game, the equivalent of a "story reel" might be a graybox playthrough with placeholder VO and storyboard stills in cutscene slots. Tools named in game sources (SketchUp layout, Visio beat flow; see section 3) support this.
- Miyazaki's method is unsafe for a mystery. Board-first discovery works for mood-driven films, but a mystery needs fixed answers. Use boards to discover the surface (scenes, staging), not the solution.
- The series composer role suggests appointing one "composition" owner who decides which chapter or episode carries which slice of the lore and mystery. This can be a different person from the dialogue writers.

### Gaps
- I did not fetch a Pixar in a Box source. Pixar's exact reel count per film and the formal "story supervisor" title were not confirmed. The Toy Story 2 "ugly babies" framing from Creativity, Inc. was not fetched directly.
- No fetched source detailed the anime "hon-yomi" (script readthrough) meeting by name, or how many drafts are typical.
- Miyazaki on Ponyo and The Boy and the Heron was not covered by the source I fetched.

## 3. Video game narrative pipelines (documents, tools, writer-designer coordination, story change)

### Takeaway
Game narrative is planned in layers. The concept and theme come first. Next comes a story outline with world and character dossiers and a timeline (the bible). Then a whole-game sequence spreadsheet or beat chart maps story beats to gameplay per chapter. Then per-chapter "chapter outlines" or mission docs carry beat flows and intensity graphs. Finally come dialogue and scripting in tools like articy:draft or ink. Lore bibles become version-controlled internal wikis with gatekeepers (Halo). Story is expected to change because levels get cut, and writers act as "narrative paramedics." Leading studios therefore lock theme, ending and core beats early and keep the surface fluid.

### Cited Findings

**Eidos Montreal, Deus Ex: Human Revolution, "The Blueprint" (the whole game in a spreadsheet)**
- The leads (producer, game director, lead designer, art director) first brainstormed concepts on paper and conference-room walls. The documents then came in stages: high-level concept, story concept, a conspiracy story outline, and detailed dossiers on characters, corporations, world state, plus a historical timeline. A central theme question tied story to gameplay — [Game Developer, Mary DeMarle GDC Online talk](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/gdc-online-the-design-document-that-made-i-deus-ex-human-revolution-i-possible)
- The Blueprint was an Excel file that broke every sequence into smaller chunks. For each one it mapped gameplay elements interwoven with narrative: scripted events, unusual gameplay, dialogue, NPC behavior, and choices and consequences. DeMarle said branching had to be tracked per sequence to keep paths coherent — [Game Developer/DeMarle](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/gdc-online-the-design-document-that-made-i-deus-ex-human-revolution-i-possible)
- Process: for each sequence, key people from all disciplines met daily, about 10am to 5pm, for roughly three months. The story team stated goals and everyone proposed gameplay to meet them. Changes flowed both ways. Milestone "gate meetings" (for example at first playable) served as peer review. The team still consulted the Blueprint two years later — [Game Developer/DeMarle](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/gdc-online-the-design-document-that-made-i-deus-ex-human-revolution-i-possible)

**Naughty Dog, The Last of Us**
- Bruce Straley and Neil Druckmann brainstormed for months, then laid the game out on a corkboard of pinned note cards showing locations and major story events. Some cards survived (Joel hurt in winter, the cannibal town). Others changed: Tess was first planned as a villain ("Ellie kills Tess") — [GameSpot](https://www.gamespot.com/articles/see-how-the-last-of-us-was-originally-envisioned-u/1100-6421393/)
- Druckmann's process (from a DICE talk): start from the major beats and arc, working backward from the ending, which he treats as the story's essence. Brainstorm on index cards. Hand the team an outline before shooting. Brief developers on tone and aesthetic in a long recorded session they can refer back to — [GamingBolt](https://gamingbolt.com/the-last-of-us-part-2-writer-and-director-discusses-his-creative-process)
- A "Chapter Outline" format (the term is borrowed from Naughty Dog), shown with a Last of Us style example, has these sections: narrative question; story goal and concrete objectives; target length (for example about 20 minutes) and constraints; synopsis, emotions and themes; mechanics (at most one new one); enemies; numbered gameplay beats, each with a reference image, space description and intended outcome; and the ending in-game cinematic written shot by shot. Supporting artifacts: a SketchUp layout prototype, a Scrivener research file (timeline, motivations, motifs), a Visio beat flow color-coded by location and intensity, an intensity graph with deliberate down beats, a PureRef mood board, and a color script — [Game Developer, "Creating a narrative-focused mission design document"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/creating-a-narrative-focused-mission-design-document-a-last-of-us-example)
- The same source recommends putting narrative moments on down beats rather than in combat, and telling story in play ("stay on stick") rather than through long cutscenes — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/creating-a-narrative-focused-mission-design-document-a-last-of-us-example)

**Bungie / Microsoft / 343 (Halo Story Bible, lore version control)**
- The Halo Story Bible was created during Halo: CE production (around 2000 to 2001) with Microsoft's Franchise Development Group. It had numbered versions (v5.0 around late 2001, v6.0 by April 2003). It began as printed ring binders. Named "keepers" (Robert McLees, Frank O'Connor) controlled it. Around 2008 a contractor (Starlight Runner) expanded it to about 2,000 pages. Around 2010 it was four binders in a locked cabinet, with sections sent to outside authors as needed. 343 later turned it into an internal wiki, the "Halo Library," maintained by a Franchise Team of named writers. Unpublished bible material is treated as non-canon until published — [Halopedia, "Halo Story Bible"](https://www.halopedia.org/Halo_Story_Bible)

**Writer-designer coordination and story change in AAA**
- Walt Williams describes writers joining pre-production and brainstorming with every team while the creative director decides what sticks. A writer's power depends on whether the creative director thinks like a writer. Rhianna Pratchett's term "narrative paramedic" describes writers called in to repair story after levels are cut. Cut cutscenes get rewritten to keep the same beat within new limits. Cinematic scripts get trimmed for budget (for example 90 down to 70 minutes). Dialogue is revised even after VO. Rocksteady gave writers ownership of specific systems or characters to keep each voice consistent, and its writers played Arkham Knight for months to tune ambient dialogue — [PC Gamer, "How blockbuster games are written"](https://www.pcgamer.com/uk/how-blockbuster-games-are-written)

**Tools: articy:draft and ink**
- Knock Knock Games used articy:draft as its master content database. Entities (items, units, missions) are built from templates of typed fields. Dialogue is built as flow fragments with global variables and hubs for branching. Data exports to Unity/Unreal as C# objects or JSON, and spreadsheets export for localization. Using articy flows for campaign progression proved impractical (save/load restore issues), so mission fragments became data containers driven by game code — [Game Developer, "How we use articy:draft"](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/effective-game-design-how-we-use-articy-draft-to-organize-game-content)
- inkle wrote all of Overboard's narrative and interactivity in its open-source ink language, connected to Unity. Content was tagged with knowledge state as it was written, to cut continuity errors (details in section 4) — [Game Developer, Overboard](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/how-get-away-murder-overboard)

**Spike Chunsoft / Kotaro Uchikoshi**
- Uchikoshi's stated order of work is: core theme, then main storyline and "main gimmick or twist," then character backgrounds and central drama, then fitting character drama and mystery into the plot. He wrote AI: The Somnium Files – nirvanA Initiative's scenario from spring to fall 2020 and gave the character designer written descriptions to interpret — [Nintendo Life interview](https://www.nintendolife.com/features/zero-escape-creator-talks-ai-the-somnium-files-nirvana-initiative)
- For Zero Time Dilemma, the first constraint was resolving the mysteries left by Virtue's Last Reward without contradicting earlier setups. Because players reorder the fragments, progression had to be planned carefully, since changing one fragment affects all its neighbors. He worked with two other writers and let characters change his original structure — [Game Developer, Uchikoshi narrative design](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/no-escape-i-zero-time-dilemma-i-and-the-narrative-design-of-kotaro-uchikoshi)
- Uchikoshi's own tongue-in-cheek pipeline for Zero Escape is still informative: plan approval, then plot approval, then the main scenario. Parallel tracks follow: character profiles leading to design and 3D, escape-room design with the team leading to backgrounds and scripting, novel-section scripting, and sound. Then scenario to recorded script to VO implementation, then debug cycles — [Siliconera](https://www.siliconera.com/how-zero-escape-games-are-developed-through-the-eyes-of-the-director/)

**Others**
- Vanillaware's 13 Sentinels: pitched in 2013 and released in Japan in 2019. It was modeled on American thriller TV, with season-like blocks of about 10 episodes, and told one story across 13 playable characters with stacked foreshadowed mysteries — [Siliconera, Akiyasu Yamamoto interview](https://www.siliconera.com/interview-understanding-13-sentinels-aegis-rim-with-vanillawares-akiyasu-yamamoto/)
- Lucas Pope's Obra Dinn: the visuals (1-bit) came first, then the death-deduction concept, then the late shift to "who they are" as the main logic puzzle. The crew size (about 60, cut from 80) was fixed before the story. The story was split into chapters or "disasters" linked by an unbroken chain of bodies, which he found extremely hard. Tools built for the 4-character demo did not scale to 60, costing almost a year — [Game Developer, Pope interview](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/for-lucas-pope-i-return-of-the-obra-dinn-i-was-a-bunch-of-appealing-design-problems); [Gamereactor](https://www.gamereactor.eu/lucas-pope-found-structure-of-obra-dinn-extremely-difficult/)
- BioWare's Mass Effect: former lead writer Drew Karpyshyn says the original trilogy ending idea (dark energy) was "not super fleshed out." It was dropped after the lead writer changed between games — [PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/mass-effect-3-series-former-lead-writer-reveals-original-ending-ideas)
- FromSoftware: a long-time localizer (Ryan Morris, Frognation) believes the lore may exist mainly in Hidetaka Miyazaki's head rather than in a written canon. This is outside speculation, not a studio statement — [GamesRadar](https://gamesradar.com/games/action-rpg/long-time-dark-souls-and-elden-ring-dev-thinks-miyazaki-stores-the-entire-lore-in-his-head)
- Larian (Baldur's Gate 3): writer Adam Smith describes the job as a Dungeon Master's, building conversations to reflect even small player choices and filling in lore "just out of scene" from existing Forgotten Realms material — [Den of Geek](https://www.denofgeek.com/games/baldurs-gate-3-dungeon-master-story-writing/)

### Inferences
- The Deus Ex Blueprint and the Chapter Outline are the most directly copyable artifacts for a non-text-heavy game. They force every story beat to have a gameplay delivery, which matters when dialogue volume is low.
- The Halo history shows a lore bible needs version numbers, a named keeper, access control, and a rule that unpublished lore stays changeable until it ships.
- The Mass Effect and FromSoftware examples show the risk of an ending or lore that is vague or lives in one person's head. For a mystery, that is unacceptable. The solution must be written down.
- Obra Dinn shows that cast and scope numbers, once fixed, constrain story structure. Lock them deliberately and test the pipeline at full scale early.

### Gaps
- Remedy (Control, Alan Wake 2): the interview I fetched held no process detail. I found no sourced account of Remedy's plot board or the Remedy Connected Universe planning documents.
- I found no sourced detail on Twine in professional pipelines (it is usually cited as a prototyping tool, but I did not verify this).
- I found no sourced detail on 13 Sentinels' internal scenario management (flowchart tools, years of writing). Larian's flag and reactivity tracking tools were not covered by the fetched source.
- BioWare's plot-tracking documents (for example, choice-tracking across trilogy saves) were not sourced.

## 4. Planning nonlinear, multi-route or player-ordered mysteries (clue graphs, three clue rule, knowledge gates)

### Takeaway
Work backward from the conclusions. List every revelation the player must reach. Give each one at least three independent clues (the Three Clue Rule). Make each location ("node") hold at least three outgoing clues and have at least three incoming clues (the inverted rule). Use loops and multi-revelation gates so order does not matter but the finale can't be skipped. Then check reachability: through tracking (ship log, knowledge model), automated playthroughs, and pruning of hard-to-find information.

### Cited Findings

**Three Clue Rule (Justin Alexander)**
- The rule: for any conclusion players must reach, include at least three clues. Players will miss one, ignore one, and misread one. A single clue is really a chain of chokepoints (search the room, examine the object, pass a check, make the deduction), and each link can fail. The designer knows the answer and so cannot judge what is "obvious" — [The Alexandrian, "Three Clue Rule"](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/1118/roleplaying-games/three-clue-rule)
- Generalization: non-chokepoints need one solution, but chokepoints need at least three. Worked example: a werewolf killer case with three required conclusions (he's a werewolf, he's a former lover, check the butcher shop), each with three clues, giving nine paths — [The Alexandrian](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/1118/roleplaying-games/three-clue-rule)
- Corollaries: permissive clue-finding (reward clever approaches you didn't plan). Proactive clues: when players stall, make something happen (another victim, the villain moves), with each event carrying new clues. Red herrings are overrated, and if used they need an unmistakable refutation. Keep a "revelation list" as a checklist: tick off clues as found and conclusions only when the players actually adopt them, then add clues where gaps appear — [The Alexandrian](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/1118/roleplaying-games/three-clue-rule)

**Node-based design and the inverted rule**
- Each node should hold at least three clues and have at least three clues pointing to it (the inverted Three Clue Rule). Clues pointing to dead ends are "bonus" clues that do not count toward the three. In a loop, four nodes each point to the other three, so players can enter anywhere. To stop a clue from letting players skip the loop to the finale, either trigger the conclusion as a proactive event after the loop, or require several revelations (each with its own three clues) to unlock it, like needing three artifacts to open a temple — [The Alexandrian, Node-Based Scenario Design Part 7](https://thealexandrian.net/?p=8032)

**Outer Wilds (knowledge-gated progression)**
- An analysis of Mobius's design describes a web of "Curiosities" and "Clues." Each Curiosity is linked to about three Clues on different planets and needs several pieces of knowledge to reach. The analysis notes this was messier in practice. The only required step is getting the launch codes; after that the game is designed to be explored in any order. The ship log shows a "rumor" graph of connections, with unvisited items as question marks. Playtesting showed some players were uncomfortable without an objective, so a museum NPC now asks the player's plan and offers multiple-choice answers. These points are the author's analysis, citing GDC talks by Alex Beachum (2019) and Kelsey Beachum (2021) — [Learning Design Insights from Outer Wilds](https://rigdern.github.io/a/learning-about-learning/learning-design-insights-from-outer-wilds/)

**Her Story (Sam Barlow)**
- Barlow first planned to gate footage behind keywords as a puzzle, then dropped that. He wrote documents on every character's life before writing dialogue. He kept a per-interview-day map of what the detectives knew and where each interviewee stood. He then ran the dialogue through a spreadsheet that counted word frequency and flagged answers that would be too hard to find by search, and he pruned or reworked those, without ever scripting a set player path — [GamesRadar, "The story behind Her Story"](https://www.gamesradar.com/making-her-story)

**Overboard! (inkle): knowledge modeling and automated reachability testing**
- inkle reused Heaven's Vault's "knowledge modeling," which tracks what the player and each character know and believe, and tagged content with it as it was written. The final scene is a non-linear "core spine" (the default if the player does nothing) with player-triggered "patches" inserted where valid. An autoplayer ran thousands of playthroughs per hour (more than 100,000 loops in total), with assertions that crash the build if, for example, a dead character speaks, the player runs out of choices, or nothing interesting happens for too long. A "Questions" menu was added to steer replays toward unseen content without spoilers — [Game Developer, Overboard](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/how-get-away-murder-overboard)

**Order-independence in fixed narratives**
- Uchikoshi says that with reorderable fragments, changing one fragment ripples into everything around it — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/no-escape-i-zero-time-dilemma-i-and-the-narrative-design-of-kotaro-uchikoshi)
- Pope describes Obra Dinn's core mechanic as filling a matrix of features per crew identity, the same structure as a logic grid — [Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/for-lucas-pope-i-return-of-the-obra-dinn-i-was-a-bunch-of-appealing-design-problems)

### Inferences
- For the target game, build a clue matrix. Rows are revelations (for example "the AGI is a future clone's mind," "the alien signal is the player's own loop"). Columns are clue sources (location, object, environmental scene, NPC behavior, audio log), tagged with the chapter or area. Requirement: three or more independent clues per required revelation, spread across at least two areas, with no single clue on a chokepoint.
- Build a knowledge-gate graph. Nodes are areas or events, and edges are "requires knowledge K." Run a reachability check: from every possible order of entry, can every required revelation be reached? inkle's autoplayer plus assertions is the professional version. Even a script that walks the graph with random orders catches dead ends.
- For time travel and clones, track two separate states: what the player knows and what each character knows (inkle's knowledge model). This prevents characters reacting to things they "can't" know in a given timeline.
- Gate the finale on multiple revelations, as in the Alexandrian temple example, not on one discoverable clue. This keeps order free without skipping.
- Avoid red herrings unless the game clearly refutes them later.

- No sourced example of a "knowledge-gate map" document from a AAA studio was found.
- Uchikoshi's actual flowchart documents (if any) were not found in fetched sources.

## 5. Very large multi-thread epics in prose and comics

### Takeaway
Long-running epics are kept consistent in one of three ways. Some use a grid that crosses chapters with subplots (Rowling). Some use a floating outline anchored by fixed "points on the map" plus parallel bullet tracks for mysteries and character arcs (Sanderson), backed by a dedicated continuity editor and internal wiki. Others carry the ending in the author's head and brief each new editor on it (Oda). Discovery writers with many POV threads (Martin) pay for it in repeated restructures.

### Cited Findings
- J.K. Rowling's handwritten grid for Order of the Phoenix (chapters 13 to 24 shown) has a column for each subplot: Prophecy, Cho/Ginny, D.A., Order of the Phoenix, Snape/Harry, and Hagrid and Grawp. Each row is a chapter, so you can see what every thread does in that chapter — [Open Culture](https://openculture.com/2014/07/j-k-rowling-plotted-harry-potter-with-a-hand-drawn-spreadsheet.html)
- Brandon Sanderson's points-on-the-map or floating outline works like this. Define characters (personality, conflicts, arcs) and setting (culture, magic, look) first. Split the book into about four parts based on its big climaxes. Place the key scenes already imagined under each part. For each key scene, list the setup it needs (introductions, hints, foreshadowing). Interleave bullets for character progression and for each mystery (its foreshadowing and small reveals in order). Fill the gaps while drafting. He needs the ending before starting ("I write for endings") — [Brandon Sanderson FAQ, "Can you go into depth about outlining?"](https://faq.brandonsanderson.com/knowledge-base/can-you-go-into-depth-about-outlining/)
- Dragonsteel employs an in-house continuity editor. Sanderson's outline runs to roughly a tenth of the finished book's word count — [Dragonsteel, "Continuity in the Cosmere"](https://www.dragonsteelbooks.com/blogs/the-cognitive-realm/continuity-in-the-cosmere)
- Karen Ahlstrom is listed as continuity editor and keeper of Brandon's internal wiki — [Coppermind, Team Sanderson](https://www.coppermind.net/wiki/Team_Sanderson)
- Malazan (Erikson and Esslemont) grew out of role-playing campaigns the two ran for each other. Whoever ran a region filled in its map. They agree on the general events first and invent details while writing. Esslemont often had to rework his storylines when Erikson, who published first, introduced new ideas — [Reactor, conversation with Erikson and Esslemont](https://reactormag.com/a-conversation-with-malazan-series-authors-steven-erikson-and-ian-c-esslemont)
- George R.R. Martin calls himself a "gardener" with no formal outline. In a 2010 blog post he mentioned moving an event in one of his "forty-seven restructures" of A Dance with Dragons. The article argues that discovery writing across dozens of POVs forces repeated re-threading (this is the article's analysis) — [IBTimes UK](https://www.ibtimes.co.uk/george-rr-martins-21-year-old-writing-confession-explains-winds-winter-delay-forty-seven-1801485)
- One Piece: editor Taku Sugita said Eiichiro Oda has mapped out the ending and final arc, and briefs each new editor on it in a phone call that once took seven hours. Oda wants editors to be near-equals but takes few plot suggestions — [SoraNews24](https://soranews24.com/2015/07/23/is-one-pieces-end-in-sight-editor-reveals-final-arc-is-mapped-out-speculates-on-timetable/)

### Inferences
- Rowling's grid is the simplest proven tool for interwoven threads. For the game, columns would be each character thread plus each mystery thread (AGI, aliens, clone line, time loop), rows would be chapters or areas, and cells would hold what advances, is revealed or is planted.
- Sanderson's parallel mystery bullets (foreshadow, foreshadow, small reveal, big reveal) are a practical format for each long-running mystery in the game.
- Oda's ending handoff is the human version of a sealed "solution document": every new lead gets the full ending on joining.
- Malazan shows that a second author or a second team (for example, a DLC team) must defer to whatever is published first. Lore should therefore be locked before content ships.

### Gaps
- I could not source the "Keeper" job title or the Arcanum (Sanderson's Words of Brandon archive) from fetched pages. The Coppermind page only names Karen Ahlstrom's role. No fetched source describes Dragonsteel's internal wiki structure or beta/gamma read process.
- I found no fetched source on Erikson's specific outline documents or how many books were planned up front.

## 6. Managing revision: locking the ending, fixing answers while the surface changes, lore version control

### Takeaway
Successful teams lock the answers (the ending, the solution, the central theme) early and let everything else move. They keep a written, versioned source of truth with a named keeper. They hold fallback "trap doors" for threads that may be cut. They run scheduled review gates (Braintrust screenings, milestone gate meetings). The "making it up as we go" failure comes from three things: no fixed answer to the central question (BSG), no end date (Lost), or answers living in one head or a vague idea that changes hands (Mass Effect).

### Cited Findings
- Lock the ending first: Druckmann works backward from the ending as the story's essence ([GamingBolt](https://gamingbolt.com/the-last-of-us-part-2-writer-and-director-discusses-his-creative-process)). Sanderson needs the ending before he starts ([Sanderson FAQ](https://faq.brandonsanderson.com/knowledge-base/can-you-go-into-depth-about-outlining/)). Oda has mapped his ending ([SoraNews24](https://soranews24.com/2015/07/23/is-one-pieces-end-in-sight-editor-reveals-final-arc-is-mapped-out-speculates-on-timetable/)). Dark fixed key endpoints (saving Regina, the dinner scene) while moving reveal timing ([THR](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/live-feed/dark-final-season-explained-baran-bo-odar-jantje-friese-interview-1302171)).
- Keep the answers fixed and move the reveal: Dark moved the parallel world from Season 2 to Season 3 without changing what it was ([THR](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/live-feed/dark-final-season-explained-baran-bo-odar-jantje-friese-interview-1302171)). Uchikoshi made Zero Time Dilemma answer earlier mysteries without contradicting earlier setups ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/no-escape-i-zero-time-dilemma-i-and-the-narrative-design-of-kotaro-uchikoshi)).
- Version-control the lore: the Halo bible had numbered versions, named keepers, locked physical access, section-by-section release to outside authors, a later migration to an internal wiki run by a Franchise Team, and a rule that unpublished material is non-canon until it ships ([Halopedia](https://www.halopedia.org/Halo_Story_Bible)). Dragonsteel has a continuity editor who keeps the internal wiki ([Coppermind](https://www.coppermind.net/wiki/Team_Sanderson)). Severance gives new writers a roughly 50-page bible and has one "answer man" ([Winter Is Coming](https://winteriscoming.net/posts/severance-writer-assures-us-that-all-the-mysteries-link-up-and-make-sense-exclusive-01j9c2q931pg)).
- Plan for cuts: Babylon 5's trap doors ([Reactor](https://reactormag.com/babylon-5-was-the-ultimate-exercise-in-plotting-vs-pantsing/)). In games, writers act as "narrative paramedics" when levels are cut and rewrite to keep the beat ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/uk/how-blockbuster-games-are-written)).
- Scheduled review gates: Pixar screens about every three months ([Screen Rant](https://screenrant.com/pixar-onward-storytelling-high-bar-story-artists-kelsey-mann/)). Eidos Montreal held milestone gate meetings ([Game Developer/DeMarle](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/gdc-online-the-design-document-that-made-i-deus-ex-human-revolution-i-possible)).
- Failure modes: BSG's premise promised a plan that did not exist ([/Film](https://www.slashfilm.com/846604/not-even-the-writers-knew-the-cylons-plan-in-battlestar-galactica)). Lost had no end date and its answers fell behind its questions ([Collider](https://collider.com/damon-lindelof-lost-original-ending-plan)). The Mass Effect ending concept was vague and the lead writer changed between games ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/mass-effect-3-series-former-lead-writer-reveals-original-ending-ideas)). Breaking Bad's flash-forwards created months-long corners ([TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/vince-gilligan-relishes-anxiety-painting-himself-corners-breaking-bad-110096/)).

### Inferences
- For the game, keep a sealed "Solution Document" (the true timeline, who/what/why for the AGI, aliens and clones, plus every causal loop). Lock it before vertical slice. Change it only through a formal change request owned by the narrative director, and require a re-audit of the clue matrix after any change.
- Separate canon tiers, as Halo did: Locked (ships, never contradicts), Working (in production), and Apocrypha (ideas, not binding).
- Each thread should have a trap door: a cheaper resolution prepared in case its level or character is cut.
- Time-travel mysteries need a single master chronology, in true causal order, alongside the player-facing order. Dark's color-per-decade rule shows how to carry the timeline into visual production.

### Gaps
- No fetched source gave an explicit studio policy on when to "lock" a story in game milestones (for example, story lock at alpha). This is common industry practice but unsourced here.
- I found no sourced account of game studios using git or Perforce specifically for lore documents.

## 7. Concrete deliverable checklist: what a professional epic story plan contains, in order

### Takeaway
Across TV, animation and games, the documents run roughly in this order: theme and concept, then the bible (world, characters, timeline, rules), then a sealed ending and solution, then the arc outline with thread grid, then chapter or episode paragraphs, then beat boards and a whole-game sequence spreadsheet, then chapter outlines with beat flows and intensity graphs, then clue and knowledge matrices with reachability tests, then boards, reels and graybox reviews, then scripts and dialogue in tools. Revision gates sit between stages and a lore keeper sits over the whole stack.

### Cited Findings (each item tied to a source practice)
1. **Theme and core concept / production proposal**: Uchikoshi starts with a theme ([Nintendo Life](https://www.nintendolife.com/features/zero-escape-creator-talks-ai-the-somnium-files-nirvana-initiative)). Eidos Montreal started with a high-level concept and a theme question ([Game Developer/DeMarle](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/gdc-online-the-design-document-that-made-i-deus-ex-human-revolution-i-possible)). Anime starts from a production proposal ([Sakugabooru](https://blog.sakugabooru.com/2017/07/05/the-pre-production-of-anime-2-scripting/)).
2. **Main twist or ending (sealed)**: Uchikoshi works out the main gimmick or twist before characters ([Nintendo Life](https://www.nintendolife.com/features/zero-escape-creator-talks-ai-the-somnium-files-nirvana-initiative)). Druckmann and Sanderson start from the ending ([GamingBolt](https://gamingbolt.com/the-last-of-us-part-2-writer-and-director-discusses-his-creative-process); [Sanderson FAQ](https://faq.brandonsanderson.com/knowledge-base/can-you-go-into-depth-about-outlining/)).
3. **Treatment / series bible / story bible** (world state, factions, character dossiers, historical timeline): Eidos dossiers and timeline ([Game Developer/DeMarle](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/gdc-online-the-design-document-that-made-i-deus-ex-human-revolution-i-possible)). Severance's roughly 50-page bible ([Winter Is Coming](https://winteriscoming.net/posts/severance-writer-assures-us-that-all-the-mysteries-link-up-and-make-sense-exclusive-01j9c2q931pg)). Halo Story Bible ([Halopedia](https://www.halopedia.org/Halo_Story_Bible)). Barlow's character life documents ([GamesRadar](https://www.gamesradar.com/making-her-story)).
4. **Arc synopsis with trap doors**: Babylon 5's seven-page synopsis plus trap doors ([Reactor](https://reactormag.com/babylon-5-was-the-ultimate-exercise-in-plotting-vs-pantsing/)). Season arc start and end ([No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/how-to-break-a-tv-season)).
5. **Character arc maps**: per-character backstory, flaws and arc ([Gizmodo/Engler](https://gizmodo.com/so-you-want-to-make-a-genre-tv-show-part-2-inside-the-writers-room-1635438731)); Sanderson's character progression bullets ([Sanderson FAQ](https://faq.brandonsanderson.com/knowledge-base/can-you-go-into-depth-about-outlining/)).
6. **Thread grid** (chapters × subplots): Rowling ([Open Culture](https://openculture.com/2014/07/j-k-rowling-plotted-harry-potter-with-a-hand-drawn-spreadsheet.html)).
7. **Episode/chapter paragraphs, then corkboard of cards**: Z Nation's paragraph-form episodes ([Gizmodo/Engler](https://gizmodo.com/so-you-want-to-make-a-genre-tv-show-part-2-inside-the-writers-room-1635438731)); The Last of Us corkboard ([GameSpot](https://www.gamespot.com/articles/see-how-the-last-of-us-was-originally-envisioned-u/1100-6421393/)); Pixar carding ([/Film](https://slashfilm.com/making-of-onward-cracking-the-story)); Breaking Bad beat cards ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2013/10/03/228813142/breaking-bad-writers-this-is-it-theres-no-more)).
8. **Whole-game sequence spreadsheet** (story beats × gameplay × choices per sequence): the Deus Ex Blueprint ([Game Developer/DeMarle](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/gdc-online-the-design-document-that-made-i-deus-ex-human-revolution-i-possible)).
9. **Chapter outlines / mission docs** with beat flow, intensity graph, color script, layout prototype: ([Game Developer, Chapter Outline](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/creating-a-narrative-focused-mission-design-document-a-last-of-us-example)).
10. **Clue matrix, revelation list and node map** (three or more clues per conclusion, three or more in and out per node): ([The Alexandrian](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/1118/roleplaying-games/three-clue-rule); [Node design Pt. 7](https://thealexandrian.net/?p=8032)).
11. **Knowledge state model and reachability tests** (autoplayer plus assertions; word/answer findability analysis): ([Game Developer, Overboard](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/how-get-away-murder-overboard); [GamesRadar, Her Story](https://www.gamesradar.com/making-her-story)).
12. **Storyboards → story reels / animatics with scratch VO → periodic braintrust screenings**: ([/Film](https://slashfilm.com/making-of-onward-cracking-the-story); [Screen Rant](https://screenrant.com/pixar-onward-storytelling-high-bar-story-artists-kelsey-mann/); [Fandom](https://www.fandom.com/articles/pixar-collaboration-teams)).
13. **Scripts and dialogue in a narrative tool** (articy:draft flows and variables; ink) exported to the engine: ([Game Developer, articy](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/effective-game-design-how-we-use-articy-draft-to-organize-game-content); [Overboard](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/how-get-away-murder-overboard)).
14. **Ongoing**: lore keeper and internal wiki with versions and canon tiers ([Halopedia](https://www.halopedia.org/Halo_Story_Bible); [Coppermind](https://www.coppermind.net/wiki/Team_Sanderson)); milestone gate reviews ([Game Developer/DeMarle](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/gdc-online-the-design-document-that-made-i-deus-ex-human-revolution-i-possible)); tone briefing recording for the whole team ([GamingBolt](https://gamingbolt.com/the-last-of-us-part-2-writer-and-director-discusses-his-creative-process)).

### Inferences
Suggested build order for the target game (a synthesis, not a single studio's process):
1. One-page theme and pitch, with the central question the mystery explores.
2. Sealed Solution Document: the true causal timeline (including time loops and clone lineages), what the AGI and aliens actually are and want, and the final reveal. Locked early.
3. Lore bible as an internal wiki: world rules (time-travel rules, cloning rules, AGI capabilities), factions, character dossiers, a master chronology, and canon tiers.
4. Character thread sheets: each character's want, secret, arc, and trap-door ending.
5. Thread grid: chapters/areas × threads (Rowling style).
6. Revelation list and clue matrix: three or more clues per required revelation, across areas and media (environment, audio, NPC behavior, mechanics). This suits a low-text game.
7. Knowledge-gate graph plus reachability script; player-knowledge vs. character-knowledge model.
8. Whole-game Blueprint spreadsheet: sequence × gameplay × story beat × clue IDs × choices.
9. Per-area Chapter Outlines with beat flows, intensity graphs and graybox layouts.
10. Storyboards and animatics for key reveals; graybox "story reel" playthroughs screened every 8 to 12 weeks to an outside braintrust, with blind playtests checking that players reach each revelation without the designer's knowledge.
11. Scripts and VO in articy or ink; continuity pass by the lore keeper; change-request process for anything touching the Solution Document.

### Gaps
- No single fetched source lays out this exact end-to-end document list. The sequence above is a synthesis of the cited practices.
