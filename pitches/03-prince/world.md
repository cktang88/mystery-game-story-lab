# Pitch 3: The world beneath The Prince

The story on top stays the same: an alien prince is murdered on a human space station, and a human cop and the prince's alien bodyguard have ten days to find the killer before the two empires go to war. This file is the iceberg under it. The main story never needs any of it to make sense.

The two sci-fi rules stay as the story states them:
- **Time:** The Aun can send a short letter back in time, up to ten days, but each letter burns up a crystal they can't replace.
- **Cloning:** Humans can grow an adult double of a person in about a month, with the body but none of the memories.

The world adds one detail to the time rule, which the story already follows: a crystal speaks only to its own past. You speak a few words into it, and the same crystal holds those words up to ten days earlier. So a letter always reaches whoever held that crystal then. In practice, that means the Empress writes to herself.

## 1. The one central thing: the crystals

Everything in this world hangs on a few hundred grey crystals, and on the fact that no more will ever be made.

They grew in the outer layers of one dying star near the Aun home. It went dark 1,200 years ago. Since then every letter has come from a single shrinking store, the Vault, which travels with whoever wears the crown.

A crystal is spent for one reason only: something went wrong. Nobody sends good news back. So each empty holder in the Vault is a disaster that someone undid. The Aun believe they have never lost a war. In truth they have lost hundreds of battles and erased each one.

Follow the crystals and the whole world falls out:
- **Politics.** Only the crown holds crystals, so only the crown sees ahead. Aun law grew up as a leash on that power.
- **Money.** The crown quietly rented letters to the banks, which saw markets ten days early. That is where the human debt came from.
- **War.** The Aun won the war because their losses kept vanishing. Humans fought back with two inventions: doubles, and an AI that could hear letters land.
- **Custom.** The Aun treat a spoken promise as law, because the one thing foresight can't fake is a word you gave before you knew the future.
- **Fear.** The Vault is nearly empty. The Aun ruling family knows its power has an end date. Some humans have guessed. That shared secret drives the peace, the murder and the deadline.

The crystals are to this world what spice is to Dune's, with one difference that makes them colder: they don't regrow. Every use brings the end closer.

## 2. The deep timeline

### Era one: the slow people (before 4,000 years ago)

The Aun come from a cold, dim world with low gravity and thick air. That world explains their bodies. Low gravity let them grow tall. Cold made their bodies slow and their lives long, about three hundred years. Thick air carries sound well and the dim sun gives little light, so they evolved huge hearing and lines of light under the skin to signal each other in the dark. An Aun's lines brighten and shift with feeling, which makes Aun bad liars at close range.

Long lives made them builders who plan in centuries. Their noble houses lasted thousands of years.

### Era two: the slow ships (4,000 to 3,000 years ago)

The Aun never found a fast drive. They flew slow ships between nearby stars, voyages of thirty or forty years. For a people who live three hundred years, that is a long posting, not a lifetime. Over a thousand years they settled about a dozen worlds. Each was run by a house.

### Era three: the Harvest (3,000 to 1,900 years ago)

A house's slow ship skimming a dying star found the crystals. Within a century every house was racing to harvest them.

This era taught the Aun the first cold lesson of foresight. When only one side has letters, it wins. When both sides have them, each side keeps undoing the other's wins, and both burn crystals at a terrible rate. The Harvest Wars lasted eleven centuries and spent most of what the star ever made. Each house was certain it was winning, because each had undone so many losses.

### Era four: the First Table (1,900 years ago)

The houses met at one great table to share the harvest and end the wars. A house leader named First Spring read a letter from ten days ahead. Whatever it said, she struck first. She ambushed the others at the table and took every crystal they had brought.

The official history says the other houses planned to betray her, and that she saved the peace. That is half true. In the future her letter came from, perhaps they did betray her. But that future never happened. She punished the other houses for something they never did.

The surviving houses could not beat a ruler who saw ahead. So they made a deal with her, and it still shapes Aun life:
- The crown alone keeps the crystals.
- The crown's spoken promise is binding. A ruler who breaks one can be removed by the Court.
- The crown may never punish anyone for what a letter says they will do. Only for what they have done.
- Before any treaty, the two leaders share a drink from one cup. If one is poisoned, both are. It is the oldest Aun safeguard against a betrayal at the table.

This is the true root of Aun "honour". It isn't a virtue they were born with. It's the leash the houses put on the crown after the crown cheated. Over 1,900 years the leash became a culture.

### Era five: the Count (1,200 years ago to 60 years ago)

The star collapsed and went dark. From that day the crown could count its future in crystals. Each Empress since has inherited a smaller Vault.

The crown spent carefully. Letters undid shipwrecks, a failed dam, a quake on a crowded world. These became the Readings: rare public moments when the crown showed a spent crystal and told the people what it had saved them from. The Readings made the crown loved. They also taught the Aun to believe that disaster was optional.

Aun banks grew out of the old houses in this era. Long lives made them patient lenders. They lent for a hundred years at a time and expected to be alive to collect.

### Era six: the humans (700 to 60 years ago)

Humans left Earth with a fast drive about 700 years ago. It jumps a ship between stars in days, if it can refuel at a gas giant on the way. Because of that limit, human space grew along chains of gas giants.

Humans spread fast, on borrowed money. The result was the Hundred Worlds: planets held together by trade and shared debt, with a Council and an elected Chancellor but no real centre.

About seventy years ago a crash on Earth froze its lending. The Hundred Worlds were starving for credit when, ten years later, a survey ship jumped into a system with a gas giant and found Aun slow ships already there.

### Era seven: the Loans (60 to 25 years ago)

First contact was peaceful and very profitable. Humans had the fast drive. The Aun had patient money. The deal wrote itself: Aun banks lent to the Hundred Worlds, and humans sold the Aun the drive.

Selling the drive was humanity's biggest mistake. The Aun went from slow to fast in one generation, and a power with foresight now had reach.

Meanwhile, human traders kept losing to Aun banks. Not every time, which would have looked suspicious. Just often enough. The truth, buried in a ledger, is that the crown rented letters to the banks. Empress Long Winter, young on the throne and short of money, approved it. Each rented letter carried a few market prices back ten days. A single one could earn more than a province. The debt that humans pay today was built on those trades.

It cost the Vault dearly. Thirty-five years of renting burned more crystals than the four centuries before it.

### Era eight: the war (25 to 20 years ago)

A Chancellor on a debt-crushed border world won power by promising to stop paying. The Hundred Worlds defaulted. The Aun came to collect.

The Aun won almost every battle, because the Empress led the war from her throne-ship with the Vault aboard. When a battle went badly, she spoke ten words into a crystal, and her past self moved the fleet. Their assassins found human leaders the same way.

Humans answered with two inventions.

**Doubles.** Growing a grown body in a month already existed as a costly medical service. The war turned it into armour. A letter can tell you where the Chancellor will be. It can't tell you which Chancellor. Within five years every human leader had one or two doubles. A generation later it seems as normal as a seatbelt.

**Lookout.** The Fleet's war AI was built to predict the Aun, and it kept failing. So it asked a different question: how do they know? Over three years it learned to hear a faint ring that a crystal gives off when a letter lands. Each ring meant "the Aun just lost something here, in a future that no longer exists." Lookout began to change the Fleet's plans at random after every ring, so the future the Empress had read no longer came true. Human crews started flipping coins before each jump to copy it.

The Aun began to bleed crystals. Battles they had already undone had to be undone again.

### Era nine: the truce (20 years ago)

The last battle was fought around a gas giant on the short route between the two peoples' space. The human flagship, Lookout's home, was crippled and left drifting in orbit around one of the giant's moons. The Empress came aboard the wreck in person to offer a truce.

Her terms were simple. The war ends. Humans keep paying, with war costs added. And Lookout must be rebuilt as a neutral keeper that may never serve a fleet again.

The public reason was that Lookout was a weapon. The real reason was that Lookout could count. Every ring it heard was a spent crystal. From its war logs, anyone could estimate how fast the Vault was emptying. The Empress needed that count to stay secret more than she needed to win.

The humans accepted, because they were still losing. The wreck became the core of a station built for trade and talks. Lookout, rebuilt, became the Clerk. The station became Halfway.

### Era ten: the shaky peace (20 years ago to now)

All trade between humans and Aun passes through Halfway, and so do the debt payments. The Debt Clock was hung over the market as a promise of honesty: everyone can see what is owed. Most humans see it as a scoreboard of shame.

Prince Promise grew up visiting Halfway as his mother's envoy, and fell for human noise, food and jokes. Eight years ago, after a scare at court, he quietly bought a human double. Two weeks before this story begins, he brought a peace that would forgive half the debt. He knew what his mother and sister knew: the Vault is nearly empty, and an empire that has never lost is about to become one that can.

## 3. The Aun: their civilisation today

The Aun Empire is a dozen worlds and about four billion people. The Hundred Worlds hold over forty billion. That ratio sits under every Aun decision. Without foresight, numbers win.

**The Crown.** The ruling family descends from First Spring. The Empress holds the Vault, commands the fleet and keeps her word in public. Only the ruler and one keeper know how many crystals are left. Today that keeper is Princess Keen.

**The Court.** The heirs of the old houses. They hold the leash: they can remove a ruler who breaks a spoken promise, and they keep the law. Many of them own the human debt through their banks. Forgiving half of it costs them directly, so most of the Court's anger at the peace is money, dressed in old law.

**The copy law.** Twenty-two years ago, during the war, the Court passed a new law at Long Winter's request: any royal who is copied can never hold the Vault. The reason is practical. The Vault is passed hand to hand, and a copy of the heir could stand in for the heir and take it. Promise broke his mother's own law.

**The people.** Most Aun have never seen a crystal. They know the Readings, the unbroken record of victory and the saying "You can't surprise the Aun." They believe the crown will always undo the worst. Losing that belief would shake them more than losing a war.

**What the Aun never built.** The Aun have no thinking machines. A people who can read the future had no need to build machines that guess it. They find the Clerk strange and slightly impressive, the way a sailor might view a compass. They respect one thing about it above all: it keeps its promises.

## 4. The present-day sides and how each came to be

**The Hundred Worlds government.** A Council of world delegates elects a Chancellor for eight years. It grew out of the old trade-and-credit union and still works like one: everyone argues, nobody leads for long. The Chancellor's office runs two things that matter in this story. It runs the Clinic, which grows and trains every leader's double. And it holds the human half of the Clerk's oversight, including the legal power to suspend it "pending review."

Chancellor Maren Hale has read a captured ledger of the crown's rented letters. It lists dates and Vault counts. Put next to the Fleet's old war logs, it lets her estimate what's left: a handful. Her logic is cold. A war now, short and close to home, would make the Empress burn her last crystals. After that, the Aun can never undo a loss again, and the debt becomes a number nobody can enforce. Signing the peace now would lock humans into paying half the debt forever, just as the Aun lose the edge that made them collect.

**The Fleet.** The Hundred Worlds' navy answers to the Council in law and to its admirals in practice. It still flips coins before jumps. Admiral Halvard Strand plays the hawk on the news because the Fleet's budget depends on fear. In private he lost half his ships in the war and never wants to fight again. His real secret is that the Fleet kept Lookout's war logs, which the truce ordered destroyed. They sit in his safe. If found, they make him look guilty of everything.

**The border worlds.** The poorest human planets sit nearest Aun space. They pay the most debt per head and took the worst of the war. Their protest slogan is "Stop the Clock." They are Maren's loudest voters and the reason she wins.

**The Clinic.** It began as a private medical firm that grew replacement bodies for the very rich. The war made it a state service. It grows a double in a month and spends a year teaching it to walk, talk and stand like its original. Doubles have numbers, not names, and serve until their original leaves office. Then they get a pension and a name. Many pick their own name early and keep it secret, as Ines did. A small doubles' rights movement wants them named from the start. The Clinic knows who is real at every human event, which makes it the most dangerous office in human space.

**The Aun Crown and Court.** See section 3. The Empress wants her son's killer and her empire's survival, in that order this week. Keen wants the throne of an empire that still exists. The Court wants its money.

**Halfway and the Clerk.** Halfway has two halves and two police forces. Human station police (Sam's night shift) patrol the markets and docks. Aun household guards keep the garden half. The Clerk runs air, power, docking and records for both. It obeys "lawful human authority on human matters, and lawful Aun authority on Aun matters." By treaty it has no sensors inside Aun rooms.

The Clerk still has Lookout's ears. It hears every letter that lands near Halfway. It may tell no one. On the morning of the murder it heard one land on the Empress's ship.

## 5. Technology, how it evolved, and what it costs

**The crystals.** You strike one so it rings, and speak a few words while it rings. The same crystal then holds those words from the moment you choose, up to ten days before. The world that sent the letter is gone. Only the words remain, and the crystal goes dark. Nobody, Aun or human, knows why they work. Humans have spent decades surveying dying stars in their own space, hoping to find another source. None has turned up yet.

*Cost:* each one is gone forever. Its holder is a record of a disaster nobody remembers.

**The fast drive.** A human invention. A ship jumps between stars in days, but it must refuel with hydrogen from a gas giant along the way. That makes gas giants the crossroads of human space, and it's why Halfway sits around one. The Aun bought the drive sixty years ago.

**Doubles.** A body grown in a sleek tank in about a month, clean and quiet, then a year of training. A double has its original's face and build, but not their memories, habits or skills. It must learn to hold a cup, which hand to sign with and how to laugh.

*Cost:* money, a year of someone's time, and a person who never chose this life. The tells that training never fully erases, like which hand someone favours or the songs they hum, are how Sam catches them.

**The Clerk's rebuilds.** As Lookout, the AI's skill was building models of where enemy ships would be. As the Clerk, it builds the same kind of model backward, from every camera, microphone and air sensor on the human half. Sam walks inside its guesses about the past. Where it has no sensors, it leaves white space and does not guess.

*Cost:* privacy. Humans accept it because the Clerk never shares more than the law forces.

## 6. Daily life and culture

**On Halfway's human half,** people eat fried noodles, tank-grown fish, hydroponic greens and very strong coffee. The market never closes. Kids flip "Halfway coins" for everything; adults say "flip on it" when they can't decide. Nobody remembers it began as a war trick to beat the Aun's foresight.

Humans fear being read: having your next move known. A common insult is "predictable."

Humans celebrate Truce Day with light shows over the market. Clock Day, when the year's debt payment goes through, brings protests and "pay parties" where border-world workers drink to the number they'll never see shrink.

**On the Aun half,** the gardens are dim, quiet and full of plants from the cold home world under glass. The Aun eat one long meal a day, slowly, with tea steeped for hours. Human wine is a fashionable Aun luxury. Promise loved it, which is why a gift of wine on the treaty eve looked natural.

The Aun say "Speak as if you'll be read back." It means: talk as if your words might return to you ten days early. Children learn that the crown can undo disasters, so most Aun are calm in ways humans find unsettling. They are not ready for a loss that stays lost.

The Aun celebrate the Readings, when the crown reveals a disaster it undid. They have had none for twenty years. Few Aun have asked why.

What the Aun fear most is a broken word. Second, though the crown alone knows it, is the empty Vault.

**Between them,** the Truce Cup custom is shared by both peoples now. Humans think it's a quaint Aun toast. The Aun know it was born at the First Table as a poison check: if one dies, both do. A poison that harms only the Aun breaks a 1,900-year-old safeguard without anyone noticing. Nobody wrote the custom for two species.

## 7. Places that carry history

**The Debt Clock.** A giant number over the market, raised under the truce as proof of honesty. Its old jumps, matched against the dates in the ledger, show that every big human loss came the day after a crown letter. It is evidence hiding in plain sight above a thousand food stalls.

**The Old Hull.** The crippled human flagship at the centre of Halfway, sealed. The truce was signed in its broken command deck. The Clerk's mind still runs in its core. The war's last moments are still in its memory, including the Empress's face when she came aboard.

**The Glass Garden.** The prince's garden on the Aun half, the murder scene. To the Clerk it's a blank. Its plants come from the Aun home world, so it is also the only place on Halfway that smells like home to Patience.

**The Clinic.** Clean white floors, quiet tanks and a training wing with mirrors and recordings of the leaders the doubles must copy. Ines and the prince's double learned to stand straight here, side by side.

**The Vault.** A room on the throne-ship lined with crystal holders: hundreds empty, a handful full. Each empty holder is labelled with what its letter undid. Together, those labels are the Aun's true history, a list of defeats nobody remembers.

**The First Table.** A long stone table in the Vault room. It carries First Spring's mark and no record of what her letter said. The first Truce Cup sits on it.

## 8. Buried truths and how the player finds them

The player can finish the game knowing none of these. Each one comes from doing, not reading.

1. **The deadline is the letter window.** Ten days from the morning the body was found is the last moment a letter can still reach the morning of the murder. The Empress picked "ten days" for that reason, both times. *How:* Sam lines up the Clerk's landing time against the fleet deadline and sees they're exactly ten days apart.

2. **The Aun have lost hundreds of times.** *How:* the Vault, its empty holders and their labels, late in the game.

3. **The debt was built on rented letters.** *How:* a side puzzle. Sam overlays the ledger dates on the Debt Clock's archived history and the Clerk's old landing log. The jumps line up.

4. **The truce's real price was silence about the count.** *How:* a rebuild of the truce signing, from the Old Hull's own memory, where the Empress's spoken terms differ from the published text.

5. **First Spring punished a betrayal that never happened.** *How:* an Aun-half archive that Patience can open, where an old house's account of the table survives in a song Patience hears in a garden. Keen shows Sam the table.

6. **The Fleet kept Lookout's logs.** *How:* tailing Strand on day 3 leads to his safe. It looks like evidence of murder. It's evidence of a different crime, and the source of Maren's count.

7. **The copy law was the Empress's own.** *How:* a Court broadcast quoting the law's author. It turns the Empress's last choice into a judgment on her own rule.

8. **No new crystals have been found, yet.** *How:* a survey report in Strand's files flags a dying star in human space. It's a door for a sequel.

## 9. Suggested changes to the story

For the owner to approve. The story already matches most of the world. These are the gaps and the chances.

1. **Make the ten-day deadline the letter window.** The Empress says "Ten days" because that is the last day a letter can still reach the morning of the murder. If Sam fails, she can try again, as she did last time. This turns the countdown itself into a time-travel clue, makes the fail state follow from the rule, and gives the day-8 reveal a second click: the deadline was never about the fleet.

2. **State the one detail of the rule: a crystal speaks only to its own past.** The story already works this way. Saying it once ("a letter only reaches whoever held that crystal") explains why only the crown sees ahead, why the Empress wrote to herself and then called her son, and why the Vault travels with her.

3. **Give the Truce Cup its origin.** It was born at the First Table as a poison check: if one leader is poisoned, both are. The Aun-only poison defeats a 1,900-year-old safeguard. One line from Patience or Keen on day 4, when the poison report arrives, makes the murder weapon feel inevitable.

4. **Sharpen Maren's motive into "make them spend the last crystals."** She doesn't only want to avoid the peace. She wants a short war now, because a war forces the Empress to burn her last letters, after which the Aun can't enforce the debt. Her real target is the Vault. It makes her the smartest person in the story and ties her to the central idea.

5. **Give the Aun light-lines a job in play.** The lines brighten with feeling. At the body, the other guards' lines flare; Patience's stay steady. The player can see "far too calm" instead of being told, and later read Aun suspects the way Sam reads human hands.

6. **Make the copy law the Empress's own.** Twenty-two years ago she asked for it, to protect the Vault from impostors. Her son broke her law to obey her warning. It deepens ending A: she must enforce her own law against the son her letter saved.

7. **Give Strand a real secret: the Fleet kept Lookout's war logs.** It makes him a strong suspect (a hidden safe, a broken treaty) and explains where Maren got the data for her count. It answers the risk the story already lists, that Strand needs a real secret.

8. **Clarify the ring.** Suggest the double wore Promise's real ring, so the guards saw nothing missing. The body has the ring but no mark beneath it. Promise, in hiding, has a bare finger with a pale band, a small visual payoff on day 5.

9. **Promise in a dock worker's coat.** Aun dock crews work Halfway's Aun-half docks, and humans find Aun faces hard to tell apart. A tall Aun in a work coat is common there. One shot of Aun dockers early on makes his hiding place fair.

10. **Optional: Keen and the Court.** Since Keen secretly needs peace, the Court's bankers should be the real Aun war voice. They own the debt and don't know the count. That keeps an Aun side that truly wants war, so the ending's choice still has weight.

### Is the time travel new enough? (owner's new rule)

Honest verdict: the mechanic on its own is a familiar trope. A message sent a few days into the past is the core of *Steins;Gate*, and an empire run on rare foresight sits close to Dune's spice and navigators. Limited charges are a common game device.

What is new is the angle. The story never shows time travel from the inside. Nobody relives a loop on screen, and nobody explains paradoxes. We see it from outside, as a finite national resource with a ledger, and the new ideas all come from that view:
- No one sends good news back, so an empire that has never lost has really lost hundreds of times.
- The enemy can hear each letter land, so every ring is a confession of a defeat, and counting rings audits the enemy's remaining power.
- A letter tells you where a leader will be, not which leader. Cloning beats time travel.
- The deadline is the letter window. The countdown is itself a time machine's range.
- The murder works because of the warning. The future's own fix made the clue.

Readers can't predict these, because they come from economics and counting, not from the usual loop and paradox beats. Our recommendation is to keep time travel, and to keep it strictly offstage: no loops shown, no paradox talk, one rule.

Still, here are the two best alternatives from a brainstorm of seven (all seven are in `world-challenges.md`, round 5). Each keeps the same jobs: an empire that seems unbeatable, an Empress who knows what she shouldn't, and a reason a hidden prince had a double.

**Alternative A: one-use instant messages.** The crystals come in pairs. Speak into one and its twin repeats the words at once, anywhere in the galaxy, and then both go dark. Every other message in this world travels at the speed of a ship, days or weeks. So the Aun always know first: who moved, what prices did, where a fleet is. Their spies send one sentence home the moment it matters. The Empress knows of the plot because a spy on Halfway spent a pair that morning. The prince had a double because he trusted human customs.
*Gains:* no time travel at all, so no paradox questions. Information speed is a cold, real economic idea, close to how markets work. The crown's pairs held by spies give a fine spy-thriller layer (who on Halfway holds the other half?).
*Losses:* "they've never lost because they've lost hundreds" goes, and so do "these ten days have happened before" and the Empress grieving a son she saved. The instant message is also an old idea (Le Guin's ansible). The best twist in the story disappears.

**Alternative B: crystals that remember sound.** Every Aun crystal records whatever is said near it, forever, and the crown can play any crystal back once it holds it. For sixty years human ships, stations and phones have run on cheap Aun crystal parts, sold with the loans. The Aun never lost because they heard every plan. The Empress knows what she shouldn't because she has heard it. Humans fight back with doubles (a voice tells you what was said, not who said it) and an AI that learned to tell which parts were listening.
*Gains:* new and grounded. It turns the debt itself into the weapon: humanity borrowed its own wiretap. It fits Aun hearing and Patience's ears. The garden blank gets a sharp twist: the Clerk can't see in, but the garden's crystals heard everything, and the Empress has them.
*Losses:* the time pillar is gone, and the brief's four pillars become three. Surveillance stories are common, and "they heard the murder" can kill the mystery unless the garden crystals are destroyed or missing, which risks feeling contrived. The vault-of-defeats reveal goes.

**Our pick:** keep the ten-day letters, shown only from outside. If the owner wants time travel out, Alternative B is the stronger replacement, because it ties the central resource to the debt and adds a twist the time version lacks.
