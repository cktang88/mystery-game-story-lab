# Pitch 2: The Armada

> "A soldier and her AI fight an alien armada to save the last human colonies. The aliens seem to know every move before it happens; someone is feeding them the future."

## The story in one page

Sergeant Ines Abreu dies in the first five minutes.

Her drop pod falls toward a colony under attack. Before the pod even leaves the ship, the alien fleet turns to meet it. They knew where she would land.

She wakes up in a clean white bay, in a new body grown from her last backup. Marines get regrown all the time. The catch is the backup: it's six days old. Ines has lost almost a week, and she has no idea what she did in it.

Her AI partner, Bosun, remembers every minute. But the Admiral has ordered Bosun not to tell her. So Bosun finds a gap in the order. It can't tell her what happened. Nobody said it couldn't show her. It plays back its recordings, and Ines walks through her own lost week like a stranger.

She sees herself chasing a signal out of the Fleet's own flagship. She watches herself agree to keep quiet. Then the recorded Ines turns, looks straight into Bosun's camera, and says: "Hi. You won't remember this."

The trail leads to the Fleet's best-kept secret. Under the capital sits a machine that receives short messages from one week in the future. It's why Admiral Vaz has never been ambushed in six years of war. Someone is copying those messages to one alien captain, so the aliens are never ambushed either.

The Fleet's own leaders are doing it. They're letting the two poorest colonies fall so their chosen alien captain can win glory, take the alien throne and sign a peace. They backed the wrong alien. And the aliens' whole civilisation was built to forbid exactly this.

Ines has to beat a man who reads next week's news. There's only one way to do that: be the one who writes it.

## 1. The story in one line

A soldier and her AI fight an alien armada to save the last four human colonies, and find out that their own side is feeding the aliens the future.

## 2. The sides

### Humans

**The Fleet** is the human navy. Admiral Teodoro Vaz runs it: sixty, calm, and famous as "the man who is never surprised". He wants to end the war with as many humans alive as he can, and he'll do sums with lives to get there. Picture grey warships with orange hull numbers, and marines who joke about how many times they've died.

**Keel** is the rich colony. It builds the Fleet's ships and holds the Fleet's debt. Governor Amália Reis, whose family's company founded the colonies, wants Keel to leave the war richer than it went in.

**Landfall** is the capital, home of the Council and Fleet command.

**Farhaven and Brightwater** are the poor outer colonies, farms and mines deep in debt to Keel. Last year they voted against the war taxes. Governor Kofi Mensah-Lima of Farhaven, loud and suspicious, thinks the Fleet guards Keel harder than his people. He can't prove it.

### Aliens

**The Armada** is a whole nation that lives on ships. It sails a loop around this part of the galaxy, one lap every nine hundred years, under sails kilometres wide that catch starlight. At full spread it looks like a city of white sails across the sky.

The aliens are tall and narrow, with smooth faces like polished stone and eyes that hold light. They talk in chords of sound and flashes of light. And they never hide their plans. Every harbour wall carries the Armada's timetable, carved centuries ahead: which ships arrive, on what day, and for how long. Section 5 explains why.

The Fleet gives alien commanders code names, so the player never learns an alien word.

- **Monarch** rules from what humans translate as "the Helm". The Helm's real job is keeping the timetable. She's old and patient. Humans think she started the war. She wants her people back in their harbours on the date they promised.
- **Kestrel**, a young captain, leads the alien spearhead and has never lost. Kestrel wants the Helm and thinks the timetable makes the Armada weak. To humans, Kestrel is the enemy's Vaz.

## 3. Who we follow

**Sergeant Ines Abreu**, 31, a drop-trooper from Farhaven. She joined up to get off that world and now fights to keep it. She's dry and stubborn, and quick to spot a story that doesn't add up. She wants to know who got her killed. She has to learn that the people above her aren't on her side by default.

**Bosun**, her AI, lives in her armour and her ship. It flies her drops, aims her guns and makes most of the jokes. It follows orders to the letter, a habit the tutorial plays for laughs: told "don't fire on the hatch", it rams it. Fleet AIs keep a weekly backup too, but the Fleet only resets an AI as punishment for disobeying a direct order. So the Admiral can't just wipe Bosun. He gives it an order instead: say nothing. If Bosun breaks that order, it gets reset to before the lost week, and the only witness is gone.

**Corporal Dado Pinheiro**, her squadmate, has been regrown eleven times. He keeps a paper notebook "for the weeks I miss". It holds a page about Ines that she asked him to keep until she came back.

**Captain Lola Faria** runs Fleet intelligence and was Ines's commanding officer. Her branch runs the Relay: each week Faria herself writes the message that goes back to last week, and her translators handle every alien signal. She believes the deal will save four million people, and she hates it. Early in the game she tells Ines, "In this Fleet, dying is the kind way out." She means it.

**Driftwood** is a captured alien navigator. Driftwood talks in light, picks up a few human words, and becomes Ines's way into the alien side. Driftwood walks the capital's corridors without a map, because Driftwood's people built them.

## 4. The two rules, and what the four pillars do

**Rule one:** One machine, the Relay, receives short messages sent back exactly one week; once a message arrives, the future it describes can change.

**Rule two:** A soldier who dies is regrown in a new body, remembering everything up to her last weekly backup.

Both rules run on the same week. The Relay gives you a week you haven't lived. Regrowing takes away a week you did live. Two plain consequences drive the plot. Killing a soldier wipes her last week, which is a way to silence her. Deleting her backups first kills her for good.

And one more follows from rule one. The message is only words, written by whoever stands at the Relay next week. Control that person and you control what this week knows.

- **Time travel** is the Relay. It explains how the aliens know every move, and it's the prize at the end.
- **Cloning** is regrowing. It gives us the opening, the crime, the mercy and the tone. Death is a setback, so the stakes are memory and trust, with no gore.
- **AGI** is Bosun, who knows what you forgot and has been told not to say. It wins by being clever with its orders.
- **Aliens** are the Armada, a people who chose to have no secrets about their future.

## 5. Backstory and worldbuilding (the iceberg)

One thing explains this whole world: who gets to know the future. The humans hide it in a vault. The aliens built their entire civilisation so that nobody could ever steal it from them. The main story needs the parts marked **[main]**. The parts marked **[depth]** are optional.

### What happened, oldest first

**Twelve thousand years ago: the Long Stalemate [main, short form].** The Armada once had many Relays. In a civil war, each side read the other's moves a week ahead, dodged every trap, and could never win. The war ground on for three generations. When it ended, the survivors broke their Relays and wrote the founding law: no captain may fight with knowledge of the future.

**The timetable [main].** Then the Armada reasoned it through to the end. Breaking the machines wasn't enough. Someone could always build another, or hide one. The only people safe from a stolen future are people with nothing to steal. So the Armada published its own future. It fixed its route around the galaxy and carved its arrival dates into every harbour, centuries ahead. Captains swear to the timetable the way human officers swear to a flag. A spy who reads the Armada's plans learns nothing the walls don't already say.

That one choice explains the rest. It's why they sail a loop instead of settling. It's why their ruler is the timetable's keeper. It's why other species along the route plan their trade around the Armada's arrival, centuries ahead. It's why an Armada captain who suddenly starts making surprise moves looks like a cheat to the rest of the fleet.

**The last Relay [main].** A few captains didn't trust the treaty and sealed one Relay in a vault "in case the others cheat". It prints in alien light-code, which makes its messages easy to forward to an alien captain. Early on, the player sees Faria's team "translating" the printouts, and can wonder: from what?

**The Harbours [main].** Along its loop the Armada keeps harbours, huge repair stations built into moons, where its ships rest for a generation. Two hundred years ago humans found four of them empty and built their colonies inside. Keel's shipyards are the Armada's dry docks. The vault sits under Landfall. The colonists called these places ruins, but the owners had only gone out, and had carved their return date on the wall.

**Two hundred years ago: the company's secret [depth].** A shipping firm from Earth's Atlantic ports founded the colonies, which is why the names are Portuguese, Cape Verdean, West African and Galician. Within ten years its engineers read the carvings, found the return date, and buried it. Nobody invests in a colony with an eviction notice. Reis's family owns that firm.

**Eleven years ago: Vaz opens the vault [main].** Then a captain, Vaz found the Relay under Landfall and told only the Council's inner circle.

**Six years ago: the Armada returns [main].** The sails appeared on the carved date, to the hour. Monarch's ships hailed. The Fleet fired. Since then the Relay has kept the Fleet alive against an enemy a hundred times its size, but it can't make them win.

**Six months ago: the deal [main].** Losing slowly, Vaz and Reis opened a secret line to Kestrel through Faria and began forwarding the Relay's messages. Kestrel would win famous battles, take back Farhaven and Brightwater, seize the Helm and sign a peace leaving humans Keel and Landfall. Monarch's offers of terms went to Faria's translators, and stopped there.

### Places that hold the history

- The Relay vault under Landfall [main]: pale stone, a slow ring of light that prints one message a week at the same hour, and a carving Driftwood reads aloud in act two. It's the founding law.
- The harbour timetables [main]: every colony has one, painted over or built behind. The player can find the Armada's arrival at Landfall carved into a wall the first level runs past.
- The Mirror Fleets [depth, side mission]: two ancient fleets still face each other in an empty system, whole and silent, where the Long Stalemate ended.
- Driftwood's star chart [depth]: the whole loop, with other species who plan their lives around the Armada's visits.
- Earth's last message [depth, left open]: Earth went quiet ninety years ago, and one archive line hints Earth had met the Armada too.

In play, the player meets each layer in order: odd number-rows on a wall in level one, Driftwood reading them in act two, and the founding law bringing Kestrel down in act three. Everything marked [depth] sits off the main path.

## 6. What to borrow, and what this looks like

### Techniques worth taking

- Halo: Combat Evolved kept its history off-screen: a few odd words, one giant structure. Here three iceberg facts carry the plot and the rest stays optional.
- In Dune, one resource explains ecology, money, religion and politics at once. Here the timetable does that job for the Armada.
- The Three-Body Problem builds its biggest ideas from cold logic. The timetable comes the same way: if foresight can't be destroyed, the only defence is to have no secrets.
- Britain's Double Cross System fed Berlin a false D-Day through turned spies. When Ines finds the leak, she keeps it open and sends a lie through it.
- Return of the Obra Dinn lets players walk frozen moments to rebuild what happened.

### Nearest lookalikes

Halo has a soldier, an AI and an alien alliance. Here Ines is an ordinary marine, her AI is gagged, and the aliens are one sailing nation coming home on a published date, with no religion and no superweapon.

In Edge of Tomorrow, aliens know the battle ahead through a time loop. Here nobody relives anything; a machine and a political deal supply the foresight.

Battlestar Galactica hunts traitors who look human; here the traitors are the Fleet's own leaders. Mass Effect's Migrant Fleet is a refugee nation on ships; the Armada is strong and owns the houses it's coming back to.

## 7. Threads

I listed fourteen candidate threads and kept five.

1. **The lost week.** Ines investigates six days she can't remember. Each answer widens the story, from her squad to the Fleet, the Council and the Armada.
2. **Bosun's order.** An AI told to keep a secret from the person it protects, where breaking the order destroys the evidence. It's the heart of the game and the main investigation tool.
3. **The deal.** Vaz, Faria and Reis trade two colonies for peace. It carries the politics and the money, and Vaz has an argument the player half agrees with.
4. **Kestrel against Monarch.** The young captain who breaks the timetable against the old ruler who keeps it.
5. **Driftwood.** The harbours, the timetable and Monarch's real wishes reach the player through a person instead of text.

Folded in: Mensah-Lima, Dado's notebook and Faria's guilt. Cut: a Relay technician who spots the pattern (Ines does that job), a brother on Farhaven (too familiar), and a rival marine with an older backup (a second memory puzzle would muddy the first).

### Where they crash into each other

Vaz reads next week, so he sees Ines coming. Files move the day before she looks for them. A witness gets transferred before she asks. The player feels the opponent playing from the future. That forces the real question: how do you surprise him?

The lost week leads to Faria, the person Ines trusts most and the one holding the pen at the Relay.

Each win the deal hands Kestrel weakens Monarch, until Driftwood sees Kestrel break the timetable and names it as foresight.

All five meet at Farhaven, the battle the deal was built to lose.

## 8. Twists and how each is planted

Five picked. Rejected: Faria is Ines's mother (soap opera); Ines is someone's clone (the stock "you're the copy" twist); a future Ines writes the messages (the best-known time twist, and it would shrink the deal).

**A. Two commanders who are never surprised (midpoint).** Vaz and Kestrel read the same messages. Planted by the opening briefing praising Vaz's record, marines joking that "Kestrel must have a Vaz of its own", and alien fleets changing course at the hour the Relay prints each week.

**B. The future got her killed (end of act one).** Next week's message told Vaz that Ines would find the transmitter. Planted by alien ships turning toward her pod before launch, and evidence vanishing a day early all through act one.

**C. Faria killed her to save her (act two).** See wow moment 3.

**D. They're coming home, on schedule (act two).** The colonies sit inside alien harbours, and the invasion date was carved on the walls. Planted by the oversized docks, the number-rows in level one, Monarch's "We are here as written", and Driftwood walking Landfall without a map.

**E. The wrong alien (act three).** Kestrel means to take all four colonies once crowned. Monarch has offered terms since day one, and Faria's translators buried them. Planted by Kestrel's ships firing first in every recorded first contact while Monarch's never do, and Faria calling Kestrel "someone you can do business with".

## 9. Wow moments

**1. The impossible dodge.** In the replay of her death, the player freezes an alien ship swerving away from a missile nobody has fired yet. *Set up by:* the opening drop, played in real time first. *Why it's fair:* it's the premise, shown instead of told, and the player gets to find it.

**2. "Hi. You won't remember this."** Deep in the recordings, lost-week Ines looks into Bosun's camera and talks to her future self. She knew Faria's plan. She walked into the ambush on purpose, because skipping the drop would prove she knew. She chose to forget so the next Ines could pass any loyalty test honestly. *Set up by:* the tutorial's squad signal (two taps on the ear means "I'm lying"), which she uses while agreeing to keep quiet; Dado's notebook page, "She said if she skips the drop, they'll know she knows." *Why it's fair:* it uses rule two exactly as stated, by a character acting on what she wants.

**3. The mercy kill.** Ines learns that her mentor killed her, and that it was the only way to save her life. *Set up by:* Faria's line about dying, the cancelled deletion in the backup log. *Why it's fair:* the player has known since minute five that death here is survivable and deleted backups aren't. Faria is the one person who did that sum for Ines.

**4. The people with no secrets.** Driftwood reads the number-rows on the wall in level one: the Armada's arrival at Landfall, carved centuries ago, to the hour. The invasion was announced two hundred years early. A whole civilisation chose to publish its future so that foresight could never be used against it again. *Set up by:* the wall in level one, "We are here as written", the sails arriving on the dot. *Why it's fair:* it follows from rule one by cold logic, and it explains why Kestrel's surprise moves look like cheating to the rest of the Armada.

**5. Writing next week.** Ines can't hide her plan from a man who reads the future. So she doesn't try. She turns Faria, who writes the messages. A week after the plan, Faria stands at the Relay and sends back a calm, empty report. This week's Vaz reads it, relaxes, and walks into his own arrest. At the same hour the same channel sends Kestrel a false future, and thousands of alien sails swing toward an attack that isn't there. *Set up by:* rule one ("the future it describes can change"), the files that vanished early in act one, and Faria's job at the Relay, shown in act one. *Why it's fair:* the player saw who holds the pen long before they realise the pen is the weapon.

**6. "Now you know."** At Farhaven, an hour before the arrest, Vaz orders Bosun to lock Ines out of the Relay. Bosun has been waiting for this. Ines has now seen every recording, so the evidence lives in her head, safe from a reset. Bosun says, "No, Admiral," knowing the reset will take its own last week. Then, for the first time: "Go, Ines." *Set up by:* the reset rule in act one and Bosun's literal obedience all game. *Why it's fair:* Bosun breaks the order only when breaking it costs the plot nothing and costs Bosun everything.

## 10. How the player plays

Third-person action and investigation, about half each.

You drop from orbit and steer your pod through flak. You fight through docks, colony streets and alien sail-ships, and sneak through your own flagship. When you die, you come back regrown.

The investigation has six moves:

- Walk the lost week. Bosun's recordings become frozen 3D moments you step through and scrub back and forth. You watch yourself from outside and read your own body language.
- Spot the impossible move. In battle replays, find each moment the enemy reacted before it could have known.
- Line up the clocks. Put Relay printouts, the Admiral's orders and alien moves on one timeline.
- Read light. Driftwood teaches you a few alien light patterns by showing and matching, so you can read signals and the harbour timetables yourself.
- Make the case. To accuse someone, fill a few slots: who, which message, which battle. The game checks the whole answer at once, so guessing doesn't pay.
- Write a false future. In act three, pick a target, a time and a fleet size Kestrel will believe, send it, then fight the battle your lie made.

## 11. Endings

**A. Break the Relay.** Ines smashes the machine. She exposes Kestrel, Monarch agrees to talk, and Vaz goes to prison. It's clean and fair, but small: the war just turns ordinary.

**B. Publish the future (my pick).** Ines carries the Relay to Monarch's ship with every message ever forwarded to Kestrel. That record is the proof the founding law needs, and Monarch's court strips Kestrel. Monarch breaks the last Relay in front of both fleets. Then Ines does the Armada's own trick: she has the treaty written as a timetable, every ship movement and handover date published for both sides, with nothing left to steal. The Armada takes back its harbours, and humans stay on in two of them as tenants. The outer colonies are moved out openly instead of being sold in secret.

Ines goes on trial for handing the aliens the Fleet's greatest weapon, and half the colonies cheer the charge. Bosun broke a direct order, so the Fleet resets it. In the last scene Bosun wakes up, a week short, and Ines says, "Welcome back. This time I'm allowed to tell you."

I pick B because it grows from the iceberg's central idea, it costs both leads something, and it echoes the opening.

**C. Keep the Relay.** Ines and Faria keep the machine and write next week themselves. Humans win, and Ines becomes the new Vaz. This dark ending could sit beside B as a player choice.

## 12. Simplicity check

After the first hour, a stranger could say: "A soldier and her AI fight an alien fleet, and someone on our side is telling the aliens our moves. She died finding out and can't remember who." By the end of act one they'd add: "The Admiral has a machine that gets messages from next week."

Five invented terms: **the Relay**, **the Armada**, **the Harbours**, **the Helm** (the alien throne) and **regrowing from a backup**. The timetable is a plain word. Code names and colony names are ordinary English.

Risks:

- Halo's shadow. The early scenes must lead with the leak and the lost week, not the armour.
- Time logic. If a message changes the future, what did it describe? Rule one answers in one sentence, and the story goes no further.
- "The aliens were right all along." Twists D and E lean that way. The guard: Kestrel is a real danger, Monarch's people did hide one Relay, and Vaz is half right.
- Ending B must not feel rushed. The timetable has to appear in level one and be read by the middle of act two.

### Contrived spots fixed in this pass

- Nobody wiped Bosun's memory. Now the Fleet resets AIs only for disobedience, so the plotters had to use an order, and the order is a trap.
- Vaz reads next week, so Ines couldn't surprise him. Now he does see her coming, and she wins by turning the person who writes the messages.
- Ines's death was a plain hit, and she walked into a known ambush. Now the death is Faria's mercy and the walk-in is Ines's own plan.
- Monarch's peace offers vanished without a reason. Now Faria's translators buried them.
- The old ending asked both sides to trust messages written by humans. Now the Relay is broken and the treaty is published.
