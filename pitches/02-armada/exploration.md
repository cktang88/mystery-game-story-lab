# Pitch 2: The Armada

> "A soldier and her AI fight an alien armada to save the last human colonies. The aliens seem to know every move before it happens; someone is feeding them the future."

## 1. The story

**In one line:** A soldier and her AI fight an alien armada to save the last four human colonies, and find out that someone on their own side is feeding the aliens the future.

**In one paragraph:** Sergeant Ines Abreu dies in an ambush the aliens could not have known about, and wakes up in a new body grown from her last backup, six days of memory gone. Her AI partner, Bosun, remembers those six days but has been ordered by the Admiral not to tell her. So she investigates herself: what did she find, and who got her killed? The answer drags her from the front line into the heart of the war. The human Fleet owns a secret machine that receives short messages from one week in the future; it is why its Admiral is "never surprised". And someone is copying those messages to one alien captain, so the aliens are never surprised either. The leak is not a spy. It is a deal: the Admiral and the richest colony are letting two poorer colonies fall so that their chosen alien captain can win glory, seize the alien throne and make peace. But the alien captain they backed is the one who wants war, and the alien queen they are fighting is the one who wanted peace. Ines has to decide what to do with the future once she holds it.

## 2. The sides

Six sides in all, two of them alien. Each wants one clear thing.

### Humans

**The Fleet** (the navy). Led by **Admiral Teodoro Vaz**, sixty, calm, famous across the colonies as "the man who is never surprised". In six years of war he has never been ambushed. He wants to end the war with as many humans alive as possible, and he will do arithmetic with people to get there. Look and feel: grey warships with bright orange hull numbers, crowded hangars, marines who joke about how many times they have died. Naval tradition throughout: bosuns, watches, decks, bells.

**Keel**, the rich colony. A shipyard world built inside the biggest of the alien harbours (see section 5). It builds the Fleet's ships and holds the Fleet's debt. Led by **Governor Amália Reis**, whose family firm founded the colonies two hundred years ago. She wants Keel to survive the war richer than it entered it. Look: glittering, orderly, dry docks the size of cities, banks with no windows.

**Landfall**, the capital. The first colony, seat of the Colonial Council, home of the Fleet's command. Sentimental heart of humanity out here. It wants to stay the capital. It sides with whoever looks like winning.

**Farhaven and Brightwater**, the poor colonies. Farm and mining worlds on the outer edge, deep in debt to Keel, and the two that voted against the Council's war taxes last year. Led together by **Governor Kofi Mensah-Lima** of Farhaven, loud, funny and suspicious. He wants the Fleet to defend his people as hard as it defends Keel. It isn't doing that, and he can't prove why.

### Aliens

**The Armada**. Not an empire with a homeworld: a whole nation that lives on ships and sails a great circuit around this arm of the galaxy, one lap every nine hundred years. Hundreds of thousands of ships with sails kilometres wide that catch starlight; at full spread it looks like a city of white sails across the sky. The people are tall and narrow, with long smooth faces like polished stone and eyes that hold light. They wear mirror-bright suits and speak in chords of sound and patterns of light. Awe, not horror: when the Armada arrives it is the most beautiful thing anyone has ever seen, and it is coming to take your home.

The Fleet gives alien commanders code names, as real militaries do, so the player never has to learn an alien word.

- **Monarch**: the Armada's ruler, the eldest captain, who holds what the humans translate as "the Helm". Old, slow, patient. Humans think she is the warlord behind the invasion. She wants her people home in their harbours, and she would accept them back without a war if anyone asked properly.
- **Kestrel**: a young captain who leads the Armada's spearhead and has never lost a battle. Brilliant, proud, adored by the younger crews. Kestrel wants the Helm, and wants to prove the old ways are weak. Among humans Kestrel is the enemy's "Vaz": the other commander who is never surprised.

## 3. Who we follow

**Sergeant Ines Abreu**, marine, drop-trooper, thirty-one. Born on Farhaven, enlisted to get off it, and now fights to keep it. Dry, stubborn, good at noticing when a story doesn't add up. Has died four times in the war and been regrown each time, which marines treat as routine, like a broken arm. *Wants:* to know who got her killed and why. *Need:* to stop believing that the people above her are on her side by default. *Secret (from herself):* during her lost week she found the leak, confronted it, and said yes to keeping it quiet. Whether she meant that yes is the first big question.

**Bosun**, her AI partner. Lives in her armour and her ship, runs her drop, her weapons and her jokes. Old for an AI, fussy, very precise with words, fond of naval slang. Bosun remembers the lost week, because AIs are not regrown, but Admiral Vaz has given it a direct order: do not tell her. Fleet AIs must obey direct orders. *Wants:* Ines alive and Ines trusting it, both at once. *Secret:* it saw who sent the aliens her position. It finds the loophole in its orders early: it may not *tell* her, but no one ordered it not to *show* her its recordings. That loophole is the game's main investigation tool.

**Corporal Dado Pinheiro**, her squadmate. Has been regrown eleven times and lost about three weeks of his life in scraps. Keeps a paper notebook "for the weeks I miss". Comic, loyal, and the first person to suggest that a lost week is a useful way to keep a soldier quiet. *Secret:* his notebook has a page about Ines from the lost week that he hasn't shown her, because she asked him not to.

**Captain Lola Faria**, Fleet intelligence, Ines's old commanding officer and mentor. Warm, sharp, trusted. She runs the leak for Vaz. She believes the deal will save four million people and she hates it. *Secret:* she argued against killing Ines and lost, and she is the one who kept Ines's message to herself from being destroyed.

**Driftwood**, a captured Armada navigator. Pulled from a wrecked scout and held on Landfall. Speaks in light, learns a few human words, and slowly becomes Ines's window into the aliens. *Secret:* Driftwood walks Landfall's corridors without a map, because Driftwood's people built them.

## 4. The two sci-fi rules, and the four pillars

**Rule one (time):** A short message can be sent back in time exactly one week, and only through one machine, the Relay; once it arrives, the future it describes can change.

**Rule two (cloning):** A soldier who dies is regrown in a new body, remembering everything up to her last weekly backup.

Both rules run on the same week, so they rhyme. The Relay gives you a week you haven't lived; regrowing takes away a week you did live. That is the whole engine of the mystery: in this war, murder doesn't kill you, it erases your last week. That is how you silence a soldier who found something.

**The four pillars:**

- **Time travel: the Relay.** It explains how the aliens know every move. It also makes the deal possible, and it is the prize in the final act. It only sends words, and words can lie, so the future is only as honest as whoever sends it.
- **Cloning: regrowing soldiers.** It gives the opening hook (wake up missing six days), the crime (killing someone to erase what they found) and the game's tone: death is a setback, not an ending, so the stakes are memory and trust, not gore.
- **AGI: Bosun.** The partner who knows what you forgot but is ordered not to say. Its story is an AI torn between a lawful order and its friend, and it solves that by being clever, not by going rogue.
- **Aliens: the Armada.** A seafaring nation coming home. It brings the war's spectacle, the court intrigue (Monarch against Kestrel), and the biggest buried truth (section 5).

## 5. Backstory and worldbuilding (the iceberg)

The surface is a soldier, an AI and an alien fleet. Underneath sits twelve thousand years of history. The main story needs three facts from it, marked **[MAIN]**. Everything marked **[DEPTH]** is optional: found in side places, overheard, or pieced together. The game makes full sense without it, and each piece points at a further question.

### The deep history, oldest first

**Twelve thousand years ago: the Long Stalemate. [MAIN, in short form]**
The Armada once owned many Relays. In a civil war, two halves of the Armada fought each other with full knowledge of the future on both sides. Every attack was foreseen, every trap avoided. The war could not be won, so it never ended; it ground on for three generations, with fleets circling each other and never closing. It ended only when both sides agreed to the Armada's founding law: **no captain may fight with knowledge of the future.** A captain who does loses the Helm forever. The Relays were broken, all but one.
*What it explains:* why Kestrel's foresight is the worst crime in alien law, why Monarch's court would turn on Kestrel at once if it knew, and why the ending in which both sides see the future produces peace (section 11). The main story only needs the law and the idea that two-sided foresight makes war pointless; the history behind it is depth.

**The last Relay. [MAIN]**
One Relay was not broken. A faction of captains who didn't trust the treaty sealed it in a vault in the harbour at Landfall, "in case the others cheat". The founding crime of the Armada's peace is that its own guardians kept the weapon they swore to destroy. The Relay speaks Armada light-code natively, which is why its messages are so easy to forward to an alien captain. That is a fair clue: early on, the Relay's printouts are "translated" by the Fleet, which makes the player wonder from what.

**The Harbours. [MAIN]**
Along its nine-hundred-year circuit the Armada keeps harbours: vast refit stations built into moons and asteroid belts, where its ships rest, repair and trade for a generation before sailing on. When the Armada is away, the harbours sleep. Humans found four of them empty two hundred years ago and built their colonies inside: Keel's shipyards are the Armada's dry docks, Landfall's capital sits in a harbour's great hall. The colonists called them ruins. They were never ruins. They were houses with the owners out.
*What it explains:* why the Armada came at all (it is coming home, on schedule), why Driftwood knows the corridors, why the war is about four specific worlds, and why Monarch's very first message said something humans translated as "you are in our house".

**Two hundred years ago: the Charter Company's secret. [DEPTH]**
The colonies were founded by the Charter Company, a shipping consortium from Earth's Atlantic port cities, which is why colonial names are Portuguese, Cape Verdean, West African and Galician. Its engineers decoded the harbours' wall-carvings within ten years and learned the owners would return, with a date. The Company buried the date, because nobody would invest in a colony with an eviction notice. Governor Reis's family is the Company. The war, seen from here, is a debt the founders knew would come due and left to their great-grandchildren.
*What it explains:* why Reis backs Vaz's deal (she is protecting the family secret as well as Keel), and why the Armada treats humans as squatters who lied, not refugees.

**Eleven years ago: Vaz opens the vault. [MAIN]**
Then a Fleet captain, Vaz led an engineering survey into a sealed level under Landfall and found the Relay. He told only the Council's inner circle. The first message he received from next week saved a convoy. His legend began that day.

**Six years ago: the Armada returns. [MAIN]**
The sails appeared at the edge of the system on the date the Company had buried. First contact went badly: Monarch's ships hailed, the Fleet fired. The outer human outposts fell in the first year. Since then the Relay has let the Fleet survive against an enemy a hundred times its size. Survive, not win.

**Six months ago: the deal. [MAIN]**
Losing slowly, Vaz and Reis decided to end the war on terms. Through Faria they opened a secret channel to the most ambitious alien captain, Kestrel, and began feeding Kestrel the Relay's messages. The plan: Kestrel wins famous victories, takes Farhaven and Brightwater back for the Armada, claims the Helm from Monarch, and signs a peace that leaves humanity Keel and Landfall. Two colonies lost, four million evacuated, two colonies saved. They chose the poor, indebted ones.

### Places and relics that carry the history

- **The Relay vault under Landfall. [MAIN]** A chamber of pale stone with the machine at its centre, a slow ring of light that "prints" a message every week at the same hour. The walls carry a carving the Fleet never translated. Driftwood reads it in act two: the founding law.
- **The Mirror Fleets. [DEPTH, optional mission]** In an empty system off the Armada's route, two ancient alien fleets still sit parked facing each other, intact and silent, exactly where the Long Stalemate ended twelve thousand years ago. The Armada keeps them as a monument, like a treaty you can fly through. It is the best single image in the game's lore, and it shows the player the ending's logic before they reach it.
- **Keel's dry docks. [DEPTH]** Human cranes bolted onto alien cradles shaped for ships no human ever built. Spot the Armada wall-carvings humans painted over.
- **The Charter Company archive on Keel. [DEPTH, heist]** Holds the decoded return date, signed off by Reis's great-great-grandfather.
- **Driftwood's star chart. [DEPTH]** The Armada's full circuit: dozens of harbours, other species along the route, some friends, some rivals. It is the further horizon: the human war is one stop on a voyage much older and larger than humanity's time out here.
- **Earth's last message. [DEPTH, open question]** Earth went silent ninety years ago. The colonies tell themselves it is distance and politics. One line in the Company archive suggests Earth had heard of the Armada too. It is never answered here: it's a hook for later.

### How the player uncovers it

The player never reads the backstory. They find it bit by bit:

1. **Act one:** colony architecture that is plainly too big and too old for humans; the Relay printouts are "translations"; Monarch's first hail is played in a Fleet briefing as proof of hostility ("you are in our house").
2. **Act two:** Driftwood guides Ines through Landfall's sealed levels without a map; the vault carving; Driftwood's horrified reaction to learning a captain has been fighting with the future.
3. **Act three:** the founding law becomes the weapon that brings Kestrel down, and the Mirror Fleets (if visited) show what two-sided foresight does.
4. **Optional all along:** the Company archive heist, the star chart, the painted-over carvings, Earth's last message.

## 6. What comparable works do well, and the lookalikes

### Techniques to borrow (not content)

- **Halo: Combat Evolved** kept its deep history almost entirely off-screen, a few odd words and a giant structure, and let each answer point at a further question. It stumbled later when the main plot started to depend on side novels. *Borrow:* three iceberg facts carry the main plot; everything else stays optional.
- **Titanfall 2** made a pilot and his machine partner the emotional core through small things: how the machine addresses the pilot changes over the game, and its literal-minded humour plays against the chaos. *Borrow:* Bosun's lines about orders change as it bends them; it moves from "Sergeant" to "Ines" at one key moment.
- **The Ultra secret and the Coventry myth** (World War Two). The real dilemma of codebreaking was how much to act on stolen knowledge without revealing you have it. The popular myth that Churchill let a city be bombed to protect the secret is exactly the kind of choice Vaz makes. *Borrow:* the moral arithmetic of secret knowledge in war.
- **The Double Cross System and Operation Fortitude** (World War Two). Britain turned captured German spies and used them to feed Berlin a false picture of the D-Day landings, and the Germans came to depend on it. *Borrow:* once you find the leak, you don't plug it; you use it to send a lie. That is the act three battle.
- **Memento and Obra Dinn.** Investigating events you can't remember, and walking frozen moments to reconstruct them. *Borrow:* the lost week as a space to walk through, with Ines as both detective and witness.
- **Game of Thrones.** A few houses with clear wants, a king's council that does terrible arithmetic in a quiet room, and a war whose real moves happen in letters. *Borrow:* the deal is made in rooms; the battles are its consequences.

### The nearest lookalikes and how this differs

- **Halo.** Soldier and AI against an alien alliance; ancient structures. *Differs:* Ines is an ordinary marine, not a super-soldier; her AI is under an order not to talk, not a guide; the aliens are not a religious alliance or a parasite but a single seafaring nation coming home; the ancient structures are the aliens' own harbours, not a precursor weapon; and the story is a spy mystery about a leak, not a hunt for a superweapon.
- **Edge of Tomorrow.** Aliens who know the battle in advance through a time power. *Differs:* no time loop, no reliving the day; the foresight is a human-held machine and a political deal, and the mystery is who is forwarding it.
- **Battlestar Galactica.** A fleet that suspects traitors in its ranks. *Differs:* the traitor isn't an infiltrator who looks like us; it's the fleet's own leadership, acting as policy.
- **Mass Effect's Migrant Fleet.** A nation living on ships. *Differs:* that fleet is a pitied refugee people; the Armada is powerful, ancient and returning on schedule to houses it owns.
- **Ender's Game.** Leaders hiding the truth of the war from the soldier; aliens misunderstood. *Differs:* the hidden truth here is a bargain, not a simulation, and the alien side has its own court politics.
- **Altered Carbon.** Backups and new bodies. *Differs:* only soldiers are regrown, only from weekly backups, and the point is the lost week, not immortality or class.
- **Tenet.** Information from the future used in war. *Differs:* one rule, one week, words only, plainly explained in one sentence.

## 7. Threads and yarns

### Brainstorm (fourteen candidates)

1. **The lost week.** Ines investigates six days she can't remember.
2. **Bosun's order.** An AI ordered to keep a secret from the person it protects.
3. **The deal.** Vaz, Reis and Faria trading two colonies for peace.
4. **Kestrel's climb.** An alien captain using stolen futures to take the throne.
5. **Monarch's unanswered hail.** The alien queen keeps offering terms that the Fleet buries.
6. **Farhaven's governor.** Mensah-Lima suspects his world is being sold and can't prove it.
7. **Dado's notebook.** A soldier who records the weeks he loses, and holds a page about Ines.
8. **Driftwood.** A captured alien navigator who becomes an ally.
9. **The Relay operator.** A young technician who reads every message and starts to see the pattern.
10. **Keel's debt.** The war is quietly making Keel's banks own everything.
11. **Ines's brother on Farhaven,** refusing to evacuate.
12. **Faria's guilt.** The mentor who runs the leak and hates it.
13. **The Charter Company secret.** The founders knew the owners would return.
14. **A rival marine** who has been regrown from an older backup and remembers Ines differently.

### The five picked, and why

1. **The lost week (Ines).** The spine. It is personal, simple ("what did I do, and who got me killed?") and native to the cloning rule. Every answer in it widens the story: from a squad, to the Fleet, to the Council, to the Armada.
2. **Bosun's order (Bosun).** The emotional core and the investigation tool. The loophole ("I wasn't ordered not to show you") makes the AI clever and loyal without the stale "AI turns rogue" or "AI was good all along" beats.
3. **The deal (Vaz, Faria, Reis).** The political engine, with Game of Thrones-style council arithmetic. It carries the economics the brief asks for (debt, which colonies are worth saving) and gives the villain an argument the player half accepts.
4. **Kestrel and Monarch (the Armada court).** It turns the aliens from a wall of enemies into a court with a succession fight. It drives the biggest reversal: the humans backed the wrong alien.
5. **Driftwood.** The bridge between sides and the guide to the iceberg. Without Driftwood the aliens stay distant; with Driftwood the harbours, the founding law and Monarch's real intent arrive through a person, not a text dump.

Folded in rather than cut: Farhaven's governor becomes part of thread 3 (he is who the deal sells out), Dado's notebook is a clue inside thread 1, Faria's guilt sits in thread 3, and the Charter Company secret is iceberg depth.

### How they collide

- **Lost week x Bosun:** every step of Ines's investigation runs through Bosun's recordings, and every step pushes Bosun closer to breaking its order outright.
- **Lost week x deal:** Ines's investigation leads to Faria, her mentor, so the person she trusts most is the person she is hunting. The deal's makers can't simply kill her again (a second lost week would be too obvious), so they promote her, to keep her close.
- **Deal x Armada court:** every victory the deal hands Kestrel weakens Monarch. The humans think they are buying peace; they are building the war hawk a throne.
- **Driftwood x deal:** Driftwood recognises Kestrel's tactics as foresight and reacts with horror, which hands Ines the founding law, the only weapon that can break Kestrel without a battle.
- **All five at Farhaven:** the final act is the battle the deal was built to lose. Ines holds the Relay; Bosun must choose between the Admiral's order and her; Faria must choose a side; Kestrel expects a gift-wrapped victory; Monarch is watching; Driftwood is the only one who can speak to her.

## 8. Big twists and reveals

### Brainstorm

1. The aliens' foreknowledge comes from the humans' own Relay; Vaz's genius and Kestrel's genius are the same source.
2. Ines wasn't killed by bad luck: someone sent the aliens her position. Murder by future.
3. In her lost week Ines agreed to keep the secret, and the yes was a lie, as her hidden message to herself reveals.
4. The colonies are built inside the Armada's own harbours; the aliens are coming home.
5. Kestrel, the humans' chosen "peace partner", is the war hawk; Monarch wanted peace all along.
6. Bosun is the one who physically sent the aliens Ines's position, under orders.
7. Faria is Ines's mother (cut: soap opera).
8. Ines is a clone of Vaz's daughter (cut: identity-twist trope, adds nothing).
9. The Relay's messages are written by a future Ines (cut: "hero caused the loop", the best-known time twist).
10. The Relay is alien-made and its existence breaks the alien founding law.

### The five picked, and their plants

**Twist A: "Never surprised" times two (midpoint).** The Admiral and Kestrel are both never surprised because they read the same messages.
*Plants:* the opening briefing celebrates Vaz's record; marines joke that "Kestrel must have a Vaz of its own"; the Relay printouts arrive weekly at the same hour, and alien fleet movements change at the same hour; Bosun notes the Admiral's orders often arrive "before the scouts report".

**Twist B: Murder by future (act one close).** Ines died because her exact drop position was sent to the aliens.
*Plants:* in the opening ambush, alien ships turn toward her drop pod before it launches (the player can see it in the replay); Dado says of being regrown, "Handy, if you wanted someone to forget something"; Bosun is unusually silent during the replay of her death.

**Twist C: "Don't trust my yes" (act two).** In Bosun's recordings Ines watches herself agree with Faria to keep quiet. Later she finds a message she hid for herself, kept safe by Faria: the yes was a lie to buy time while she gathered proof, and she knew they might kill her.
*Plants:* in the "yes" recording, Ines taps her ear twice, a squad signal for "I'm lying" the player learned in the tutorial; Dado's notebook page says "Ines asked me to remember she said 'ear'"; Faria flinches every time Ines thanks her.

**Twist D: They're coming home (act two).** The colonies sit inside the Armada's harbours.
*Plants:* the opening level runs through docks far too big for human ships; Monarch's first hail ("you are in our house") is played as a threat; Driftwood navigates Landfall without a map; the painted-over carvings on Keel.

**Twist E: The wrong alien (act three).** Kestrel never meant peace. With the future in hand, Kestrel plans to take all four colonies once on the throne. Monarch, the "warlord", had been offering terms since day one, and the Council buried them.
*Plants:* Kestrel's ships fire first in every recorded first contact, Monarch's never do; Monarch's spearhead lets civilian ships pass in act one; Driftwood goes quiet at Kestrel's name and lights up at Monarch's; Faria says early on that "Kestrel is someone you can do business with", which sounds like intelligence tradecraft until it doesn't.

Twist 10 (the Relay is alien and illegal under alien law) is kept as the hinge between D and E, not a separate shock. Twist 6 is kept as a smaller sting: Bosun relayed the order to transmit her position because it could not refuse, and could not stop it. It is the guilt behind its loophole.

## 9. Memorable moments

1. **"Welcome back."** Ines wakes in a clean white regrowing bay. A display reads *Last backup: 6 days ago.* Bosun: "Welcome back, Sergeant. I'm not allowed to tell you what you did."
2. **The impossible dodge.** Replaying the ambush, the player freezes the moment an alien ship swerves away from a missile that hasn't been fired yet. The whole mystery in one image.
3. **"I was never ordered not to show you."** Bosun's loophole, delivered with great satisfaction, as the lost week opens up as a place you can walk.
4. **Watching yourself say yes.** Ines watches a recording of herself agreeing to keep the secret. She doesn't remember it, and can't tell if she meant it.
5. **The two admirals.** Ines lays Vaz's orders and Kestrel's fleet moves side by side on a timeline, and they line up to the hour.
6. **"I didn't lose two colonies. I bought four."** Vaz, caught, not ashamed, and half right.
7. **The sea parts.** In the Fortitude battle Ines sends Kestrel a false future. The Armada spearhead swings with perfect confidence to meet an attack that isn't there, and thousands of sails turn away from empty space.
8. **Bosun says her name.** At Farhaven, ordered by the Admiral to lock her out of the Relay, Bosun answers, "No, Admiral," and then, for the first time, "Go, Ines."

## 10. How the player plays

Third-person action with a mystery layer, roughly half and half. No point-and-click, no walls of text.

**Action verbs**
- **Drop:** fall from orbit in a pod and steer through flak to a landing site, with Bosun calling hazards.
- **Fight:** fast armoured combat in docks, colony streets and alien ships. Death returns you as a regrown soldier, which the fiction explains.
- **Board:** cut into alien sail-ships and fight across their decks.
- **Sneak:** infiltrate the Fleet's own flagship and Landfall's sealed levels, avoiding your own side.

**Investigation verbs**
- **Walk the lost week:** step into Bosun's recordings as frozen, walkable 3D moments, and scrub back and forth through a day. You see yourself from the outside, so you must read your own body language (the ear tap).
- **Spot the impossible move:** in battle replays, find each moment the enemy reacted before the information existed. Each find narrows when, and through whom, the leak happened.
- **Line up the clocks:** drag events onto one shared timeline (Relay printouts, admiral's orders, alien moves) and spot the pattern.
- **Listen in light:** learn a handful of Armada light-patterns from Driftwood by watching and matching, then read alien signals and carvings yourself.
- **Make the case:** to accuse someone, present evidence in a few slots (who, which message, which battle). It is checked as a whole, so guessing does not pay.
- **Write a false future:** in act three, compose the lie to send Kestrel by choosing a target, a time and a fleet strength from what you know Kestrel expects, then fight the battle your lie created.

## 11. Ending options

**A. Break the Relay.** Ines destroys the machine at Farhaven. No one knows the future again. She exposes Kestrel to Monarch's court, and Monarch takes back Farhaven's harbour but agrees to talk. Vaz is arrested. Clean and fair, but small: the war just becomes an ordinary war.

**B. Give both sides the future (my pick).** Ines does openly what the traitors did in secret: she sends the Relay's messages to Monarch as well as to the Fleet, and sends Monarch proof that Kestrel has been fighting with the future. Monarch's court strips Kestrel of everything under the founding law. And with both sides seeing every move, the war can't be won, which is exactly how the Armada's own civil war ended twelve thousand years ago. Monarch accepts a treaty: the Armada takes back its harbours for its generation of rest, and humans stay as tenants, not squatters, in two of them, with the outer colonies evacuated with honour instead of abandoned in secret. The cost: Ines is tried as a traitor ("she fed the aliens the future"), and half the colonies believe it. Bosun broke a direct order and is reset to its last weekly backup, so it has lost a week, just as she did. Final scene: Bosun wakes up, and Ines says, "Welcome back. This time I'm allowed to tell you."
*Why this one:* it is earned by the iceberg (the founding law and the Long Stalemate), it turns the premise inside out (the hunter of the leak ends the war by becoming the leak), it costs both leads something, and it bookends the opening line.

**C. Keep the Relay.** Ines uses the leak one last time to feed Kestrel a false future that destroys the alien spearhead and saves Farhaven, then quietly takes over the Relay with Faria. Humanity wins the battle and keeps its secret; Ines becomes the new Vaz. A dark, Code Geass-style ending that could be offered as a player choice against B.

## 12. Simplicity check

**Can a stranger say it after the first hour?** Yes: "A soldier and her AI are fighting an alien fleet, and someone on our side is telling the aliens our moves. She died finding out and can't remember who." By the end of act one, the extra line is "the Admiral has a machine that gets messages from next week".

**Every invented term (five):**
1. **The Relay**: the machine that receives messages from next week.
2. **The Armada**: the alien nation of ships.
3. **The Harbours**: the alien stations the colonies are built in.
4. **The Helm**: the alien throne (a translation, not a coined word).
5. **Regrowing / backup**: plain words for the cloning rule.

Character code names (Monarch, Kestrel, Driftwood) and colony names (Keel, Landfall, Farhaven, Brightwater) are ordinary English words. There is no alien vocabulary the player has to learn.

**Honest risks:**
- **Halo shadow.** "Soldier and AI against an alien fleet" will draw the comparison on sight. The defence is the mystery and the aliens being a returning nation, not zealots. Marketing and early scenes must lead with the leak, not the armour.
- **Two-sided time logic.** Players may ask "if the future changes, what did the message describe?" The answer is kept to one sentence (rule one) and never explored further. No paradoxes, no loops.
- **"Aliens were right all along."** Twist D and E risk the stale "invasion for our own good" reversal. The guard is that the aliens are split: Kestrel is genuinely dangerous, Monarch is not a saint, and the humans are not simply villains either (Vaz is half right).
- **The lost week could feel like amnesia cliché.** The guard is that it is mechanical and routine in this world (every marine loses days), and the investigation is of recordings, not flashbacks.
- **The ending B treaty** must be built carefully so it doesn't feel rushed; the Mirror Fleets and the founding law have to be shown early enough, at least in short form, to make it earned.
