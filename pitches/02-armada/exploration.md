# Pitch 2: The Armada

> "A soldier and her AI fight an alien armada to save the last human colonies. The aliens seem to know every move before it happens; someone is feeding them the future."

## The story in one page

Sergeant Ines Abreu dies in the first five minutes.

Her drop pod falls toward a colony under attack. Before it even leaves the ship, the alien fleet turns to meet it. They knew where she would land.

She wakes up in a clean white bay, in a new body grown from her last backup. Marines get regrown all the time. The catch is the backup: it's six days old. Ines has lost almost a week, and she has no idea what she did in it.

Her AI partner, Bosun, remembers every minute. But the Admiral has ordered Bosun not to tell her. So Bosun finds a gap in the order. It can't tell her what happened. Nobody said it couldn't show her. It plays back its recordings, and Ines walks through her own lost week like a stranger.

She sees herself chasing a signal out of the Fleet's own flagship. She sees herself confront her old mentor. Then she watches herself agree to keep quiet.

The trail leads to the Fleet's best-kept secret. Under the capital sits a machine that receives short messages from one week in the future. It's why Admiral Vaz has never been ambushed in six years of war. And someone is copying those messages to one alien captain, so the aliens are never ambushed either.

No spy is doing this. The Fleet's own leaders are. The Admiral and the richest colony are letting the two poorest colonies fall. In return, their chosen alien captain wins famous battles, takes the alien throne and signs a peace.

Except they backed the wrong alien. The captain they're feeding wants all four colonies. The alien queen they're fighting has been offering peace since day one, and nobody passed it on.

Ines now knows where the future comes from. She has to decide who gets it.

## 1. The story in one line

A soldier and her AI fight an alien armada to save the last four human colonies, and find out that their own side is feeding the aliens the future.

## 2. The sides

### Humans

**The Fleet** is the human navy. Admiral Teodoro Vaz runs it: sixty, calm, and famous as "the man who is never surprised". He wants to end the war with as many humans alive as he can, and he'll do sums with lives to get there. Picture grey warships with orange hull numbers, and marines who joke about how many times they've died.

**Keel** is the rich colony. It builds the Fleet's ships and holds the Fleet's debt. Governor Amália Reis, whose family's company founded the colonies, wants Keel to leave the war richer than it went in.

**Landfall** is the capital, home of the Council and Fleet command. It sides with whoever looks like winning.

**Farhaven and Brightwater** are the poor outer colonies, farms and mines deep in debt to Keel. Last year they voted against the war taxes. Governor Kofi Mensah-Lima of Farhaven, loud and suspicious, thinks the Fleet guards Keel harder than his people. He can't prove it.

### Aliens

**The Armada** is a whole nation that lives on ships. It has no home planet. It sails a loop around this part of the galaxy, one lap every nine hundred years, under sails kilometres wide that catch starlight. At full spread it looks like a city of white sails across the sky.

The aliens are tall and narrow, with smooth faces like polished stone and eyes that hold light. They talk in chords of sound and flashes of light. When the Armada arrives, it's the most beautiful thing anyone has seen. It has also come to take your home.

The Fleet gives alien commanders code names, so the player never learns an alien word.

- **Monarch** rules from what humans translate as "the Helm". She's old and patient. Humans think she started the war. She wants her people back in their harbours, and would have taken them back without a fight if anyone had asked.
- **Kestrel**, a young captain, leads the alien spearhead and has never lost. Kestrel wants the Helm and wants to prove the old ways weak. To humans, Kestrel is the enemy's Vaz.

## 3. Who we follow

**Sergeant Ines Abreu**, 31, a drop-trooper from Farhaven. She joined up to get off that world and now fights to keep it. She's dry and stubborn, and quick to spot a story that doesn't add up. She wants to know who got her killed. She has to learn that the people above her aren't on her side by default. Her secret is hidden from herself: in the lost week she found the leak and said yes to hiding it.

**Bosun**, her AI, lives in her armour and her ship. It flies her drops, aims her guns and makes most of the jokes. It's fussy about words and fond of navy slang. AIs aren't regrown, so Bosun remembers the lost week, and Fleet AIs must obey direct orders. It wants Ines alive and trusting it, both at once. Its own secret: the order to send her landing spot to the aliens passed through Bosun, and Bosun couldn't refuse.

**Corporal Dado Pinheiro**, her squadmate, has been regrown eleven times. He keeps a paper notebook "for the weeks I miss". It holds a page about Ines he hasn't shown her, because she asked him not to.

**Captain Lola Faria** runs Fleet intelligence and was Ines's commanding officer. She runs the leak for Vaz. She believes the deal will save four million people, and she hates it. She argued against killing Ines and lost. Then she hid the message Ines had left for herself.

**Driftwood** is a captured alien navigator. Driftwood talks in light, picks up a few human words, and becomes Ines's way into the alien side. Driftwood also walks the capital's corridors without a map, because Driftwood's people built them.

## 4. The two rules, and what the four pillars do

**Rule one:** One machine, the Relay, receives short messages sent back exactly one week; once a message arrives, the future it describes can change.

**Rule two:** A soldier who dies is regrown in a new body, remembering everything up to her last weekly backup.

Both rules run on the same week. The Relay gives you a week you haven't lived. Regrowing takes away a week you did live. So in this war, the way to silence a soldier who found something is to kill her and let her come back without it.

- **Time travel** is the Relay. It explains how the aliens know every move and becomes the prize at the end. It only sends words, and words can lie.
- **Cloning** is regrowing. It gives us the opening, the crime and the tone: death is a setback, so the stakes are memory and trust, with no gore.
- **AGI** is Bosun, who knows what you forgot and has been told not to say. It wins by being clever with its orders and never turns on anyone.
- **Aliens** are the Armada. They bring the spectacle, the fight for the alien throne, and the biggest buried truth: they're coming home.

## 5. Backstory and worldbuilding (the iceberg)

Below the surface sits twelve thousand years of history. The main story needs the parts marked **[main]**. The parts marked **[depth]** are optional, and the game makes sense without them.

### What happened, oldest first

**Twelve thousand years ago: the Long Stalemate [main, short form].** The Armada once had many Relays. In a civil war, both halves could see the future, so each side saw every attack coming and dodged every trap. Nobody could win, and the war dragged on for three generations. It ended with the Armada's founding law: no captain may fight with knowledge of the future, on pain of losing the Helm for good. Both sides broke their Relays. This law is why Kestrel's cheating is the worst crime the aliens know, and why the ending works.

**The last Relay [main].** A few captains who didn't trust the treaty kept one, sealed in a vault "in case the others cheat". It prints its messages in alien light-code, which makes them easy to pass to an alien captain. Early on, the player sees the Fleet "translating" the printouts, and can wonder: from what?

**The Harbours [main].** Along its loop, the Armada keeps harbours, huge repair stations built into moons where its ships rest for a generation. Between visits they sit empty. Two hundred years ago, humans found four and built their colonies inside. Keel's shipyards are the Armada's dry docks. The vault with the last Relay sits under Landfall. The colonists called these places ruins, but the owners were only away. That's why the Armada came, and why Monarch's first message said "you are in our house".

**Two hundred years ago: the company's secret [depth].** A shipping firm from Earth's Atlantic ports founded the colonies, which is why the names are Portuguese, Cape Verdean, West African and Galician. Its engineers read the harbour carvings and learned the owners would return, and when. The firm buried the date. Reis's family owns that firm.

**Eleven years ago: Vaz opens the vault [main].** Then a captain, Vaz led a survey into a sealed level under Landfall and found the Relay. He told only the Council's inner circle. His first message from next week saved a convoy.

**Six years ago: the Armada returns [main].** The sails appeared on the buried date. Monarch's ships hailed, and the Fleet fired. Since then the Relay has kept the Fleet alive against an enemy a hundred times its size, but it can't make them win.

**Six months ago: the deal [main].** Losing slowly, Vaz and Reis opened a secret line to Kestrel through Faria and began sending over the Relay's messages. Kestrel would win famous battles, take back Farhaven and Brightwater, seize the Helm, and sign a peace leaving humans Keel and Landfall. They picked the poor colonies to lose.

### Places that hold the history

- The Relay vault under Landfall [main]: pale stone, a slow ring of light that prints one message a week at the same hour, and a carving nobody in the Fleet can read. In act two Driftwood reads it aloud. It's the founding law.
- The Mirror Fleets [depth, side mission]: two ancient alien fleets still sit facing each other in an empty system, whole and silent, where the Long Stalemate ended. Flying through them shows the player how the ending will work.
- Keel's dry docks [depth]: human cranes bolted onto alien cradles, and alien carvings humans painted over.
- The company archive on Keel [depth, heist]: the return date, signed by Reis's great-great-grandfather.
- Driftwood's star chart [depth]: the Armada's whole loop, dozens of harbours and other species along the way. The human war is one stop on a far older voyage.
- Earth's last message [depth, left open]: Earth went quiet ninety years ago, and one line in the archive hints Earth had met the Armada too.

### How the player digs it up

In act one the player sees docks too big for human ships, printouts marked "translated", and Monarch's hail played at a briefing as a threat. In act two Driftwood leads Ines through sealed levels, reads the vault carving, and recoils at the news that a captain fights with the future. In act three the founding law brings Kestrel down. Everything marked [depth] sits off the main path.

## 6. What to borrow, and what this looks like

### Techniques worth taking

- Halo: Combat Evolved kept its history off-screen: a few odd words, one giant structure. Later games stumbled when the plot leaned on side novels. Here three iceberg facts carry the plot and the rest stays optional.
- In Titanfall 2 the robot switches from "Pilot" to the pilot's first name at one key moment. Bosun calls Ines "Sergeant" until the end.
- The Ultra secret in World War Two raised a real question: how much can you act on stolen enemy plans without giving away that you have them? Vaz faces the same sum.
- Britain's Double Cross System turned German spies and used them to feed Berlin a false D-Day. When Ines finds the leak, she keeps it open and sends a lie through it.
- Memento and Return of the Obra Dinn show how to investigate a past you can't remember by walking frozen moments.

### Nearest lookalikes

Halo has a soldier, an AI and an alien alliance. Here Ines is an ordinary marine, her AI is gagged, and the aliens are one sailing nation coming home, with no religion and no superweapon.

In Edge of Tomorrow, aliens know the battle ahead through a time loop. Here nobody relives anything; a machine and a political deal supply the foresight.

Battlestar Galactica hunts traitors who look human. Here the traitors are the Fleet's own leaders. Mass Effect's Migrant Fleet is a nation on ships, but a refugee one; the Armada is strong and owns the houses it's coming back to. Altered Carbon has new bodies, but here only soldiers get them, from weekly backups.

## 7. Threads

I listed fourteen candidate threads and kept five.

1. **The lost week.** Ines investigates six days she can't remember. Each answer widens the story, from her squad to the Fleet, the Council and the Armada.
2. **Bosun's order.** An AI told to keep a secret from the person it protects. It's the heart of the game and the main investigation tool, and it skips the worn "AI turns evil" beat.
3. **The deal.** Vaz, Faria and Reis trade two colonies for peace. It carries the politics and the money, and Vaz has an argument the player half agrees with.
4. **Kestrel against Monarch.** The fight for the alien throne turns the aliens into a court, and sets up the biggest reversal.
5. **Driftwood.** The harbours, the founding law and Monarch's real wishes reach the player through a person instead of text.

Folded in: Mensah-Lima (the man the deal sells out), Dado's notebook and Faria's guilt. Cut: a Relay technician who spots the pattern (Ines does that job), a brother on Farhaven (too familiar), and a rival marine with an older backup (a second memory puzzle would muddy the first).

### Where they crash into each other

Every step of the lost week runs through Bosun's recordings, and every step pushes Bosun closer to breaking its order.

The lost week leads to Faria, so the person Ines trusts most is the one she's hunting. The dealmakers can't kill her twice without it looking odd. They promote her and keep her close.

Each win the deal hands Kestrel weakens Monarch. The humans think they're buying peace while they build a warmonger a throne.

Driftwood recognises Kestrel's tactics as foresight, which hands Ines the founding law: the one weapon that can break Kestrel without a battle.

All five meet at Farhaven, the battle the deal was built to lose. Ines holds the Relay. Bosun must pick between the Admiral and her. Faria must pick a side. Kestrel expects a gift-wrapped win, and only Driftwood can speak to Monarch.

## 8. Twists and how each is planted

Five picked. Rejected: Faria is Ines's mother (soap opera); Ines is someone's clone (the stock "you're the copy" twist); a future Ines writes the messages (the best-known time twist, and it would shrink the deal).

**A. Two commanders who are never surprised (midpoint).** Vaz and Kestrel read the same messages. Planted by the opening briefing praising Vaz's record, marines joking that "Kestrel must have a Vaz of its own", and alien fleets changing course at the same hour each week that the Relay prints.

**B. Killed by the future (end of act one).** Someone sent the aliens Ines's landing spot. Planted by alien ships turning toward her pod before it launches, visible in the replay; Dado calling regrowing "handy, if you wanted someone to forget something"; and Bosun going quiet whenever her death replays.

**C. "Don't trust my yes" (act two).** Ines watches herself agree to hide the leak. Later she finds a message she left herself: the yes was a lie to buy time, and she knew they might kill her. Planted by her tapping her ear twice in the recording, the squad signal for "I'm lying" from the tutorial, and Dado's notebook: "Ines said to remember 'ear'."

**D. They're coming home (act two).** The colonies sit inside the aliens' harbours. Planted by the first level's oversized docks, Monarch's "you are in our house", and Driftwood walking Landfall without a map.

**E. The wrong alien (act three).** Kestrel means to take all four colonies once crowned. Monarch has offered terms since day one, and the Council buried them. Planted by Kestrel's ships firing first in every recorded first contact while Monarch's never do, Monarch's ships letting civilian transports pass in act one, and Faria calling Kestrel "someone you can do business with".

## 9. Moments people will talk about

1. Ines wakes in the white bay. A screen reads *Last backup: 6 days ago.* Bosun: "Welcome back, Sergeant. I'm not allowed to tell you what you did."
2. In the replay, the player freezes an alien ship mid-swerve, dodging a missile nobody has fired yet.
3. Bosun, pleased with itself: "I was never ordered not to show you."
4. Ines watches herself say yes, and can't tell if she meant it.
5. Vaz, caught and unashamed: "I didn't lose two colonies. I bought four."
6. Ines sends Kestrel a false future. The alien spearhead swings to meet an attack that isn't there, and thousands of sails turn toward empty space.
7. At Farhaven the Admiral orders Bosun to lock Ines out of the Relay. Bosun answers, "No, Admiral." Then, for the first time: "Go, Ines."

## 10. How the player plays

Third-person action and investigation, about half each.

In the action half, you drop from orbit and steer your pod through flak while Bosun calls hazards. You fight through docks, colony streets and alien sail-ships. You sneak through your own flagship, hiding from your own side. When you die, you come back regrown.

The investigation has six moves:

- Walk the lost week. Bosun's recordings become frozen 3D moments you step through and scrub back and forth. You watch yourself from outside and read your own body language.
- Spot the impossible move. In battle replays, find each moment the enemy reacted before it could have known.
- Line up the clocks. Drag Relay printouts, the Admiral's orders and alien moves onto one timeline and find the pattern.
- Read light. Driftwood teaches you a few alien light patterns by showing and matching, so you can read signals and carvings yourself.
- Make the case. To accuse someone, fill a few slots: who, which message, which battle. The game checks the whole answer at once, so guessing doesn't pay.
- Write a false future. In act three, pick a target, a time and a fleet size Kestrel will believe, send it, then fight the battle your lie made.

## 11. Endings

**A. Break the Relay.** Ines smashes the machine, so nobody sees the future again. She exposes Kestrel, Monarch agrees to talk, and Vaz goes to prison. It's clean and fair, but small: the war just turns ordinary.

**B. Give both sides the future (my pick).** Ines does in the open what the traitors did in secret. She sends the Relay's messages to Monarch as well as the Fleet, with proof that Kestrel cheated. Monarch's court strips Kestrel under the founding law. With both sides seeing every move, nobody can win, just as in the Long Stalemate.

Monarch signs. The Armada takes back its harbours, and humans stay on in two of them as tenants. The outer colonies are moved out openly, with help, instead of being sold in secret.

Ines goes on trial as a traitor, and half the colonies believe the charge. Bosun broke a direct order, so the Fleet resets it to its last weekly backup. In the last scene Bosun wakes up, and Ines says, "Welcome back. This time I'm allowed to tell you."

I pick B because the iceberg sets it up, it flips the premise, it costs both leads something, and it echoes the opening.

**C. Keep the Relay.** Ines feeds Kestrel one last false future, wipes out the alien spearhead and saves Farhaven. Then she quietly takes over the Relay with Faria and becomes the new Vaz. This dark ending could sit beside B as a player choice.

## 12. Simplicity check

After the first hour, a stranger could say: "A soldier and her AI fight an alien fleet, and someone on our side is telling the aliens our moves. She died finding out and can't remember who." By the end of act one they'd add: "The Admiral has a machine that gets messages from next week."

Five invented terms: **the Relay** (gets messages from next week), **the Armada** (the alien nation of ships), **the Harbours** (alien stations the colonies sit in), **the Helm** (the alien throne) and **regrowing from a backup** (the cloning rule). Code names and colony names are ordinary English words.

Risks:

- Halo's shadow. The early scenes must lead with the leak and the lost week, not the armour.
- Time logic. If a message changes the future, what did it describe? Rule one answers in one sentence, and the story goes no further.
- "The aliens were right all along." Twists D and E lean that way. The guard is that the aliens are split, Kestrel is a real danger, and Vaz is half right.
- The lost week could read as a stock memory-loss plot. Here every marine loses days, and Ines digs through recordings, not flashbacks.
- Ending B must not feel rushed. The founding law has to appear by act two.
