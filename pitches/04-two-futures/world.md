# Pitch 4: Two Futures, the world

This is the world beneath `exploration.md`. The one-line frame and its two rules stay as they are:

- Rule 1: only information can travel back through the Gate, so time travellers arrive as recordings of their minds and are grown new bodies here.
- Rule 2: every change to the present rewrites the future, so a time traveller's knowledge goes stale.

The present year is 2391. The challenge log in `world-challenges.md` shows how each part below was argued into shape.

## 1. The one central thing: the lag

**News can travel no faster than a ship.**

Human ships cross between stars faster than light; radio cannot. So a letter, a price or an order from the capital moves only as fast as the courier carrying it. Lagos Nova to Meridian Station takes six days. Lagos Nova to the far edge of the Union takes five weeks. People call this delay the lag, and every part of human life is built around it.

- **Money.** Cargo spends weeks in flight, so merchants borrow against it, and the old banks grew rich on that credit. Whoever hears the news first wins the market, so the banks also run the fastest courier drones.
- **Power.** Orders from Lagos Nova are always weeks old when they land, so each world's governor acts on her own for months at a time. The Union is a loose league, not a tight empire.
- **Machines.** If you can't get the news early, the next best thing is to guess it. So humans built forecasting AIs, and then passed a law to stop them trading on their guesses. Sabi is the best of these forecasters.
- **Talk.** People greet each other with "What news?" Old information is "stale". "Every day out, you know less" is a sailor's proverb before the Admiral ever says it.

The Gate breaks the lag twice. A ship that enters it comes out at another ring, hundreds of light-years away, in the same instant. And a signal sent into it can come out earlier than it went in.

So the Gate threatens everyone who lives off the lag: the banks, the Varela Line, the governors who like being left alone. It also tempts everyone, because a message from the future is the fastest news there is. The two teams of time travellers are the lag trade taken to its limit. And the Glass are what that trade looks like when it is allowed to run for six hundred years.

## 2. The deep timeline

### The builders (more than 40,000 years ago)

Someone built a few hundred rings, each forty kilometres across, scattered over a large part of the galaxy. Nobody knows who. Beside some of the rings sit worlds with quiet, whole cities and no one in them.

The hidden truth: the builders found the rings' back channel and argued themselves out of existence, as the Glass later nearly did.

### The Glass, early (60,000 to 40,000 years ago)

The Glass evolved on a bright world under a hot white star. Their bodies are living crystal. They feed on light, think slowly and live about three thousand years. They reached nearby stars in slow ships over thousands of years, which suited a people who could wait.

About 40,000 years ago they found their first ring and learned to light it. Lit rings let a ship step from one ring to any other. The Glass spread across hundreds of worlds and grew to billions. They found the builders' empty cities and assumed the builders had moved on.

### The early echo (40,000 to 11,200 years ago)

Lit rings did one strange thing. A signal sent into a ring sometimes came out a fraction of a second before it went in. For tens of thousands of years this "early echo" was a curiosity for Glass scholars. Slowly, over centuries of patient work, they learned to stretch it: to a second, a day, a year.

### The Long Argument (about 11,000 years ago)

Two Glass powers, the near rings and the far rings, fell out over who would control the new trick. Each began warning its own past: "They will attack. Strike first." Each warning changed history, and the other side answered with warnings of its own. Wars started earlier and earlier.

Few Glass died in battle. The real damage was quieter. A Glass child takes decades to grow and centuries to come of age, and every family plans that far ahead. During the Argument no plan held, because tomorrow kept being rewritten. Cities stopped growing children. Over six centuries the Glass fell from billions to a few million.

Their cities still stand on dozens of worlds, whole and sunlit, built for people who were never born. Humans call them the Empty Worlds.

### The Ledger (end of the Argument)

The Argument ended with a cold insight. A message from the future is a weapon only while it is secret. If everyone reads it at once, it gives nobody an edge, and nobody has a reason to send one.

So the Glass did not ban the trick. They made every use of it public. A Ledger ship now sits beside every lit ring and writes every signal that comes through, forwards or backwards, into crystal that cannot be edited. Every Glass world may read it. Anyone who reads it swears never to act on a message from the future. Since then the backward channel has fallen almost silent.

The Glass also gave up recording anything else. Their speech is light, and it fades, and cameras are banned in Glass rooms, because a recording is exactly what got sent back.

After the Argument, Glass scholars looked again at the builders' empty cities and understood them. The Glass are the second people to find the rings. Their quiet aim since then has been that there is no third disaster.

### The Glass, frozen (11,000 years ago to now)

The Glass stopped making new things. New ships, new tools and new ideas all felt like risks. Their ships today are grown to the same patterns as ships from the Argument. A Glass warship from 11,000 years ago looks almost the same as one from last year.

### Humans on Earth (to 2090)

Humans grew up with their own lag. For centuries Lagos and Lisbon traders waited months for ships to bring prices across the Atlantic, until the telegraph ended that lag in 1866.

As the seas rose in the 2000s, the Lagos and Lisbon shipping houses grew rich again, building sea walls and floating ports. They had capital, shipyards and the habit of waiting a long time for a return.

### The seed ships (2090 to 2290)

Between 2090 and 2135 the Lagos–Lisbon houses launched twelve seed ships. These were slow ships, crawling at a few percent of light speed, carrying frozen embryos, clone vats and a ship AI. Each took one to two centuries to arrive.

When a ship arrived, its AI woke the vats and grew the first settlers as children, then raised them. Settlers on those worlds still say "the ship raised us".

The charter of every ship held a founding crime. Every person grown aboard owed the cost of their own making, with interest, to the house that owned the ship. People called it the passage debt. A child woke up already in debt.

### Sending (2230 to 2270)

The young colonies sat light-years apart, linked only by radio. Radio took years, but slow ships took centuries. So the colonies learned to record a person's mind and genome, radio both to another world and grow that person there in a vat. A doctor or an engineer could be "sent" to a world that needed one.

The original stayed home. The law treated the sent copy as a new person, a twin by radio. Sending is why vats, mind recordings and people with doubles feel ordinary to the Union, and why Rule 1 surprises nobody on Meridian. The Rearguard came the old way, just through time instead of space.

### The first drive and the courier age (2268 to 2300)

In 2268 an engineer on Lagos Nova built the first human faster-than-light drive. It was crude: a light-year every two weeks. It was still faster than radio, and the lag as we know it began. Sending faded. Courier drones carried the news, and the shipping houses ran the couriers.

The passage debt kept compounding. In the 2290s whole worlds stopped paying.

### The Settlement and the Union (2301)

In 2301 the worlds met inside the hull of the old seed ship *Ayo*, in orbit over Lagos Nova, and founded the Union. The Settlement cancelled the passage debt. That is the story every child learns.

The truth is that the Union bought the debt with bonds, and it still pays the houses interest from a toll on every freight crossing. The debt was never cancelled. It moved onto everyone's freight bill. The Senate still sits in *Ayo*'s old vat hall, where the first settlers of Lagos Nova were grown.

### The Varela find (2330)

In 2330 a Varela survey ship was mapping the Meridian system. Meridian is a dim red star with a metal-rich belt and a dark ring that human surveyors had puzzled over since 2309. Duarte Varela, Tomás's grandfather, found a dead Glass warship drifting in the belt.

It was a wreck from the Long Argument, 11,000 years old. Its crystal hull was dead and silent. Duarte told nobody. He filed a mining claim on the whole system and built yards in the belt.

Varela engineers copied the drive design. By 2335 Varela ships crossed a light-year in two days instead of two weeks. The Varela Line became the Union's biggest shipbuilder, and the Varela drive made the lag shorter for everyone.

### First contact (2338)

Varela test runs near the dark ring threw off a drive wake that Glass sensors knew well. It looked like a Glass warship waking up. In 2338 the Meridian ring lit for the first time in human memory, and a Glass ship came through to look. The Varela theft caused first contact. No history book says so.

### The Meridian Skirmish (2341)

For three years Glass ships searched the Meridian belt, politely, for something they would not name. In 2341 the Union cruiser *Calabar* fired on a Glass ship there. Its captain, Inês Varela, was Duarte's niece. Two human ships and one Glass ship were lost.

The Glass shut the ring for nine years. Officially, nobody ever made peace, which is why the 2391 treaty is called the first peace treaty. During those nine years, Varela moved the wreck into a sealed dry dock cut into an asteroid.

### The licensed trade (2350 to 2391)

In 2350 the Glass relit the ring and allowed a narrow, escorted trade. Meridian Station went up beside the Gate between 2350 and 2356, and the Varela Line was its main builder. The Glass sold grown crystal for sensors and computer cores. They bought new things, mostly songs, recipes, games and stories, because they had made so little that was new in 11,000 years.

In 2352 the Navy's Gate monitors logged the early echo for the first time: a test signal came out of the ring a quarter of a second before it went in. The Navy classified it. The Glass noticed that humans had noticed.

In 2353 a bank's forecasting AI on Porto Novo guessed the courier news so well that it drained the world's other banks in a week. The Senate passed the Pilot Law: an AI may forecast, but it may never trade, own anything, or hide a forecast from its human master.

In 2368 the orbital ring around Lagos Nova was finished, largely with Varela money. It is the Union's port and its busiest city. In 2371 Sabi went live as the Meridian harbour pilot. In 2383 the Glass proposed a treaty. They had spent forty years deciding to.

### 2391

The treaty is thirty days away. Then the Gate flares.

## 3. The two futures

### The Rearguard's future (real until it is changed)

- 2391: The treaty is signed. The Glass light the two dark rings in Union space. A five-week crossing becomes an afternoon.
- 2392 to 2398: Varela freight collapses. The freight toll falls, and the bond interest with it. The treaty gives the Glass the right to inspect any yard that builds ships for ring travel. Tomás fights the inspection of his yards in the Senate and the courts for seven years.
- 2399: The Senate orders the inspection. That spring a "Glass" warship burns the Lagos Nova orbital ring. It is the Varela wreck, refitted with a Varela drive. The Glass deny it. Nobody believes them.
- 2400 to 2430: War. The Glass shut the rings, and the lag returns, worse than before. The Varela Line builds the war fleet. In 2412 the Navy captures the Ledger ship at Meridian. Over the next decade, human scientists learn to stretch the early echo.
- 2431: Six soldiers record their minds and send them forty years back.

Reaching back costs power that climbs steeply with each year. Forty years took every plant the Union could spare for a month. A signal can only come out while the ring was lit, so nobody can ever reach back past 2338. The Rearguard aimed for the last moment before the signing, when the Chancellor, the Senate delegation and the vats were all on Meridian.

### The Envoys' future (never real)

Sabi wrote this one. It starts from the Rearguard's memories and rewrites them into a history where the treaty was never signed, the Navy fired on the Glass fleet at the Gate in 2392, and the same thirty-year war followed. Because it is built from the same memories, both futures share the same odd details. Both teams use this to accuse the other of theft.

## 4. The Glass today

There are about four million Glass on some sixty worlds, guarding a few hundred rings. They stand in sunlight to feed, so their cities are open to the sky. They talk in shifting light. Humans can't pronounce their names, so sailors give them nicknames, like Eight Bells, named for the chime its light makes on ship sensors. On a human ship, eight bells also marks the end of a watch.

Glass ships are grown, not built. Their living hulls hum when the drive runs, and the Glass have never tried to quiet them. In the Long Argument the enemy always knew your moves from the future anyway, so stealth was pointless. A Glass ship that makes no sound has a dead hull. Someone else is flying it.

A Glass birth happens a few times a year across all their worlds. Every Glass world marks it with a slow light-song.

Glass law has two great rules: never act on a message from the future, and never record what can fade. Their saying, put in plain English, is "Let tomorrow come to you".

The Glass want the treaty for cold reasons. Humans outnumber them a thousand to one. Humans already copied a Glass drive from a wreck, and humans have already seen the early echo. Shutting the door would only delay the day humans learn the trick alone, outside the Ledger. Signing brings them inside first. The inspection clause exists because the Glass have suspected the Meridian belt for fifty years.

Not every Glass agrees. Some would rather shut every ring near human space and wait. Eight Bells belongs to the side that believes you bind a young people with a promise and a seat at the Ledger, and the treaty grants humans exactly that seat.

This also explains Eight Bells' hint. "We heard one voice from tomorrow. Not two." It gives a count, not the content. By Glass law even that is a small crime.

## 5. The present-day factions

**The Union.** Thirty worlds and about six billion people, most of them vat-born. The Chancellor is elected by the Senate for six years. Each world's governor rules almost alone between couriers. The Senate splits along the old Settlement line. The charter worlds, home of the bondholding houses, want the Gate kept narrow. The newer worlds and the small merchants want it open, because instant travel kills the cargo credit that keeps them in debt. Chancellor Ijeoma Ferreira is the first vat-born Chancellor and a passage-stock woman to her bones.

**The charter houses.** About forty old families on Porto Novo and Lagos Nova who own the banks, the courier fleets and the Settlement bonds. They gave up vats a century ago and give birth the old way, as a mark of status. They oppose the treaty in public with words about safety, and in private because it ends the lag they live on.

**The Varela Line.** New money that married into the charter houses. Its fortune is a stolen drive and a hidden wreck. For Tomás the treaty means three disasters: the end of slow freight, the end of navy orders, and Glass inspectors walking into Dock Zero. He funds hospitals and keeps twenty people who know the secret. Most of them believe the hull in Dock Zero is a legal Glass hull plate bought in the licensed trade.

**The Union Navy.** Born in the courier age to chase pirates and forged couriers. It still remembers the *Calabar* as a ship that stood up to the aliens. It holds the classified early-echo data. Many officers expect war with the Glass one day and would like to start it on good terms.

**Meridian Station.** About 180,000 people: dockworkers, customs, traders in the licensed trade, vat doctors, Glass delegates and their human staff. The station belongs to the Union, but Varela built it and still services half its systems.

**The forecasting AIs.** Every port, bank and navy squadron has one. Under the Pilot Law they advise and never act. People trust them the way they trust a good harbour pilot: completely, and without ever asking how they know.

**The Rearguard and the Envoys** are the two new factions in the present. Their history sits in section 3.

## 6. Technology and what it costs

**Human drives.** Varela-pattern drives cross about a light-year in two days. They burn costly fuel and wear out fast, so freight is dear and fast freight is dearer.

**Courier drones.** Small uncrewed ships that carry news. The drive works as fast on a drone as on a liner, so news can never beat ship speed. The race is to launch first, from closer, more often. The banks and the Union Post run most of them, and forging a courier is one of the oldest crimes in the Union.

**Vats and sending.** A vat grows an adult body from a genome in two days and loads a recorded mind into it. A vat bay booking costs about a year's pay for a dockworker. A genome is only data, and the Union keeps everyone's on file for medicine, so DNA proves nothing about a grown person. The vats on Meridian are white, quiet medical bays, and people step out dressed.

**Mind recording.** A full recording takes hours in a scanner. The same tool replays memories as a frozen 3D scene, which is how Dez walks the Admiral's memory. Real memories are patchy; a copied one is too tidy.

**Forecasting AIs.** They learn from shipping traffic, prices, weather, votes and gossip. A good one is right about ships nine times in ten. Sabi is right about ships every time, because ships are what it was built for.

**Hum-trackers.** Human sensors that listen for the hum of Glass hulls. Every warship and dock carries them.

**The Gate.** A lit ring links to any other lit ring in an instant. Matter goes only forwards in time. A signal can go back, but only to a moment when that ring was lit, and the power cost climbs steeply with every year back. Every signal through a lit ring lands in the Ledger.

**The Ledger ships.** Grown Glass ships that sit beside each ring and record every signal in crystal. Their crews read it, swear the oath and do nothing with it.

## 7. Daily life and culture

**Food.** Union food is West African and Portuguese sea food, grown in tanks instead of caught. Vat-grown salt cod, pepper soup, jollof rice, grilled spiced suya, custard tarts, and palm wine on festival nights. The best coffee comes from the Lagos Nova highlands and costs a week's pay on a frontier world.

**Time.** Ships and stations still ring bells to mark the watch. Every station runs on Lagos Nova time and shows the courier lag beside the clock: "Lagos: 6 days."

**Post day.** When the courier from Lagos Nova docks, markets reopen, prices jump and families crowd the mail office for letters. Stations treat post day like a small holiday.

**Talk.** "What news?" is hello. "Fresh" means just in by courier; "stale" means anything older than the last post. Calling a man's news stale is an insult. Calling his forecast stale is worse.

**Belief.** Most people are Christian or Muslim, and many keep Yoruba proverbs and saints' days alongside. The civic faith is simpler: keep your word and keep your watch. A signed promise is sacred because the person who signed it may be five weeks away when it falls due.

**Celebrations.** Waking Day marks the day a world's first settlers stepped out of the vats. Children dress in white and walk out of a decorated hall into sunlight. Settlement Day marks the founding of the Union. The treaty signing is meant to become a third.

**Fears.** People fear being the last to know, forged couriers, debt, and AIs that trade. Many also carry a quiet unease about the Glass, who never seem to hurry and never seem surprised.

**Doubles.** Sending made two-of-you a familiar idea; old songs tell of a woman who met her sent twin forty years later. When the Admiral steps out of the vat, the station gossips but does not panic.

## 8. Places that carry history

**The Gate.** The Meridian ring, forty kilometres of dark metal older than the Glass. Up close, its inner face is scarred from a Long Argument battle 11,000 years ago. It is the same battle that left the Varela wreck in the belt.

**Meridian Station.** Built 2350 to 2356 at the mouth of the Gate. It has a Union half and a Glass half. The Glass rooms are bright, warm and sunlit, and no camera works in them. The observation deck sits under the ring.

**Dock Zero.** The Varela Line's sealed dry dock, cut into an asteroid in the belt in 2341. It holds the wreck, now nearly refitted with a Varela drive and human guns.

**The Ledger ship.** A grown Glass ship of humming crystal anchored beside the Gate. Its walls carry every signal since 2338 in slowly moving light.

**The *Ayo* and the Senate.** The first seed ship to reach Lagos Nova, now the Senate. Senators debate in the vat hall where the first settlers were grown. The passage-debt charter hangs framed outside the chamber, labelled "Cancelled 2301".

**The Lagos Nova orbital ring.** Forty million people live and work on a ring around the capital, linked to the ground by three elevators. In the Rearguard's memory, this is what burns.

**The *Calabar* wrecks.** The two Union ships lost in the Skirmish still drift in the Meridian belt as a Navy memorial. Pilots dip their lights when they pass.

**The Empty Worlds.** Glass cities of grown crystal under bright suns, never lived in. On side trips the player walks streets built for children who never came. The tone here is awe, not sorrow.


## 9. Buried truths and how the player finds them

Each truth is optional depth unless marked as main.

1. **The Varela wreck is 11,000 years old (main, supports Twist 5).** Teo can read the old light-writing on its hull: a near-rings warship pattern from the Long Argument. The Glass no longer build that pattern.
2. **Varela caused first contact.** The 2330 survey records the Admiral asks for show the find. The Ledger ship's first human-era entry, in 2338, is a Glass ship coming to look for a waking warship.
3. **The *Calabar* fired to hide the wreck.** The Navy memorial lists the captain's name, Inês Varela. The Skirmish logs show the Glass were searching the exact asteroid that became Dock Zero.
4. **The inspection clause targets Varela.** The player can read it in the treaty text. Eight Bells' interest in Dez's smuggling cases is really interest in what moves through the belt.
5. **The passage debt was never cancelled.** Freight bills on any dock show the Settlement toll, and the toll is paid to the bondholders. Rui knows, because he pays it.
6. **The early echo.** Navy monitor logs from 2352 show a signal arriving before it was sent. This shows the Glass were right to hurry.
7. **The Pilot Law explains Sabi.** Sabi could not legally warn anyone or act on its forecast. Building the Envoys was the only way it could act at all.
8. **The Long Argument and the Empty Worlds.** The player pieces these together from Ledger-ship walls, Glass rooms and the Empty Worlds, once Teo's lessons let them read light.
9. **The builders fell the same way.** On an Empty World, the player finds a Glass record made at the end of the Argument comparing Glass cities to the builders' cities, the one recording the Glass ever allowed outside the Ledger. It reads: "We are the second."
10. **Humans are on the same road.** The Rearguard's future is the third people beginning the Argument. Ending B shows it.

## 10. Is the time travel new enough?

The owner's new rule says time travel stays only if it adds something new. An honest answer: the mechanics alone are not new. Signals sent back appear in Steins;Gate and the novel *Timescape*. Minds sent back into other bodies are the premise of the TV show *Travelers*. Rival factions from different futures appear in *This Is How You Lose the Time War*, *Dark* and *Terminator*. "You can't go back before the machine was switched on" comes from *Primer* and Steins;Gate. A reader who knows the genre will recognise each rule.

What this world adds is three uses, not three rules:

1. **True foresight decays, so the perfect prophet is the fake.** Most time stories treat future knowledge as a fixed trump card. Here it is a perishable good, like news in a lag economy, and its decay is the lie detector. The team that is always right must be a forecaster working from today. That twist cannot happen in a story where future knowledge holds steady.
2. **One of the two futures isn't time travel at all.** The rival future is an AI's forecast dressed up as a memory. The story's real contest is prophecy against prediction, and the player learns to tell them apart.
3. **The cure for time war is a public record.** The Glass beat their own time war by letting everyone read every message, so that no message gives an edge. The story asks whether humans will accept that. This answer to the paradox problem is the world's own.

That is enough to keep time travel, in my view, but only if the story leans on these three uses and keeps the rules plain. If the owner wants a mechanic that is new in itself, the two best alternatives are in section 11.

## 11. Suggested changes to the story

These are for the owner to approve. `exploration.md` is unchanged.

**A. Give Tomás a sharper motive: the inspection clause.** The treaty lets Glass inspectors into any yard that builds ships for ring travel. That would expose Dock Zero. It explains why Tomás must kill the treaty now, before signing, and why in the honest future he waited eight years while lawyers stalled the inspection. Slow freight stays as his public motive.

**B. Make the silent ship an 11,000-year-old wreck from the Long Argument, found beside the Meridian ring itself.** "An empty system" becomes the Meridian belt. This removes the question of why the Glass never came looking for a lost ship: they did, which is the Skirmish. It also means Varela's yards sit next to the station for a reason, and Teo can read the hull's age.

**C. Varela's drive tests caused first contact, and the *Calabar* fired in 2341 to hide the wreck.** This gives "first peace treaty" a real earlier fight, and it plants Twist 5 decades deep. The Varelas have started a fight with the Glass to hide this ship once already.

**D. Show the lag in hour one.** Post day, "What news?", the six-day clock beside Lagos time, and traders calling a forecast "stale". Rule 2 then feels like common sense rather than a rule to memorise, and Twist 1 lands harder. The Admiral's "Every day I'm here, I know less" becomes a sailor's proverb she gives a new meaning to.

**E. Make sending an old custom.** Two centuries ago, colonies radioed people's minds to each other and grew them in vats. The station then treats the Rearguard's arrival as strange but familiar, and the player learns Rule 1 from how calmly the vat doctors handle it. Add one line: a genome is just data, so DNA proves nothing.

**F. Add the Pilot Law.** AIs may forecast but never act or hide a forecast. This explains why Sabi works in secret and gives ending A's shutdown a legal basis. It also makes Sabi's crime clear without making it evil.

**G. Make the Ledger shared, not secret.** Every Glass world reads it, readers swear not to act on it, and the treaty would give humans a seat. Eight Bells' "one voice, not two" then breaks a real law in a small way, which tells the player how much it cares. Ending C gains weight: humans confessing is the Ledger idea in action.

**H. Clarify the Empty Worlds.** The Glass emptied them by ceasing to have children while no future held still, not through slaughter. This matches the tone rule and makes the Argument's danger follow from Rule 2.

**I. Add the lit-ring limit to Rule 2's explanation.** A signal can only come back to a time when the ring was lit, which means never before 2338. This stops players asking why nobody went back further, and explains the Rearguard's choice of date.

**J. A deep wow for the endgame or a sequel: "We are the second."** The builders' empty cities and the Glass Empty Worlds are the same story. Humans are about to be the third. This is the iceberg's bottom, and it answers why the galaxy is so quiet.

### Two alternative premises, if time travel goes

Seven premises keep the core appeal (two rival sources of impossible knowledge, each calling the other a fake) without time travel:

1. Two backups of an assassinated envoy's mind wake in two vats, each saying the other was tampered with.
2. The station AI forks in two; one half says make peace, the other says strike first, and each says the other has been hacked by the aliens.
3. Two expeditions fly out ahead of old starlight and film the past: each brings back footage of who started the old war, and the two films disagree.
4. Two clones of a dead general each claim to be the true copy and give opposite orders.
5. Two Glass crystal records of the Long Argument, each blaming the other side.
6. Two rival forecasting AIs, one human and one Glass, each predicting the other side will strike.
7. A Glass "living memory" bonded to two different humans, each remembering a different truth.

I graded them on novelty, simplicity and fit with this world. Numbers 4 and 1 lean on Pitch 5 and on *Altered Carbon*. Numbers 5 and 7 bury the frame in alien lore. Number 6 is not impossible knowledge. The best two:

**Alternative 1: Old light.** One rule: ships outrun light, so if you fly far enough out, you can watch any past event in its old light. This fits the lag world perfectly, since the same fact that makes news slow makes the past visible. Frame: "Two expeditions return with film of what started the war; one says the aliens fired first, the other says humans did, and a spy has thirty days to find out whose film is fake." Gains: a mechanic few stories have used for a mystery, new scenes (flying out to the right distance to watch a crime unfold, racing a rival ship to the same patch of old light) and the same lie-detector logic, because real old light has real limits (blur, angle, blocked views) while a fake is too clean. Losses: the older-self scenes, Nneka's arc as a daughter from a future, and the "every change rewrites the future" tension. The Admiral would become an old veteran instead.

**Alternative 2: The split pilot.** One rule: an AI copied into two cores becomes two minds that each believe they are the original. Sabi is split during a Gate surge; one Sabi says sign, one says strike, and each says the other was tampered with by the Glass. Gains: the AGI pillar becomes the whole engine, the player's partner becomes the mystery, and the "always right" twist survives (the copy with fresh data stays perfect; the one cut off goes stale). Losses: the human faces of the two sides, the older selves, the second vat scene, and a lot of the wide political scale, which gets harder to stage when both prophets are voices in the station's walls.

My recommendation is to keep time travel, framed around the three uses in section 10, and to keep Old light as the reserve. If the owner wants a different mechanic, Old light keeps the most of this world.
