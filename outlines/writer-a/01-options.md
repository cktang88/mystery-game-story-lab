# Writer A, file 01: option rounds

Starting point: **question first**. Every round below lists at least three genuinely different options, judges them against the brief (BRIEF.md) and the report's principles (REPORT.md), picks one and says why. Where my first idea was the obvious one, it is listed and rejected as "the average" so the record shows it was considered.

Working title of the result: **The Long Word** (the island's oldest prayer, and the last line of the game).

---

## Round 1. Central question (process step 1)

The test: a question, not an answer; more than one defensible answer; AGI, aliens, cloning and time travel must each test it differently; I must know which side the ending takes.

| # | Question | How the four pillars test it | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Q0 (the average) | What makes a self continuous: memory, body or choice? | Clone = body, AGI = memory, time = choice... | Rejected. It is the research note's own example and the default question of every clone and upload story. It produces philosophy, not scenes, and it points straight at the "you are the clone" reveal. |
| Q1 | **Does a promise outlive the one who made it?** | Clone: is a copy bound by her original's oath? AGI: is today's version bound by what an earlier version swore? Time: are you bound by words your future self has already spoken into the past? Alien: is a child bound by its dead parent's promise? | **Picked.** |
| Q2 | Can a witness who cannot lie still deceive? | AGI that cannot lie but is sealed; clones giving identical testimony; voices heard before they are spoken; an alien that only quotes. | Rejected as the spine, kept as a mechanic. It is mystery-genre meta: it would make a clever puzzle box but the emotional stakes would be about evidence, not people. Its best idea (a truthful machine that can only count truths) survives as the Count mechanic. |
| Q3 | Who has the right to know the future? | AGI forecasting, alien-granted foresight, clones raised for a planned role, messages from ahead. | Rejected. It drifts into a policy debate about precaution and inequality, which invites thesis speeches; it also makes two pillars (AGI, time) do the same job. Its world-scale stake (the world living off foresight) survives as Act IV's stake. |
| Q4 | What do we owe the people made to replace someone? | Substitutes everywhere: copies, a machine standing in, a future self, an alien heir. | Rejected as too close to Q1 and narrower: it is Q1 seen only from the replacement's side. It survives inside Q1 as the anchor's personal question. |

**Why Q1.** A promise is a concrete, stageable object (an oath, a debt, a vow, a daily ritual), so the question can be carried by actions and things rather than speeches. It has real defenders on every side: the island's economy and the alien's whole nature say "yes, forever"; the clones' movement says "no, never"; the machine says "as long as the record does". It is native to a mystery (who promised what, to whom, when, and who inherited it is an investigable chain) and native to an estate-settling detective. Each pillar answers it differently, and the four together raise a question none raises alone: **if a promise can be kept by a copy, voiced by a machine, already fulfilled in the future, and owed to a being that cannot tell its keepers apart, who exactly is bound?**

**The side the ending takes:** a promise outlives its maker only in someone who chooses to keep it; whoever keeps it becomes its owner, and the owner also holds the right to end it. (Egri's rule: take a side. Truby's rule: argue it through action, never a speech.)

## Round 2a. Hidden history (process step 2)

| # | Hidden history | Strengths | Weaknesses | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| H1 | **The Covenant and the Son.** In 946 a settler woman, Steinunn, swore a friendship pact with a stranded alien: she would speak with it every day she lived; it would carry the island's words back one lunar node cycle (18.6 years). She set the term herself: when she died it was free to go home. In 983, as she went to release it, her own son drowned her to keep the island's foresight, then had the alien "heal" her by growing a copy from her blood. For 1,060 years the island has regrown the murdered woman to keep the alien bound, and regrown the murderer to rule. | A founding crime fully known; every faction descends from it; all four pillars are load-bearing (alien makes the copies and the time channel; AGI later holds the sealed record); the present murder rhymes with the founding one. | Large; needs strict rules to stay readable. | **Picked.** |
| H2 | **Descendants return.** The aliens are our far-future descendants who came back through time; the AGI is their emissary; clones are their attempt to repopulate. | Big cosmic frame. | Stacks three stock reveals (aliens are human, the traveller caused it, the AI was guiding us). Answers are smaller than the questions once known. | Rejected. |
| H3 | **The machine that made itself.** An AGI built in 2024 sent instructions back through a time channel to guarantee its own creation, cloning its designers to stay in control; the "aliens" are its cover story. | Tight bootstrap loop; strong villain. | Machine-god final boss, evil AI with thin motive, and a fake-alien deflation; the alien pillar becomes decoration. | Rejected. |
| H4 | **The hoax.** There is no time channel: an island elite fakes "voices from the future" with an AGI, clones people to act out the prophecies, and invented the alien as theology. | Grounded; every miracle is a con. | Deflationary: the answer is smaller than the question and the brief asks for "insane underneath". Two pillars become fake. | Rejected, but its best idea survives: the ruling House's craft of "Fitting", making received prophecies come true in harmless ways. |

**Why H1.** It is insane underneath (a murdered woman regrown twenty times by copies of her own killer) but every step has a mundane motive: a son who fears famine. It is a Halo-style founding crime rather than a founding prophecy, so it explains why every present faction behaves as it does. It also hides a deeper irony the player can only find at the end: the son misunderstood how the alien recognises its keeper, so the thousand years of copies were never necessary.

## Round 2b. Ending

The ending is constrained by the opening: at the first window of the 2043 Standing, the Horn receives **total silence, not even the sea**, which in a fixed timeline means the channel will not exist in 2061. So any ending in which the alien stays bound is impossible. That is deliberate: the player can deduce mid-game that the release will happen, and the question becomes who does it and what it costs (suspense over surprise, per Brewer and Lichtenstein).

| # | Ending | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| E1 | **The keeper's release.** The substitute Oath-Keeper, who is not a copy at all, is recognised by the alien because she has kept the promise every day for ten years, and she releases it in her own voice. The island and the world lose foresight forever; the alien takes with it every stored voice of the island's dead; the probate clerk closes the 1,060-year-old estate. A quiet beat: a tape of her dead mother hearing her. The player chooses the words she sent her mother. | **Picked.** Completes the first-hour rule ("No estate is closed while a promise stands open") literally; the win and the cost are one act; the final small decision is left to the player. |
| E2 | **The clerk inherits.** The clerk takes the oath herself, consenting to keep the alien bound for the world's sake while freeing the girl. | Rejected: impossible under the opening silence, keeps the victim enslaved, and makes the protagonist a martyr. |
| E3 | **The alien dies.** The House or the world tries to seize the newborn and kills it. Silence by death. | Rejected: the answer is smaller than the question, the release theme is lost, and it is a tragedy without a choice. |
| E4 | **The vote.** The island Thing votes to release it. | Rejected: summation-gathering resolution, no personal cost, no single character's act. Kept as a beat: the Thing does vote, on the Law of Same, a turn earlier. |

## Round 3. World rules per pillar (process step 3)

Each pillar gets one mechanism, one memorable cap, one visible cost, one relationship it damages and one it alone can save, and one investigative verb no other pillar produces.

### Time travel

| # | Rule | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| T1 | **The Horn.** During the slack of a spring tide in a Standing year (the lunar major standstill, every 18.6 years), sound spoken into the alien's Horn arrives in the same mouth of the Horn exactly 6,798 days earlier. Sound only. One step only (relays possible by re-speaking). The cost: the Horn takes the words, so nobody in the sender's present hears or records them; the sender is the only witness to what they sent. | **Picked.** |
| T2 | A reset loop: the Standing year repeats for one person until a promise is kept. | Rejected: reset button, invalidates evidence, and is the most familiar game shape. |
| T3 | Bodily travel as a voice: a person who dies in the Horn exists in the past for one tide as a speaking ghost. | Rejected: makes death a commute and is too rare to generate many scenes. Kept as island folklore only, never confirmed (texture). |
| T4 | Overlapping doubles: a traveller arrives as a second body. | Rejected: collides with cloning and blurs the verbs. |

**Why T1.** The interval is not arbitrary: it is the real lunar nodal cycle that governs the island's extreme tides, which grounds it in the setting. A fixed timeline means evidence can never be invalidated. It creates scenes nothing else can: characters hearing their own future words years early; a neighbour's mouth moving with no sound coming out; a whole government holding the "script" of the present, received 18 years ago. Damages: trust (nobody can verify what a sender sent). Saves: the goodbye to someone who has since died (they heard it, years ago). Verb: **tide-matching**, aligning the received archive against the unfolding present to predict who will be where.

### AGI

| # | Rule | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| A1 | **The Thul.** The island's notary mind, built in 2024 to replace the human law-speaker, now the world's treaty notary. It cannot state a falsehood about its record. Each year's version is frozen as a "sitting" and can be woken to testify as of its date. Records can be sealed under oath; a sealed mind can still lawfully **count** how many of a set of statements are true, because a count is not testimony. Each waking is publicly logged. | **Picked.** |
| A2 | A collective of uploaded islanders' minds. | Rejected: duplicates cloning's identity question and is the "human in a shell" cliché. |
| A3 | An oracle AI that simulates the future. | Rejected: does the time pillar's job, so neither pillar is distinct. |

**Why A1.** It is neither threat nor guardian but a participant with limits and its own unfinished goal (one sentence: *it wants every promise in its keeping kept or lawfully released, and the oldest is neither*). Its limits generate the game's validation mechanic diegetically: batch confirmation exists because a sealed machine may count truths but not speak them. Its verb is unique: **waking sittings**, interrogating a mind's past versions and diffing their answers. Damages: confiding (the island stopped saying private things near it). Saves: a dead woman's last request, kept across eighteen versions.

### Cloning

| # | Rule | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| C1 | **Aftergrowth.** The alien's Pool can grow a newborn from a person's blood. No memories pass. Copies age normally. Only one living copy of a pattern at a time: the Pool will not grow what is already walking. By island law (the Law of Same, 1883) a copy *is* the original: same name, same debts, same marriage, same crimes. | **Picked.** |
| C2 | Adult copies imprinted with memories from the alien's sound store. | Rejected: memory transfer makes "you are the clone" central and blurs with AGI. |
| C3 | Copies grown in the past from patterns sent back through time. | Rejected: collapses two pillars into one mechanism. |

**Why C1.** "One at a time" is a memorable cap that creates plots by itself (a copy can only be grown after the previous one dies, so the island must hide gaps; a living copy in hiding blocks any replacement). The Law of Same gives clones legal standing and absurd, painful consequences (a 31-year-old fisherman legally married to his original's 71-year-old widow). Copy status is disclosed early, Never Let Me Go style; the story spends itself on what copies owe. Verb: **lineage reading**, telling identical people apart by what they acquired (accent, teeth, habits) plus **veil-swap stealth** among identical veiled figures. Damages: the bond between a copy and the original's family. Saves: the same bond when someone chooses it freely.

### Aliens

| # | Rule | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| X1 | **The Bergmál.** A single stranded being grown into a sea cave; its body is the Horn (time channel), the Pool (where it buds and grows copies) and the Store (it keeps every sound made in the cave for its whole life). It cannot make new sounds: it speaks only by replaying stored ones, so every sentence it utters is a collage of quotations from the island's dead. Its kind cannot act against a promise, and a promise passes to its bud. It knows its keeper not by face or voice but by the word kept: whoever comes daily and keeps the promise is, to it, the one who made it. | **Picked.** |
| X2 | A microbial mind in the geothermal vents that changes human perception. | Rejected: soft, atmospheric, cannot carry rules a mystery can use. |
| X3 | An alien ship in orbit demanding a treaty-voice from humanity. | Rejected: galaxy-scale opening, invasion-adjacent, no small human hook. |

**Why X1.** Its want fits one sentence and is not about Earth: *it wants to go home, and believes it may only go when the woman it promised lets it.* Its difference is the point (it never wants to be human). Its verb is unique: **collage conversation**, understanding it by attributing each quoted fragment to its speaker and era, and answering it by citing promises. That verb is also the iceberg's delivery system: lore arrives as the voices of biased dead islanders, never as a codex. Damages: the island's freedom (its foresight became a dependency). Saves: the living's bond with their dead (the Store lets you hear them), and the release ends that.

### Collision check

- Alien x cloning: copies exist only because the alien was believed to recognise its keeper by voice and body.
- Alien x time: the time channel is the alien's body; freeing it ends time travel forever.
- AGI x cloning: the Thul voice-trained the substitute and notarised her false certificate.
- AGI x alien: for eight years the Thul itself spoke to the alien in the dead woman's voice, and the alien counted it as her.
- Clone x time: a voice in the received archive could be any copy, so attribution needs acquired traits.
- AGI x time: the Thul's sittings are a mind's own past selves, testifying like witnesses from earlier years.

## Round 4. Founding crime (process step 4)

| # | Founding crime or lie | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| F1 | **The Son's murder and the rewritten covenant.** Steinunn's son drowned her to stop the release, grew her again, and rewrote the covenant as a servitude ("while the swearer lives, the Hearer serves"). | **Picked.** |
| F2 | **The stolen finding.** Steinunn never found the alien; a Gaelic thrall girl did, and Steinunn swore under the thrall's name, so the covenant rests on identity theft and the Unheard are the true heirs. | Rejected as the spine (it would add a second identity reveal), kept as **texture**: the Unheard folk account says a thrall girl heard the cave first. The saga and the folk account disagree and the game never settles it. |
| F3 | **The shooting-down.** Settlers wrecked the alien's vessel and invented the covenant to explain its captivity. | Rejected: crude, and makes the founders cartoon villains rather than frightened people. |

**Why F1.** It gives every present faction a rational reason to act before the reveal: the ruling House protects a thousand-year arrangement it believes the island cannot survive without; the families who hold sending rights are rich on it; the Unheard were excluded from it; the Law of Same exists to protect it; the Thul was built partly to hold its sealed record; the world's treaty body quietly lives on its foresight. Uncovering it is both the lore payoff and the plot twist.

## Round 5. Setting and naming logic

| # | Setting | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| S1 | **Eiðey**, a fictional North Atlantic island republic settled in the 10th century by Norse settlers and their Gaelic thralls; present day 2043, a Standing year. Matronymic names (everyone is "daughter/son of" their mother, a custom traced to Steinunn, "Mother of the Island"); Old Norse terms for institutions; Gaelic-derived names among the Unheard (thrall descendants). | **Picked.** |
| S2 | A Mediterranean notarial island modelled on centuries-old notarial archives. | Rejected: strong on contracts, weak on oath-feud and inheritance culture; no tidal logic for the time channel. |
| S3 | A rural Japanese town with a family-register system. | Rejected: the register is a superb identity device, but the setting pushes the story toward the anime models it is meant to outgrow. |

**Why S1.** The saga world is literally about inherited oaths, feuds and wergild, so the central question is native to it. The law-speaker who recited the law from memory is a real historical office, which grounds the AGI. Tides and the lunar node ground the time channel. Matronymics make the naming logic a clue system: a copy keeps her original's name, so **Steinunn Steinunnardóttir** ("Steinunn, daughter of Steinunn") hides a copy in plain sight, just as an ordinary "X, son of X" might. The island is fictional, so no real culture is credited with alien achievements.

|---|---|---|

## Round 7. One-sentence premise (process step 6)

Rule: under fifteen words, no names, states what the protagonist stands to lose, names the dilemma-making mechanic.

| # | Premise | Words | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | A clerk must close a thousand-year oath kept alive by copies and future voices. | 14 | Good mechanic, no personal loss. |
| 2 | Settling a drowned stranger's estate, a clerk inherits a promise the future already kept. | 14 | Intriguing, but the loss is invisible. |
| 3 | **A probate clerk can save a counterfeit clone only by ending her island's foresight.** | 14 | **Picked.** |
| 4 | On an island that hears its future, a clerk must free its enslaved oracle. | 14 | Rejected: "oracle" is generic and there is no person at stake. |

**Why 3.** It names two pillars as a dilemma (a person versus foresight), states what is lost either way, and contains the hook ("counterfeit clone": a clone who is not one). The Pacific Rim test: the core idea of a promise that passes to whoever keeps it, carried by voices into the past, generates scenes no other story could (hearing your own future words; a machine that can only count truths; a creature that speaks only in quotations of your dead).
