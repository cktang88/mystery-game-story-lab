# Writer C: option rounds (ending first)

Working title: **LANDUNTER** ("land under": the North Frisian word for the tide that covers a Hallig until only the house mounds stand above the sea).

Starting point: the final emotional image. Following Yoko Taro's "backwards scriptwriting" and Uchikoshi's ending-first practice (REPORT, "Professionals lock the answers first"), I fixed the last picture first, refined what makes it hurt, then derived the question, the hidden history that makes the picture inevitable, the rules that make the history possible, and the clues that make it fair. The rounds below are in the order I actually ran them, so Round 0 (the image) comes before the central question.

Every round has three genuinely different options, a judgement against the brief and the report's checklist, a pick and the reason. Where my first idea was the obvious one, I say so and say why it lost or survived.

---

## Round 0. The final emotional image (three options)

**Option A: "The returned bell."** The whole island stands on the mudflats at the last low tide. Every person who ever crossed through the alien ring glows faintly; the dead in the churchyard glow too. The ring dies, the lights go out one by one, and the protagonist's own light goes out last, revealing that she crossed as a baby and never knew.
- For: huge, visual, wordless.
- Against: the last beat is "the protagonist was secretly X", the single most catalogued identity twist (TV Tropes, "you are the clone/the traveller"). It is spectacle plus a stock reveal. Answer is smaller than the question.

**Option B: "The empty plate."** The protagonist steps into the ring so that a drowned child can come forward from the past. Final image: a soaked child gasping on the flats in the present, wrapped in the protagonist's coat; the protagonist is gone into the past for good.
- For: a clean cost, a life for a life.
- Against: this is the "self-erasure to close the loop" trope the table flags, and it makes the hero's absence the ending. The brief wants a human anchor kept throughout; this removes the anchor at the end.

**Option C: "The unrecorded kitchen."** A daughter walks a frightened eighteen-year-old girl, whom she has come to love like a sister, into the sea on the night of the last crossing and lets her go nineteen years into the past. Minutes later she climbs back to her house on its mound while the flood rises around it, and the same girl is waiting at the kitchen table, thirty-seven years old, having spent nineteen years raising her. Across the table sits a stranger with the same face, eighteen years old, the woman whose life that was, just arrived from the other end of the same night. The machine that has recorded every second of the daughter's life says: "I'll close my eyes now. Just for a bit." The player decides whether to let it. If they do, the scene we never hear is the first moment in forty years that nobody can replay and nobody foresaw.
- For: the goodbye and the reunion are the same minute, which only this premise can produce. It keeps the anchor (the daughter) in the last frame. The win (the last crossing done freely), the cost (losing the girl) and a small thing left for the player (whether to watch) are one act. It completes a first-hour line literally. Nothing in it is a thesis speech.
- Against: risks being quiet after a large climax. Fixed by placing a full action climax (the free hour after the last crossing) before it, and by making the player's choice to watch or not carry an in-world stake (see Round 9).

**Pick: C.** It is the only option where the emotional payload depends on all four pillars at once, and the only one that is not a catalogued twist. Refinement (Yoko Taro's "what makes it sad"): she is eighteen; she was raised in a bird hide and has never had a home; she calls being sent "going home"; the daughter is the elder; the kitchen sits on a mound in a rising flood; the machine has never once looked away of its own accord. Each of those details is then planted backward through the timeline (see 02 and 05).

## Round 1. Central question (three options)

**Q1. "Who does a life belong to: the one it was given to, or the one who lived it?"**
**Q2. "If the past cannot change, what is still ours to choose?"**
**Q3. "Do we owe a debt we did not incur?"**

Test (REPORT step 1): more than one defensible answer, and the writers know which side the ending takes.

- Q1 runs as a different experiment through each pillar. Time travel: a girl is given a life and loses nineteen years of it in one second. Cloning: a copy lives a life that was given to someone else. AGI: a machine holds the complete record of everyone's life; does the record own it? Alien: the ring uses human lives to carry its own. Defensible both ways (the original was robbed; the copy earned it). The ending sides with the one who lived it, while refusing to dismiss the one it was given to (the original is not punished, and the kitchen conversation is hers too).
- Q2 is the mechanical corollary of a fixed timeline, and it gives the AGI and the ending their meaning (what nobody witnessed is free). But alone it is abstract, closer to a physics puzzle than a human question.
- Q3 is the founding crime's question (the German word *Schuld* means both debt and guilt). Strong for the iceberg, but as the whole story's question it points at institutions more than at the mother and daughter.

**Pick: Q1**, with Q2 as its sub-question and Q3 as the iceberg's question. The ending's answer: a life belongs to the one who lived it, and the part of it nobody witnessed belongs to no one else at all. The three questions are not competitors; they are the personal, mechanical and historical faces of the same story, and the outline keeps Q1 in front.

## Round 2. Setting and naming culture (three options)

Names must come from a specific culture and logic inside the world (brief, "Fighting the average"), so the setting had to be chosen before the cast.

**S1. A North Frisian Hallig in the Wadden Sea, 2047.** Tiny unprotected islands whose houses stand on artificial mounds (*Warften*); the sea covers everything else several times a year (*Landunter*). Mudflats walkable at low tide. Whaling-era wealth (18th-century island commanders) recorded on "speaking gravestones" that carve a life story into stone. Names: North Frisian and Low German (Inken, Moiken, Keike, Marret, Kerrin, Tade, Arfst, Ocke, Nommen; patronymic surnames like Brarens, Ketelsen, Nommensen). Naming custom: a child's name "comes back" from a grandparent.
**S2. An Akan lagoon town on the Ghanaian coast, 2047.** Day names (Kwame, Ama, Kofi, Akua) encode the weekday of birth, a ready-made identity clue for clones and travellers.
**S3. A Seto Inland Sea salt town, 2047.** The family register (*koseki*) records every person; a time traveller or clone breaks the register.

Judgement:
- S2 gives the cleverest single clue (a clone born on a different weekday), but one clue is not a world, and I would be writing a culture I can only render from outside, with high risk of decoration.
- S3 gives an institution (the register) but no landscape mechanic.
- S1 gives a landscape that is itself a game system: the tide is a clock, the flats are traversable only for hours, the houses become islands, birds on the flats are a natural alarm system, sea fog is a stealth layer. It gives an iceberg object (speaking gravestones), a historical economy (whaling commanders) that can carry a founding crime, a naming logic, and a folk tradition of drowned churches and changelings that can grow out of the alien rather than be pasted on. It is almost unused in games.

**Pick: S1.** Risk logged: a German small-community time story invites comparison with *Dark* (see Round 12 and 07-self-check); mitigations are recorded there.

## Round 3. Hidden history (three options)

All three keep the ending image of Round 0 possible.

**H1. "She raised the girl who became her."** In 2028 an eighteen-year-old island mother, Inken, ran into the alien ring to drag her boyfriend out of a state program that was about to trade him into the future. On the same tide, from 2047, two people were sent back: a state agent, and Inken's teenage clone. The agent swapped with the boyfriend; the clone swapped with Inken. The clone walked home and lived Inken's life, raised Inken's baby daughter, and, because clones grow at human speed, made and secretly raised her own younger self for eighteen years so that the girl could be sent back on the 2047 tide. The daughter, now nineteen, is the one who walks her.
**H2. "She ran ahead."** In 2028 Inken chose to be pulled into the future to escape the island, and the state, needing to hide a breach, installed a clone to replace her. The clone is a state creation who slowly becomes a real mother. In 2047 the daughter must decide whether to welcome back the woman who abandoned her.
**H3. "The arrival is the copy."** Inversion: the woman who raised the daughter is the original, and the girl who arrives in 2047 is a state clone sent to replace her.

Judgement:
- H3 is a flipped expectation and nothing more; flipping is itself a catalogued move (overused_tropes note, section on subversion), and it makes the arrival a villain's tool rather than a person.
- H2 is morally interesting but makes the original's choice selfish and the state the clone's author, so the clone's love is a malfunction of a state plan. The daughter's role is passive (judge).
- H1 makes every person's action a choice inside a fixed timeline: Inken chose to save someone; the clone chose to make and raise the girl she had been; the daughter chooses how the sending happens. It requires all four pillars (the ring to swap, the no-shortcut cloning rule to force eighteen years of raising, the AGI's blind spot to hide the swap, the alien's rules to set weight and once-only). It also produces the ending image without contrivance.

**Pick: H1.** Full sealed chronology in 02-hidden-history.md.

## Round 4. The ending: events (three options for how the last night resolves)

The image is fixed; these are three versions of the events that lead into it. Following the report, I fix meaning more firmly than events.

**E1. The state wins the night.** The girl is sedated and placed in the ring by the state, as the state's "debt doctrine" requires. The daughter can only watch. The kitchen happens anyway.
**E2. The daughter breaks the loop.** She refuses to send the girl; the timeline is somehow rewritten.
**E3. Fixed facts, free meaning, then a free hour.** The sending must happen (the mother remembers it), so the daughter's task is to make it happen as a choice: retrieve the girl from the state and walk her, so that it is a homecoming and not a deportation. Then the timeline runs out: past the moment of the last crossing, nobody alive has any memory or record of what happens next. The real climax is fought in that free hour (the alien ring's death, the state's attempt to cut out its heart, the machine's right to publish the record).

Judgement: E2 breaks the one time rule (the fixed timeline) that the whole mystery depends on; a mystery game needs evidence that can never be invalidated (REPORT, "Choose one time model"). E1 removes agency at the climax. E3 keeps the rules and puts real uncertainty after the fixed point, which the player can feel because the interface's "known timeline" bar literally ends there.

**Pick: E3.**

## Round 5. World rules per pillar (three options each)

Each pillar needs one mechanism, one memorable hard limit, one visible cost, and a verb no other pillar provides.

### 5a. Time travel

**T1. The Waag (weighing): weight for a weight, nineteen years, once.** On the lowest equinox tides, a living body standing in the alien ring is exchanged with the nearest living body of the same weight (within two kilograms) that stood in the ring on the same night nineteen years earlier. Clothes and what you hold go with you. A body that has crossed can never cross again.
**T2. The Echo.** The ring replays sound from exactly nineteen years ago; nothing travels, only sound. Fixed and safe, but it is a sensor, not travel; no human cost, no dilemma.
**T3. Mind-leap.** A consciousness jumps back into its own body days earlier. Directly Steins;Gate's territory and Higurashi's; derivative.

**Pick: T1.** It generates scenes nobody else has: to send someone back you must take someone else forward, so every journey has a stranger's price; people weigh themselves before a crossing; an agent's selection depends on his weight; a girl must be fed to exactly fifty-six kilograms. The nineteen years are the Metonic cycle (the moon returns to the same phase on the same date every nineteen years), which gives the rule a one-line folk explanation islanders already half-know: "the moon sits where it sat". Cap: nineteen years exactly, equinox nights only, once per body. Cost: the stranger taken from the past, and the nineteen years lost by whoever comes forward.

### 5b. Cloning

**C1. No shortcuts.** Cloning is a standard procedure (legal in Denmark since the 2030s, banned in Germany, but copies born abroad have had personhood here since a 2041 ruling). A clone is a baby. It grows at human speed, with its own fingerprints, scars, teeth and tattoos. Same genome; different body history.
**C2. Vat-grown adults.** Accelerated growth produces adult bodies with blank minds. Allows instant replacements, but produces the "disposable clone" trope and kills the clock.
**C3. Memory-carrying copies.** A copy wakes with the original's memories. That is SOMA's and many others' territory and collapses identity questions into a mind-upload story.

**Pick: C1.** The hard limit "a clone takes eighteen years to make an eighteen-year-old" is the engine of the hidden history: it is why someone had to raise the girl, and it turns a nineteen-year time offset into a nineteen-year act of care. Cost: the copy owns no past; the people who loved the original see a stranger in the right face. Verb: identity attribution by body history (fingerprints, scars, tattoo quality, dental work), because DNA cannot tell them apart.

### 5c. AGI

**A1. The Schriever (scrivener): it cannot lie; it can only look away.** A state archive mind built in 2016 to witness the island and keep the crossing ledger. Its record is append-only and cryptographically chained: nothing can be edited, only not recorded. It answers truthfully what it is asked and volunteers nothing. Every equinox its state is archived, and any old version can be booted and questioned about what it knew then. It senses the island through cameras, microphones, hydrophones and the load sensors under every floor (installed as flood sensors), so it literally knows everyone's weight.
**A2. The Forecaster.** A predictive mind that simulates people to anticipate the future. Machine-god risk; overlaps the time pillar's job.
**A3. The Swarm.** An intelligence distributed across islanders' devices, with no centre. Interesting, but its verbs (crowd testimony) overlap ordinary witnesses.

**Pick: A1.** Its rule is the cleanest "unreliable instrument" in the report's sense: it never shows a false fact, only true facts with a gap the player must learn to question. Hard limit: it cannot record what it did not sense. Cost: no one on the island is ever unwatched, and everything it sees belongs to the state. Verbs only it provides: **deposition** (boot and cross-examine its own past versions) and **weighing the past** (replay a scene as load data, so a guest who was never seen still leaves fifty-six kilograms on a bed).

### 5d. Aliens

**X1. The Waag itself.** The ring is one organism that fell into the marsh in a medieval storm-tide: forty dark posts that are not wood, thick in time rather than in space (it exists at both ends of a nineteen-year span at once). Each crossing seeds a dormant spore in the body that crossed; under ultraviolet light, crossed bodies glow. Each crossing makes the ring hum at a pitch set by the weight that crossed. Its want: **to have its seed carried out of the marsh in bodies that walk away.** It is old, and in 2047 it fruits and dies.
**X2. A signalling civilisation.** Distant aliens who answer human signals with a nineteen-year delay. Their messages make a time channel. Generates messages, not scenes; and its motive drifts toward "contact with humanity", a stock want.
**X3. A mind that wears clones.** An alien intelligence that inhabits cloned bodies. Pulls cloning into body-snatcher territory, and its want is about humans.

**Pick: X1.** Its want has nothing to do with humanity (we are pollinators, not targets) and it passes the checklist test: one sentence, not conquest, not becoming human. It supplies two verbs no other pillar does: **listening** (the hum's pitch gives the weight of whoever crossed and the count of crossings, which turns an old woman's hobby recordings into hard evidence) and **reading the glow** (who has crossed, alive or dead). And its natural death explains the "Silence" (Round 7) without a villain.

### 5e. How the four collide (the question none raises alone)

- Time × cloning: because the past already received a clone, someone in the present must *make and raise* her; the past creates an obligation to grow a person.
- Time × AGI: an append-only record of a fixed timeline means the only freedom is in what was never recorded; the machine's one blind spot is the only place a choice could hide.
- Time × alien: the alien's death sets the end of crossings, so the fixed timeline has a horizon after which nobody knows anything.
- Cloning × alien: the ring ignores genomes and weighs bodies, so a clone may cross even though her genome already has; the alien's verdict on identity is "a body is a life, not a code".
- Cloning × AGI: the machine forks itself to raise the clone in secret, and the fork, a copy of a mind, becomes a parent.
- AGI × alien: the machine's charter releases the record "after the last crossing", which only the alien's death can certify.

Together they raise: *if the past is fixed and recorded, who owns a life?*, which none raises alone.

## Round 6. Founding crime (three options)

**F1. The Wedderkinner trade, 1712–1806.** A whaling commander discovered the weighing rule. For ninety-four years the island's commander families bought poor teenagers from mainland poorhouses, stood them in the ring on equinox nights, and sent their own sons back nineteen years carrying knowledge of which whaling grounds would be rich and which ships would sink. The bought children were pulled forward among strangers, called *Wedderkinner* ("children who came again"), declared soulless by the island's pastor ("the Waag takes the soulless and returns the blessed"), and indentured to Greenland whalers. The commanders' speaking gravestones still boast that "God showed him where the whales lay". The family ledger of every purchase is the *Schuldbuch* (debt book).
**F2. The drowned parish.** In the storm-tide that sank the medieval parish, its priests used the newly fallen ring to save themselves and drowned their flock in exchange. Old and grim, but nobody alive is downstream of it except as legend; it explains no present faction.
**F3. The founding of the program, 1990.** The federal state's first crossing agent was a lie: the program was sold to parliament as tide research, and its first "volunteer passenger" was a kidnapped child. Present-day, but shallow: it is a political scandal rather than a history.

**Pick: F1.** It is a causal chain with heirs. The commander line becomes the state station's director family; the pastor's lie becomes the islanders' changeling folklore that makes a grandmother reject her "changed" daughter; the debt book becomes the template for the modern program's ledger (and the AGI's name); the bought children become the modern "passengers" the state keeps; the alien's seeds travelled the world in the bought children's bodies. F2 survives as texture (the drowned church, unexplained), and F3's insight (a program sold to parliament under a false name) survives as one layer of the modern institution.

## Round 7. The world-scale stake (three options)

**W1. The Silence.** Since 2029 nobody has arrived from the future. The state has had nineteen-year foresight since 1990 (each year an agent arrived from nineteen years ahead carrying an almanac), and its horizon has shrunk by a year every year; 2047 is the first year in four decades the state does not know. Nobody has ever come from after the 2047 equinox. Factions read the Silence differently (enemies destroy the ring; an unpaid debt unravels the world). Truth: the ring is dying of age and fruits on that night. The stake is who controls a power to know the future, and whether the record of what it cost is ever made public.
**W2. Offspring rings opening in world ports.** Seeds carried by the eighteenth-century bought children have germinated in harbours where they died; gates start opening abroad. Large, but it is scale without a personal hook, and the plot would leave the island.
**W3. Exposure only.** The stake is whether the program is revealed. Familiar ("truth vs stability") and passive.

**Pick: W1, with W2 kept as the final vista and W3 folded in as its consequence.** The Silence is a planted fact the player can reason about (if nobody has ever come from after this night, what happens tonight?), it frames the whole game year as the state's "blind year", and its answer opens a further vista rather than closing one (the ring's seeds now live in a handful of people, and one faint hum is heard in a distant harbour after the credits).

## Round 8. Main characters and names (three options for the protagonist, then names)

### 8a. Whose story is it?

**P1. The daughter (nineteen), raised by the clone.** She lacks the past by position, not by amnesia. Her want can be stated at every moment and changes three times. She is the only person who can walk the girl into the ring. She can be in the first frame and the last.
**P2. The original (eighteen), arriving in 2047.** A spectacular opening (a teenager wakes on the flats nineteen years late, her baby grown, her face on a woman of thirty-seven), but she can only arrive at the climax in a fixed timeline unless the climax is moved to the opening, which forces the mystery into flashback and loses the growth ladder.
**P3. The clone-mother (thirty-seven).** The richest inner life, but she knows almost everything, so as a viewpoint she would have to conceal from the player, which the report forbids for playable characters.

**Pick: P1 as the human anchor and main viewpoint**, with P2 delivered as a reconstructed past thread (the player follows Inken through 2028 in the machine's records, so that by the time she arrives they know and love her; Yoko Taro's "spread the details before the tragedy") and P3 kept unplayable until her secrets are out, then given one short playable memory in the final act.

## Round 9. The player's final decision (three options)

**D1. Let the machine close its eyes, or keep watching.** If it closes them, the kitchen is never heard. If the player insists, they hear it, but the record is now public (the charter has just released everything), so the family's first private hour goes to the world.
**D2. Choose who raises the daughter from now on.** Turns the ending into a custody verdict and makes the player judge a family.
**D3. Choose whether the alien's seed is destroyed.** A world-scale switch at the end; it re-opens the climax and dilutes the quiet.

**Pick: D1.** It is small, it is the player's own curiosity set against a family's privacy, it has a real in-world consequence, and it completes the machine's arc (it was once asked to look away and obeyed; at the end it offers to, by its own choice). Refusal is respected: the game does not moralise either way.

## Round 10. One-sentence premise (three options; under fifteen words, no names, a mechanic, a stake)

1. "A daughter must send her own mother, aged eighteen, through a tide that trades lives." (15 words)
2. "On a sinking island, a tide trades bodies across nineteen years, and somebody's mother came back wrong." (17; too long, and "came back wrong" points to horror)
3. "An honest machine that never forgets must decide whether one family's night goes unrecorded." (14; true, but names the wrong protagonist and hides the mechanic)

**Pick: 1.** It names the generating mechanic (a tide that trades lives), the protagonist's loss (her mother, as a girl she loves), and the impossible-sounding situation that only this premise creates. Pacific Rim test: could another story produce "send your eighteen-year-old mother into the past so she can raise you"? Not without all three of the exchange rule, the no-shortcut cloning rule and a fixed timeline.

## Round 11. Key scenes (three options each for the opening, the midpoint and the climax image)

### Opening image (must contain a question)

**O1. "Hark, close your eyes."** The player begins as the archive's camera network on the 2028 equinox night, switching between feeds: guards walking a hooded boy onto the flats, a baby asleep in a crib, an old woman lowering a microphone into a tide pool, and a teenage girl at the station's school terminal whispering to it. The only input offered is to obey. The screen goes black and a counter starts: forty-one minutes.
**O2. The guest room.** Nineteen years later, a daughter comes home to find her mother has prepared a spare bedroom for someone: clothes nineteen years out of date, size small, a calendar with one night circled and "56 kg, 23:40".
**O3. The wet stranger.** A drowned-looking man on the mainland flats insists it is 2009.

**Pick: O1 as a two-minute prologue, then O2 as the first playable scene; O3 opens the investigator's thread in Act I.** O1 plants the final line and the anchor (the baby in the crib) in the first minute; O2 is the small, personal, solvable hook the report demands; O3 lets the one thread that starts elsewhere start with a body.

### Midpoint

**M1. The hide.** During breeding season the daughter creeps through a tern colony (birds are the alarm system) to the forbidden bird warden's hut and finds an eighteen-year-old girl with her mother's face, who says, "I'm your sister."
**M2. The archive gap is filled early** by a recovered tape. Too soon; it removes the long question.
**M3. The mother confesses everything.** A conversation, not a deduction; violates "solve by doing".

**Pick: M1.** It reclassifies Act I (the guest room was never for the arrival; it was for the girl's last weeks), and it opens the larger question in the same beat: if she goes back in September, who comes forward?

### Climax image

**K1. Two ends of one night.** Split screen: the 2028 reconstruction of a girl running into the ring to drag her boyfriend out, and the live 2047 flats where the daughter walks the clone and the agent into the same ring. Two hums. The 2028 side goes black (the gap). On the 2047 side, a girl and a boy rise from the water, she still gripping his jacket.
**K2. The posts burn.** The state sets the ring alight; the ring screams. Spectacle; no human anchor.
**K3. The daughter alone in the ring at the flood.** Pure atmosphere; no event.

**Pick: K1.** It is thread convergence through time, it answers the long question with an image rather than a speech (her grip on his jacket says what she was doing), and it is only possible in this premise.

## Round 12. Trope review log (at the time of selection)

Each match was stopped and reviewed (brief: tropes are a review prompt, not a ban). Full table in 07-self-check.md.

| Beat | Trope it matches | Better opportunity tried | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mother is a clone | "The protagonist (or a loved one) is the copy" | Disclose it at the end of Act I by the player's own attribution work, in Never Let Me Go fashion, and spend the rest of the game on consequences | Kept, disclosed early |
