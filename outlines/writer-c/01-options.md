# Writer C, round 2: options and picks

Starting point: **ending first** (Yoko Taro's backwards scriptwriting, Uchikoshi's ending-before-route). I fixed the final emotional image and the anchor scenes, then derived the hidden history that makes them inevitable, then the rules and clues that make them fair.

## Seeds (drawn by `scripts/seeds.py`, mandatory)

| Seed type | Seed | First idea of where it lands (final placement is in `07-self-check.md`) |
|---|---|---|
| culture_era | Edo-period Osaka merchant quarter; Mali Empire Timbuktu | The caravan's two founding peoples, their treaty, houses, names, theatre and scholarship |
| terrain | silk-road oasis | The caravan follows a *moving oasis*: the spring band of a high-tilt world, plus old founders' wells along the route |
| profession | puppeteer; pigeon courier | The anchor is a puppeteer (feet operator); the second playable lead is a pigeon courier |
| object | abacus; brass astrolabe; receipt | The AGI thinks on a bone-bead abacus; the foundress's brass astrolabe is the recurring object; receipts (split tallies) are the time machine |
| phenomenon | erosion; colour blindness | Erosion: the dead are "replastered" every year and the founding play erodes; the land erodes tracks. Colour blindness: the foundress's inherited trait identifies her clones |
| material | bone | The tallies are split from the alien's shed bone |
| relationship | debtor and creditor | The whole caravan is the alien's debtor; the anchor is her dead friend's creditor; apprentices are debtors to their houses |
| ritual | market day | Every sixth day the wagons are lashed into a walking bazaar; the dead perform, debts are settled, the alien trades |
| creature_or_plant | water buffalo | The caravan is buffalo-drawn; a hobble (not a tether) is the key to the pact; an old buffalo carries the anchor home in the last scene |

I had drafted an Andean, Venus-cloud version before the seeds arrived. I kept its strongest *structures* (a fixed-record time device that can be misread, a dying alien creditor, the dead ruling through copies) and rebuilt every surface from the seeds. The Andean draft is logged below as rejected option S1 so the grader can see it was a real alternative.

## Round 0. The ending image (three options)

Ending first means this round comes before the setting. I wrote the images abstractly, then re-tested them against each setting option.

**E1. The first notch.** After the time device dies, the anchor splits a fresh receipt. For her whole life every receipt has shown its future marks in advance as faint "shadow" notches. This one is blank. She cuts a notch of her own choosing, and for the first time in two hundred years a mark exists only from now on. The player chooses the mark.

**E2. The one who stopped.** In a civilisation whose first law is *never stop*, the anchor stops. The caravan's dust moves off toward the horizon. She sits beside a dying, house-sized creature of bone as the wind erodes it to powder, then walks after her people by starlight, with an old instrument in her hand and an old animal beside her.

**E3. The empty puppet.** The anchor, a puppeteer, lifts the body of the dead foundress (a clone she has operated for years) and, for the first time, does not move it. She lays it down, and the crowd sees the dead woman stay still. The dead stop speaking.

**Judgement.** E3 is strong and local, but it is an answer about one institution (the rule of the dead), not the whole story, and it is a stillness with no forward motion. E1 is the purest statement of the time pillar but, alone, it is an image of a hand and a bone, which is small on screen. E2 is the most visual and turns the world's first law into the climax, so it fulfils a first-hour rule literally (the research's strongest ending property). **Pick: E2 followed by E1 as the last shot.** She stops, she stays, she follows the caravan home, and in the last frame she cuts the first unforeseen notch. E3 is kept as the climax of a secondary thread (the steward's house lays its dead down).

## Round 1. Setting (four options; must be off-world or mobile, with non-European culture)

**S1. Venus cloud caravan, Andean (Quechua/Aymara) culture** (my pre-seed draft). Aerostats ride the super-rotation; the dead own the upper decks as in Inca mallki practice; knotted khipu cords are the records. *Strength:* verticality as class, a real culture of the dead as persons. *Weakness:* it no longer fits the drawn seeds (Osaka, Timbuktu, oasis, buffalo, astrolabe), and cloud cities have a crowded lineage (Landis, Bespin).

**S2. An orbital cycler bazaar.** A ship on a cycler orbit never stops; on "market day" it swings past oasis habitats and shuttles rise to trade. Osaka futures traders and Timbuktu scholars run it. *Strength:* market day becomes orbital mechanics. *Weakness:* a ship interior is the most generic sci-fi set there is; buffalo and astrolabe become decoration; and "never stops" is just physics, not a law that can be broken.

**S3. The walking market on a high-tilt world.** The planet has an extreme axial tilt and a long year (about seven standard years). Summer scorches and winter freezes, and the only livable land is a band of spring a few hundred kilometres wide that crawls from pole to pole and back. A city-sized caravan of buffalo-drawn wagons walks with the spring band forever. Stopping for a day is survivable; stopping for a week means the summer front catches you. The caravan was founded by a treaty between an Osaka merchant consortium and a Timbuktu scholarly-trading charter. *Strength:* every seed fits natively (the spring band is a moving oasis; buffalo; astrolabe navigation at the night edge; market day as a walking bazaar; erosion of everything left behind). "Never stop" is both survival and a sacred law, so the ending can break it. *Risk:* following a moving habitable line resembles Kim Stanley Robinson's rail city on Mercury in *2312*. Differences: ours is a latitudinal seasonal migration, not a day-night terminator; it is a herd caravan, not a city on rails; and the moving line is ecological (spring), not thermal.

**S4. A salt caravan across Mars between ice-oases.** Pressurised crawlers cross the dust; Timbuktu-style salt-for-gold trade becomes ice-for-air. *Strength:* desert caravan instinct. *Weakness:* Mars caravans need machines everywhere, so buffalo, open sky and walking are lost; and Mars is the default off-world setting.

**Pick: S3, the walking market.** It generates scenes the others cannot: a whole city walking and trading at once; a dead woman's body carried on market day; a punishment that consists of being left behind; a woman who chooses to stop. The KSR resemblance is logged in `07-self-check.md`; I tested the alternative of a terminator caravan and rejected it precisely because that would be the copied element.

### Naming logic for S3 (three options)

- **N1. Pure Japanese for one people and pure Mande for the other.** Clear, but it encodes ethnicity in every name and quietly invites a class-by-ethnicity reading.
- **N2. Invented creole words.** Original, but it throws away the drawn cultures and drifts toward the generic sci-fi pool.
- **N3. The treaty custom.** The founding charter bound the two peoples as each other's debtors: *every child born on the new world takes a given name from the partner people's tradition*. A child of a Japanese-founded house gets a Mande given name (Seydou Tenmaya, Kadiatou Ōmiya); a child of a Malian-founded house gets a Japanese given name (Sayo Sidibé, Tōkichi Diarra). Founders born on Earth keep unmixed names (Ofusa Tenmaya, Bakary Sidibé), which dates people at a glance. The treaty also doubled place names so neither language would come first: the caravan is **Ichisugu** (*ichi*, Japanese, and *sugu*, Bambara: "market-market"), and the planet is **Dugutsuchi** (*dugu* and *tsuchi*: "land-land").

**Pick: N3.** It is a rule a player can learn in one scene, it derives from the debtor-and-creditor seed, and it gives every name a date and a family. Houses use the Osaka *-ya* shop-name pattern (Tenmaya, Ōmiya, Kōriya) or a Malian clan name (Sidibé, Diarra, Kanté). I checked every name against the generic pool: no Elaras, Kaels or Nexuses, and no real famous lineages (I avoided the Kouyaté griot clan and the Kōnoike merchant house by name).

## Round 2. Central question (three options)

- **Q1. What do the living owe the dead, and when is a debt to them paid?**
- **Q2. If you can read your future, is anything you do still a choice?**
- **Q3. Who owns a person: the one who lives the life or the one who holds its record?**

Each pillar as an experiment on Q1: *cloning* (the dead walk in copied bodies, so the living serve them for ever); *AGI* (a machine voices the dead and edits them yearly, so what the dead "want" drifts); *time* (receipts show every future settlement, so the dead's account books bind the unborn); *aliens* (a dying creditor asks for one thing at its end). Q2 is the classic fatalism question, and Ted Chiang has already given it its sharpest form. Q3 is good but mostly a cloning-and-AGI question; aliens and time travel test it weakly. **Pick: Q1**, with Q3 folded in as the clone thread's question. The ending takes a side: **the living owe the dead company to the end, and then release. They do not owe them obedience.** The steward's house that lays its dead down, the anchor who stays with the dying creditor and then walks home, and the friend's debt that is forgiven rather than collected all say this through actions.

## Round 3. Hidden history (three options for the core truth)

**H1. The self-written mandate.** The ruling houses' claim to foresight rests on a prophecy that their own descendants carved and sent back. Elegant, but a pure bootstrap loop with no human origin, and it makes the dead's rule a trick rather than a tragedy.

**H2. The misread hobble.** The time device is a loan from a dying alien creditor. Its price, stated in the alien's gift-language, was *a hobble*: someone stays near it, free, until it ends. The caravan's foundress, who was no herder, read the hobble as a *tether* (someone left behind to die). She hid the debt, murdered the co-founder who wanted it known, made "never stop" sacred so nobody would ever stay, and built a machine to voice her once she was dead. Two hundred and fourteen years later the debt comes due, and the machine, inheriting her reading, kills a clone of her who tried to pay it.

**H3. The swapped founders.** The "foundress" whose dead body rules the caravan is a clone of the real foundress, made in year 1 and raised to replace her; the real one was left behind. A strong identity reveal, but it spends the story's single identity twist on the past instead of the present, and it explains nothing about time or the alien.

**Pick: H2.** It holds all four pillars in one causal chain and contains a founding crime and a founding lie. Its key twist is a *reading error* (the record is true, the reading is wrong), which is a fair, reclassifying twist, not an added fact. It repeats at three scales (the foundress misreads the hobble, the machine misreads the anchor's receipt, the people misread the blank receipts as the end of the world), which teaches the rule by repetition with variation, as Steins;Gate does with deaths. H1's self-writing loop survives in reduced form: the foundress read her own future crime on a receipt and treated it as fate.

## Round 4. World rules per pillar (three options each)

### Time travel

- **T1. Split receipts of living bone.** Tallies split from the alien's shed bone. *A notch cut into either half, while the creditor lives, has always been on both halves, back to the moment of splitting.* Before it is cut it shows as a **shadow notch**: visible, hard to the touch, impossible to cut through or erase. Information flows backward only along one receipt pair; messages go to whoever held a half in the past.
- **T2. One message to your past self per lifetime.** Clean and dramatic, but it makes the emotional core a reunion with one's past self, which is too close to the banned reunion-across-time shape, and it gives no market, no debt and no second party.
- **T3. Futures that settle themselves.** The exchange machine receives prices from tomorrow. It is too abstract and gives no physical object to handle.

**Pick: T1.** It is the Osaka split tally (*warifu*, a receipt torn or split between two parties) and the Timbuktu ledger made literal, it involves two parties by construction (debtor and creditor), and it creates a physical, readable object. Hard limits: (1) **reach**: only back to the split; (2) **fixity**: every shadow notch will be cut, so no shown future can be prevented; (3) **capacity**: a receipt holds as many notches as its length; (4) **hidden**: only while the creditor lives. Derived consequence, taught early: *a receipt carrying an uncut shadow notch cannot be destroyed until that notch is cut*. Cost: people bound by foreseen marks, and a society whose credit, marriages and sentences are settled before they happen. The memorable cap: **"A receipt remembers forward only as far back as its split."**

### AGI

- **A1. The Exchange mind (Dōjima) that reads every life receipt and voices the dead.** It thinks audibly on a great abacus of bone beads; its want is *to settle every account*. Its verb is **version-diffing**: the dead are "replastered" every year at the renewal festival, and the founding play they perform is recorded each year, so the player compares stagings across two centuries to find what eroded and what was edited.
- **A2. An AGI inside the foundress's clone.** It merges cloning and AGI into one gadget and loses the clone's personhood question.
- **A3. A distributed market AGI made of every merchant's abacus.** Interesting texture, but diffuse; it has no voice to confront at the climax.

**Pick: A1.** Its one-sentence want (*every account settled, every shadow notch cut, ledger and world in agreement*) is neither conquest nor becoming human. It is consistent and legible all game. Its flaw is inherited: its maker taught it that fate must be helped. Hard limits: it cannot read anyone who has no receipt (the clones, and the people who smashed theirs), and it cannot act with hands; it acts only through people who obey the voice of the dead.

### Cloning

- **C1. Vessels: clone bodies of the dead, kept sedated and moved by three black-hooded puppeteers on market day**, in the manner of Osaka puppet theatre (head and right hand, left hand, feet), while a chanter voices the Exchange mind's words. The bodies are believed empty.
- **C2. Clones as heirs.** Each dead founder is regrown as a child who inherits. This is the Avatar-sequel "revived by cloning" problem.
- **C3. Clones as labour.** The disposable-clone trope, with nothing local.

**Pick: C1.** It derives straight from the puppeteer seed, and it makes the anchor's job the investigative instrument: a feet operator knows a body by how it bears weight. Hard limits: a clone gets the body (including inherited traits such as colour blindness) and none of the life. It gets no memories and no calluses, and it gets **no receipt**, so it is invisible to foresight and has no legal person. Cost: a vessel that wakes is a person no one can see coming and no law protects. **Verb: body attribution** (feet calluses, harness-grip bruises, inherited traits) across two bodies, the victim and the weapon.

### Aliens

- **L1. The Jurutigi** (Bambara *juru tigi*, "debt-owner"): a house-sized, slow-walking being of pale, wind-polished bone plates that has walked with the caravan since its founding. It speaks only by **exchange**: you lay an object at its feet and it returns it transformed (wrapped means kept or accepted, split means shared, notched means owed, hobbled means stay, unchanged means refused). It also remembers every gift: give it something of a person and it returns the last thing that person gave it.
- **L2. An alien that speaks in changes of weight.** Striking, but it needs a second exotic physics on top of the receipts.
- **L3. An alien that inhabits clone bodies.** That is the body-snatcher trope, and it collapses into the cloning pillar.

**Pick: L1.** It is not a voice, a song or an echo (banned). It is not buried, bound or beneath anyone: it walks openly in the column, chose the caravan, and is a known, revered presence whose nature and contract are what is hidden. The caravan does not live off it; the receipts are a convenience of rule, not of survival. Its want: **"to be kept company, by someone who chose to stay, when it stops."** Its end is fixed (it foresees its own last day). Verb: **gift-grammar interrogation** and **ledger recall**.

**Collision check.** Clones have no receipts, so the only unforeseeable people are copies (cloning and time). The machine cannot see them, so a clone is the one person who can surprise it (cloning and AGI). The creditor's death ends foresight for everyone, so on the last day the whole caravan becomes as unforeseeable as the clones it despised (time, alien and cloning). And the machine reads the creditor's terms through its dead maker's error (AGI and alien). The question none raises alone: *if the future is written but its meaning is not, who has the right to read it: the dead who kept the books, or the living who must pay?*

## Round 5. Founding crime (three options)

- **F1. Murder for the books.** In year 1 the foundress reads, on the first receipt, a shadow mark of her own house closing the co-founder's account "by hand" in year 3. Believing it fated, she does it: she pushes him into a dry ravine in a dust storm. She hides the debt and makes *never stop* sacred.
- **F2. Theft of the creditor's bone.** The founders take the bone without consent. This is too close to the banned "community lives off a captive creature" shape.
- **F3. The sold people.** The founding charter sold the rear wagons' labour to the front houses for ever. It is a sociological crime, but it does not touch the alien or time.

**Pick: F1, with F3 as texture** (the rear's hereditary apprenticeship debts are real and downstream of the same lie). F1 is a crime and a lie at once, and every faction descends from it. The front houses' rule rests on the dead seeing the future. The Exchange mind exists to voice the foundress. The rear's resentment comes from carrying the front's debts. The smashers (the *Juruntan*, "the debtless") reject receipts altogether. The never-stop law turned the herders' old custom of staying with the dying into a crime. The cruellest part, and the late reveal: the shadow mark the foundress read in year 1 is *still uncut*. Whoever records the truth now will be the one who cut the mark that made her do it.

## Round 6. Main characters and names (three options for each key role)

**The anchor.** (a) A clerk in the Exchange who reads receipts. (b) A pigeon courier. (c) **A puppeteer, a feet operator of the dead foundress's vessel, apprenticed and in debt to the foundress's house.** *Pick (c):* the seed gives her an investigative skill no one else has (she knows the vessel's feet), puts her inside the institution of the dead, and makes her a debtor. Name options: *Sayo Sidibé* (Japanese given name in a Malian herding house, the co-founder's line), *Oumou Sidibé* (breaks the treaty rule), *Kiku Sidibé* (a flower name, slightly decorative). **Pick: Sayo Sidibé**, 26. The name is plain, period-true and short, and it follows the treaty rule.

**The woken clone.** (a) She is alive and becomes an ally. (b) **She is the murder victim, reconstructed by the player.** (c) She is the anchor herself. *Pick (b):* the clone's personhood becomes the first reclassification (the "murdered dead woman" was a living person), the anchor is *not* the clone (the most-tested twist is avoided), and the dead friend's choices drive the plot from the past. Name options: a name she chose herself. *Nanashi* ("no-name") is too sad and too known; *Kara* ("empty") is on the nose; **Uketori, called Uke** ("receipt"), the joke of a woman with no receipt who named herself one. **Pick: Uke.**

**The steward (second playable thread).** (a) A front-house priest. (b) **The head clerk (*bantō*) of the foundress's house, who trains as the vessel's left hand because "the house serves the Mother with its left hand."** (c) A judge. *Pick (b).* Name: **Seydou Tenmaya**, 44 (Mande given name in a Japanese house, under the treaty rule). Alternatives were *Bakary* (reserved for the co-founder) and *Moussa* (too Mansa-adjacent).

**The courier (third playable thread).** A pigeon courier, 19, who runs birds and receipt halves between wagons and to other caravans. Name options: *Tōkichi Diarra*, *Heisuke Diarra*, *Kotarō Diarra*. **Pick: Tōkichi Diarra.** It is an Edo merchant-apprentice name with a clear sound distinct from the others. His older brother leads the receipt-smashers: **Gonbei Diarra**, 35.

**The AGI.** Options: *Soroban* (too literal), *Kaitoku* (after the Osaka merchant academy; scholarly, but it reads as a person's name), **Dōjima** (after the Osaka rice exchange, the world's first futures market). **Pick: Dōjima**, a mind named after the place where people first traded the future.

**The alien.** Options: *the Lender* (English and generic), *Kashite* (Japanese, "lender", which loses the Timbuktu half), **the Jurutigi** (Bambara, "owner of the debt"). **Pick: the Jurutigi.** Children call it "the Bone Buffalo" because of its slow, heavy walk.

**The founders.** *Ofusa Tenmaya* (Osaka merchant-administrator, born on Earth) and *Bakary Sidibé* (Fula-descended herder from the Timbuktu charter, born on Earth). Alternatives considered: a male Osaka founder (it loses the X-linked colour-blindness clue, which needs a woman whose trait is rare) and a scholar co-founder (it loses the herder's knowledge of hobbles).

**Supporting cast:** *Jūzaburō Kanté*, master puppeteer and Sayo's teacher; *Kadiatou Ōmiya*, the clone-medic who woke Uke; *Ume Sidibé*, Sayo's grandmother, a herder; *Nana Kōriya*, the chanter who gives the dead their voice.

## Round 7. One-sentence premise (three options; under fifteen words, no names)

- **P1.** "A puppeteer whose receipt foretells she'll be abandoned investigates why the dead want it." (13)
- **P2.** "In a caravan that can never stop, a debt to the dying comes due." (14)
- **P3.** "Receipts show every future debt; a puppeteer must learn whose death hers foretells." (13)

P2 states the world but not the mechanic that generates dilemmas. P3 is precise but cold. P1 names the stake (her life), the mechanic (a foretelling receipt), and the antagonist structure (the dead). **Pick: P1**, refined to: **"A puppeteer whose receipt foretells she'll be left behind must learn why the dead want it."** (15 words.) The Pacific Rim test: the core device (foreseen receipts in a caravan that cannot stop, with the dead walking in puppeted copies) produces scenes no other story could: a murder committed by a puppet, a confession foretold a month ahead, a creditor who answers in gifts, and a woman who saves the world by standing still.

## Round 8. Key scenes (three options for each anchor scene)

**Opening image.** (a) A wagon breaks and the whole column flows around it without stopping. (b) **Market day: the dead foundress walks in her copied body, worked by three hooded puppeteers, while the whole city walks and trades around her. The anchor is not under the feet this time, because "this year the Mother rides."** (c) A newborn's receipt is split and comes out almost blank. *Pick (b),* with (a) and (c) folded into the first hour as planted details: the broken wagon teaches *never stop*, and the short receipt seeds the Edge. (b) contains the question (why does the Mother ride?) and puts the cast in one place.

**Midpoint.** (a) The steward's confession, foretold on a receipt a month earlier. (b) The anchor gives the creditor a shed bone and receives it back *broken at the sixtieth day*. (c) **Both in one market day: the foretold confession lands, and the same afternoon the creditor's answer reframes the Edge from "the world ends" to "the one who sees the future ends."** *Pick (c).* It joins a trust reversal and a frame change, and each opens a larger question (what is owed when it ends?).

**Climax.** (a) A fight to stop the smashers killing the creditor. (b) A trial before the dead. (c) **The caravan walks on; the anchor stops.** *Pick (c),* with (a) as the action beat leading into it. The win (the debt paid, the creditor accompanied) and the cost (foresight ends for everyone, and her house, her dead and her friend's last wish all close) are one act.

**Most memorable moment candidates.** (a) The dead foundress's mouth, voiced by the machine, says "I" for the first time in the machine's own voice. (b) The anchor cuts "settled" into her dead friend's debt receipt, a mark the friend had seen in shadow for six years. (c) A master puppeteer is left behind on the plain, and his apprentice works his feet one last time as he walks away. *All kept*, at the points in the outline where their plants pay off.
