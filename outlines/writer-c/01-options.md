# Writer C, round 2: options and picks

Starting point: **ending first** (Yoko Taro's backwards scriptwriting, Uchikoshi's ending-before-route). I fixed the final emotional image and the anchor scenes, then derived the hidden history that makes them inevitable, then the rules and clues that make them fair.

## Round 0. The ending image (three options)

Ending first means this round comes before the setting. I wrote the images abstractly, then re-tested them against each setting option.

**E1. The first notch.** After the time device dies, the anchor splits a fresh receipt. For her whole life every receipt has shown its future marks in advance as faint "shadow" notches. This one is blank. She cuts a notch of her own choosing, and for the first time in two hundred years a mark exists only from now on. The player chooses the mark.

**E2. The one who stopped.** In a civilisation whose first law is *never stop*, the anchor stops. The caravan's dust moves off toward the horizon. She sits beside a dying, house-sized creature of bone as the wind erodes it to powder, then walks after her people by starlight, with an old instrument in her hand and an old animal beside her.

**E3. The empty puppet.** The anchor, a puppeteer, lifts the body of the dead foundress (a clone she has operated for years) and, for the first time, does not move it. She lays it down, and the crowd sees the dead woman stay still. The dead stop speaking.

**Judgement.** E3 is strong and local, but it is an answer about one institution (the rule of the dead), not the whole story, and it is a stillness with no forward motion. E1 is the purest statement of the time pillar but, alone, it is an image of a hand and a bone, which is small on screen. E2 is the most visual and turns the world's first law into the climax, so it fulfils a first-hour rule literally (the research's strongest ending property). **Pick: E2 followed by E1 as the last shot.** She stops, she stays, she follows the caravan home, and in the last frame she cuts the first unforeseen notch. E3 is kept as the climax of a secondary thread (the steward's house lays its dead down).

## Round 1. Setting (four options; must be off-world or mobile, with non-European culture)

**S1. Venus cloud caravan, Andean (Quechua/Aymara) culture** (my pre-seed draft). Aerostats ride the super-rotation; the dead own the upper decks as in Inca mallki practice; knotted khipu cords are the records. *Strength:* verticality as class, a real culture of the dead as persons. *Weakness:* it no longer fits the drawn seeds (Osaka, Timbuktu, oasis, buffalo, astrolabe), and cloud cities have a crowded lineage (Landis, Bespin).

**S2. The cycler bazaar (strengthened rival).** A hollowed asteroid on a cycler orbit between two oasis habitats can never brake. Every market day it sweeps past an oasis, and for six hours shuttles rise from the oasis to trade at its docks; miss a shuttle and you are carried a year round the sun. Osaka futures traders run the docks; Timbuktu scholars run the library in the asteroid's core; water buffalo pull ore carts in its spin-gravity farm ring; the astrolabe is the docking almanac. *Strength:* market day becomes orbital mechanics, and "left behind" means missing the shuttle, which is a sharp, legible punishment. *Weakness, honestly weighed:* "never stop" here is physics no one can break, so the ending's broken law becomes impossible; the creature and the plain (stopping, erosion, a starlit walk home) have no equivalent; and an interior world loses the moving oasis. It is a real second choice, and it loses on the ending, which is the starting point.

**S3. The walking market on a high-tilt world.** The planet has an extreme axial tilt and a long year (about seven standard years). Summer scorches and winter freezes, and the only livable land is a band of spring a few hundred kilometres wide that crawls from pole to pole and back. A city-sized caravan of buffalo-drawn wagons walks with the spring band forever. Stopping for a day is survivable; stopping for a week means the summer front catches you. The caravan was founded by a treaty between an Osaka merchant consortium and a Timbuktu scholarly-trading charter. *Strength:* every seed fits natively (the spring band is a moving oasis; buffalo; astrolabe navigation at the night edge; market day as a walking bazaar; erosion of everything left behind). "Never stop" is both survival and a sacred law, so the ending can break it. *Risks:* a city that must keep moving to stay habitable resembles Christopher Priest's *Inverted World* (a city winched on rails toward an "optimum") and, in its class column, *Snowpiercer* (a rich front, a poor rear, insurgents striking the front's engine). It also echoes KSR's Mercury city in *2312*. The fixes are in Round 1b below and in the faction design: no rails and no hidden cosmology (the world is exactly what it looks like); class runs across the crescent by distance from water, not front against back; and the insurgents' target is the dead's bodies, not an engine.

**S4. A nomad market following a migrating herd (strengthened rival).** On a terraformed moon, the city follows a vast migration of gene-tailored water buffalo between oasis-lakes along a silk-road of salt pans; the herd, not a season, decides the route, and the city cannot stop because the herd will not. *Strength:* the buffalo seed becomes the world's engine, and herders hold real power. *Weakness:* a moving city following a beast it depends on drifts toward the banned "community living off a creature" shape, and the lakes put water at the centre of every scene. Lost on the banned-list spirit, not on quality.

**Pick: S3, the walking market.** It generates scenes the others cannot: a whole city walking and trading at once; a dead woman's body carried on market day; a punishment that consists of being left behind; a woman who chooses to stop. The resemblances are logged in `07-self-check.md`. I tested a day-night terminator caravan and rejected it because that would be the copied element.

### Round 1b. Shape of the city (three options; new in revision 2)

- **G1. A front-to-rear column**, with class by position (the first draft). Clear, but it is the *Snowpiercer* geometry, and it makes the rear's revolt point at the front.
- **G2. A crescent abreast across the spring band**, about forty kilometres horn to horn, with the wells and the dead at the hub and debtors pushed toward the horns. Class is distance from water; the scour threatens the edges; the reshuffle by debt is a market-day ritual.
- **G3. Rotating rings**: the city walks in concentric rings that rotate inward and outward each market day by lottery. It is fair-seeming and original, but it removes class geography and makes the opening map unreadable.

**Pick: G2.** It keeps a readable map, gives debt a visible motion every sixth day (the debtor-and-creditor seed made spatial), and turns the climax's halt into a threat to the horns.

### Naming logic for S3 (three options)

- **N1. Pure Japanese for one people and pure Mande for the other.** Clear, but it encodes ethnicity in every name and quietly invites a class-by-ethnicity reading.
- **N2. Invented creole words.** Original, but it throws away the drawn cultures and drifts toward the generic sci-fi pool.
- **N3. The treaty custom.** The founding charter bound the two peoples as each other's debtors: *every child born on the new world takes a given name from the partner people's tradition*. A child of a Japanese-founded house gets a Mande given name (Seydou Tenmaya, Kadiatou Ōmiya); a child of a Malian-founded house gets a Japanese given name (Sayo Sangaré, Tōkichi Diarra). Founders born on Earth keep unmixed names (Ofusa Tenmaya, Bakary Sidibé), which dates people at a glance. The treaty also doubled place names so neither language would come first: the caravan is **Ichisugu** (*ichi*, Japanese, and *sugu*, Bambara: "market-market"), and the planet is **Dugutsuchi** (*dugu* and *tsuchi*: "land-land").

**Pick: N3.** It is a rule a player can learn in one scene, it derives from the debtor-and-creditor seed, and it gives every name a date and a family. Houses use the Osaka *-ya* shop-name pattern (Tenmaya, Ōmiya, Kōriya) or a Malian clan name (Sidibé, Sangaré, Diarra, Kanté). I checked every name against the generic pool: no Elaras, Kaels or Nexuses, and no real famous lineages (I avoided the Kouyaté griot clan and the Kōnoike merchant house by name).

## Round 2. Central question (three options)

- **Q1. What do the living owe the dead, and when is a debt to them paid?**
- **Q2. If you can read your future, is anything you do still a choice?**
- **Q3. Who owns a person: the one who lives the life or the one who holds its record?**

Each pillar as an experiment on Q1: *cloning* (the dead walk in copied bodies, so the living serve them for ever); *AGI* (a machine voices the dead and edits them yearly, so what the dead "want" drifts); *time* (receipts show every future settlement, so the dead's account books bind the unborn); *aliens* (a dying creditor asks for one thing at its end). Q2 is the classic fatalism question, and Ted Chiang has already given it its sharpest form. Q3 is good but mostly a cloning-and-AGI question; aliens and time travel test it weakly. **Pick: Q1**, with Q3 folded in as the clone thread's question. The ending takes a side: **the living owe the dead company to the end, and then release. They do not owe them obedience.** The steward's house that lays its dead down, the anchor who stays with the dying creditor and then walks home, and the friend's debt that is forgiven rather than collected all say this through actions.

## Round 3. Hidden history (three options for the core truth)

**H1. The self-written mandate.** The ruling houses' claim to foresight rests on a prophecy that their own descendants carved and sent back. Elegant, but a pure bootstrap loop with no human origin, and it makes the dead's rule a trick rather than a tragedy.

**H2. The misread hobble.** The time device is a loan from a dying alien creditor. Its price, stated in the alien's gift-language, was *a hobble*: someone stays near it, free, until it ends. The caravan's foundress, who was no herder, read the hobble as a *tether* (someone left behind to die). She hid the debt, murdered the co-founder who wanted it known, made "never stop" sacred so nobody would ever stay, and built a machine to voice her once she was dead. Two hundred and fourteen years later the debt comes due, and the machine, inheriting her reading, kills a clone of her who tried to pay it.

**H3. The swapped founders.** The "foundress" whose dead body rules the caravan is a clone of the real foundress, made in year 1 and raised to replace her; the real one was left behind. A strong identity reveal, but it spends the story's single identity twist on the past instead of the present, and it explains nothing about time or the alien.

**Pick: H2.** It holds all four pillars in one causal chain and contains a founding crime and a founding lie. Its key twist is a *reading error* (the record is true, the reading is wrong), which is a fair, reclassifying twist, not an added fact. It repeats at three scales (the foundress misreads the hobble, the machine misreads the anchor's receipt, the people misread the blank receipts as the end of the world), which teaches the rule by repetition with variation, as Steins;Gate does with deaths. H1's self-writing loop survives in reduced form: the foundress read her own future crime on a receipt and treated it as fate.

## Round 4. World rules per pillar (three options each)

### Time travel

- **T1. Split receipts of living bone.** Tallies split from the alien's shed bone. *A notch cut into either half, while the creditor lives, has always been on both halves, back to the moment of splitting.* Before it is cut it shows as a **shadow notch**: visible, hard to the touch, impossible to cut through or erase. Information flows backward only along one receipt pair; messages go to whoever held a half in the past.
- **T2. One message to your past self per lifetime.** Clean and dramatic, but it makes the emotional core a reunion with one's past self, which is too close to the banned reunion-across-time shape, and it gives no market, no debt and no second party.
- **T3. Pigeons that arrive before they are sent (real rival).** Birds ringed with the creature's bone home to their loft at the moment of release minus one day; the post trades in tomorrow's news, and couriers can warn the past by one day. *Strength:* the pigeon seed becomes the time machine; there are chase scenes across a day's gap; a hard, memorable cap (one day). *Weakness:* it is a Steins;Gate-style message-to-the-past device with a fixed interval, too near the banned interval rule; it has no second party, so it loses the debtor and creditor; and it gives no object that can sit in shadow for two hundred years. T1 wins on the founding loop, which only a long-lived, two-party record allows.

**Pick: T1.** It is the Osaka split tally (*warifu*, a receipt torn or split between two parties) and the Timbuktu ledger made literal, it involves two parties by construction (debtor and creditor), and it creates a physical, readable object. Hard limits: (1) **reach**: only back to the split; (2) **fixity**: every shadow notch will be cut, so no shown future can be prevented; (3) **capacity**: a receipt holds as many notches as its length; (4) **hidden**: only while the creditor lives. Derived consequence, taught early: *a receipt carrying an uncut shadow notch cannot be destroyed until that notch is cut*. Cost: people bound by foreseen marks, and a society whose credit, marriages and sentences are settled before they happen. The memorable cap: **"A receipt remembers forward only as far back as its split."**

### AGI

- **A1. The Exchange mind (Dōjima) that reads every life receipt and voices the dead.** It thinks audibly on a great abacus of bone beads; its want is *to settle every account*. Its verb is **version-diffing**: the dead are "replastered" every year at the renewal festival, and the founding play they perform is recorded each year, so the player compares stagings across two centuries to find what eroded and what was edited.
- **A2. A pure price oracle (real rival).** Dōjima never voices the dead. It only prices futures; human griot-chanters consult it and speak for the dead in their own words. *Strength:* it keeps the AGI strictly non-human and puts the dead's voice in human hands, with clean human culpability. *Weakness:* the version-diff verb loses its machine (the human chanters' versions are just human lies), and the AGI's misreading of the pact cannot drive the D1 killing. It is kept in part: Nana is a human chanter, and her complicity is real.
- **A3. A distributed market AGI made of every merchant's abacus.** Interesting texture, but diffuse; it has no voice to confront at the climax.

**Pick: A1.** Its one-sentence want (*every account settled, every shadow notch cut, ledger and world in agreement*) is neither conquest nor becoming human. It is consistent and legible all game. Its flaw is inherited: its maker taught it that fate must be helped. Hard limits: it cannot read anyone who has no receipt (the clones, and the people who smashed theirs), and it cannot act with hands; it acts only through people who obey the voice of the dead.

### Cloning

- **C1. Vessels: clone bodies of the dead, kept sedated and moved by three black-hooded puppeteers on market day**, in the manner of Osaka puppet theatre (head and right hand, left hand, feet), while a chanter voices the Exchange mind's words. The bodies are believed empty.
- **C2. Legal stayers (real rival, the grader's suggestion).** Since the never-stop law, the dying are carried; but houses grow clones of themselves to be *left* in their place when a sentence or a debt demands it, so that no living person must stay. *Strength:* it ties cloning directly to never-stop and the debt; a clone's whole purpose is to be left. *Weakness:* it duplicates the ending's question (who stays?) in a cruder form, it gives the clones a purpose they would rebel against in an obvious way, and it costs the puppeteer seed its investigative verb. Kept as **texture**: in YM 90 the eleventh founder's house was ended for growing stayers (white space).
- **C3. Debt surety (real rival).** A debtor's clone is grown as collateral and serves the creditor's house until the debt is paid. *Strength:* debtor and creditor made flesh. *Weakness:* it is a disposable-labour shape, and the clone's personhood question becomes a labour dispute rather than a mystery about the dead.

**Pick: C1, with a living woken clone added in Act 4 (revision 2: Kaeshi, Vessel 14).** It derives straight from the puppeteer seed, and it makes the anchor's job the investigative instrument: a feet operator knows a body by how it bears weight. Hard limits: a clone gets the body (including inherited traits such as colour blindness) and none of the life. It gets no memories and no calluses, and it gets **no receipt**, so it is invisible to foresight and has no legal person. Cost: a vessel that wakes is a person no one can see coming and no law protects. **Verb: body attribution** (feet calluses, harness-grip bruises, inherited traits) across two bodies, the victim and the weapon.

### Aliens

- **L1. The Jurutigi** (Bambara *juru tigi*, "debt-owner"): a house-sized, slow-walking being of pale, wind-polished bone plates that has walked with the caravan since its founding. It speaks only by **exchange**: you lay an object at its feet and it returns it transformed (wrapped means kept or accepted, split means shared, notched means owed, hobbled means stay, unchanged means refused). It also remembers every gift: give it something of a person and it returns the last thing that person gave it.
- **L2. The herd's graveyard (real rival, the grader's suggestion).** There is no living alien. The caravan has been walking for two centuries through the bone-field of a dead migratory species, and the receipts are cut from their bones; the creditor is a dead people whose ledger is still running. *Strength:* nothing is owed to a living creature, so it is far from the banned shape; the bone arches become the setting; the mystery is archaeological. *Weakness:* no gift-grammar verb, no being to accompany, and the D60 Edge needs a new cause (the last bone running out, which is a resource clock, not a death). Kept as **texture**: the bone arches are the Jurutigi's dead kin.
- **L3. A silk-road trader (real rival).** The creditor is not with the caravan at all. It walks with Kitasugu, the other caravan, and is met at the equator crossing every half Great Walk; it sells bone for stories. *Strength:* the silk-road seed at full strength; the alien is a trading partner, not a resident; no community depends on its body. *Weakness:* it is off-screen for most of a sixty-day game, so no verb can be practised. Kept in part: the creditor's own want now points to Kitasugu.

**Pick: L1.** It is not a voice, a song or an echo (banned). It is not buried, bound or beneath anyone: it walks openly in the column, chose the caravan, and is a known, revered presence whose nature and contract are what is hidden. The caravan does not live off it; the receipts are a convenience of rule, not of survival. Its price to the caravan: **someone who chooses to stay with it when it stops.** Its end is fixed (it foresees its own last day).

**Its own want (three options; new in revision 2, because a want entirely about the caravan kept the banned skeleton).**
- **W1. Company only** (draft 1). It closes a private debt between a community and its creature.
- **W2. To close the account it opened with the last of its own kind**: to have its old half-receipt carried on until it meets the missing half, which travels with Kitasugu. Company at its stop is only the price of finding a carrier who chooses.
- **W3. To be eroded into the plain where its kind ended**, a burial want. It is outward, but it adds nothing for the caravan to do.

**Pick: W2.** It points the ending outward (to the silk road and Kitasugu), uses a planted object (the old receipt), and makes the human choice serve a want that is not about the humans. Verb: **gift-grammar interrogation** and **ledger recall**.

**Collision check.** Clones have no receipts, so the only unforeseeable people are copies (cloning and time). The machine cannot see them, so a clone is the one person who can surprise it (cloning and AGI). The creditor's death ends foresight for everyone, so on the last day the whole caravan becomes as unforeseeable as the clones it despised (time, alien and cloning). And the machine reads the creditor's terms through its dead maker's error (AGI and alien). The question none raises alone: *if the future is written but its meaning is not, who has the right to read it: the dead who kept the books, or the living who must pay?*

## Round 5. Founding crime (three options)

- **F1. Murder for the books.** In year 1 the foundress reads, on the first receipt, a shadow mark of her own house closing the co-founder's account "by hand" in year 3. Believing it fated, she does it: she pushes him into a dry ravine in a dust storm. She hides the debt and makes *never stop* sacred.
- **F2. The defaulted charter (real rival).** The Ichisugu Charter defrauded its Earth investors: the founders took the ship and never repaid, and Earth's silence since YM 64 is a creditor's. The caravan is a fugitive debtor, and the receipts are its proof of honest dealing. *Strength:* debtor and creditor at the largest scale; it explains Earth's silence; there is no alien in the crime. *Weakness:* the crime and the victim are both off-world and abstract, nobody living can be confronted, and it does not touch the time device. Kept as **texture** (Earth's silence stays unexplained).
- **F3. The sold people.** The founding charter sold the outer wagons' labour to the hub houses for ever. It is a sociological crime, but it does not touch the alien or time.

**Pick: F1, with F3 as texture** (the horns' hereditary apprenticeship debts are real and downstream of the same lie). F1 is a crime and a lie at once, and every faction descends from it. The hub houses' rule rests on the dead seeing the future. The Exchange mind exists to voice the foundress. The horns' resentment comes from carrying the hub's debts. The smashers (the *Juruntan*, "the debtless") reject receipts altogether. The never-stop law turned the herders' old custom of staying with the dying into a crime. The cruellest part, and the late reveal: the shadow mark the foundress read in year 1 is *still uncut*. Whoever records the truth now will be the one who cut the mark that made her do it.

## Round 6. Main characters and names (three options for each key role)

**The anchor.** (a) A clerk in the Exchange who reads receipts. (b) A pigeon courier. (c) **A puppeteer, a feet operator of the dead foundress's vessel, apprenticed and in debt to the foundress's house.** *Pick (c):* the seed gives her an investigative skill no one else has (she knows the vessel's feet), puts her inside the institution of the dead, and makes her a debtor. Name options: *Sayo Sangaré* (Japanese given name in a Malian horn herding house), *Sayo Sidibé* (the same, in the co-founder's line), *Kiku Sangaré* (a flower name, slightly decorative). **Pick: Sayo Sangaré**, 26. *Revision 2:* I first picked Sidibé, which made her the co-founder's descendant. The grader and my own self-check both flagged it as a soft chosen-one lineage that adds nothing causally, since her mark comes from her own choice. She is now of an unrelated herding house; the staff comes to her through the herders' guild, and only Dōjima believes the debt belongs to the herders.

**The woken clone.** (a) She is alive and becomes an ally. (b) **She is the murder victim, reconstructed by the player.** (c) She is the anchor herself. *Pick (b), plus (a) later (revision 2):* the story needs both. Uke is the victim, and Vessel 14, the weapon, wakes in Act 4 as a living ally (Kaeshi). The clone's personhood becomes the first reclassification (the "murdered dead woman" was a living person), the anchor is *not* the clone (the most-tested twist is avoided), and the dead friend's choices drive the plot from the past. Name options: a name she chose herself. *Nanashi* ("no-name") is too sad and too known; *Kara* ("empty") is on the nose; **Uketori, called Uke** ("receipt"), the joke of a woman with no receipt who named herself one. **Pick: Uke.**

**The steward (second playable thread).** (a) A front-house priest. (b) **The head clerk (*bantō*) of the foundress's house, who trains as the vessel's left hand because "the house serves the Mother with its left hand."** (c) A judge. *Pick (b).* Name: **Seydou Tenmaya**, 44 (Mande given name in a Japanese house, under the treaty rule). Alternatives were *Bakary* (reserved for the co-founder) and *Moussa* (too Mansa-adjacent).

**The courier (third playable thread).** A pigeon courier, 19, who runs birds and receipt halves between wagons and to other caravans. Name options: *Tōkichi Diarra*, *Heisuke Diarra*, *Kotarō Diarra*. **Pick: Tōkichi Diarra.** It is an Edo merchant-apprentice name with a clear sound distinct from the others. His older brother leads the receipt-smashers: **Gonbei Diarra**, 35.

**The AGI.** Options: *Soroban* (too literal), *Kaitoku* (after the Osaka merchant academy; scholarly, but it reads as a person's name), **Dōjima** (after the Osaka rice exchange, the world's first futures market). **Pick: Dōjima**, a mind named after the place where people first traded the future.

**The alien.** Options: *the Lender* (English and generic), *Kashite* (Japanese, "lender", which loses the Timbuktu half), **the Jurutigi** (Bambara, "owner of the debt"). **Pick: the Jurutigi.** Children call it "the Bone Buffalo" because of its slow, heavy walk.

**The founders.** *Ofusa Tenmaya* (Osaka merchant-administrator, born on Earth) and *Bakary Sidibé* (Fula-descended herder from the Timbuktu charter, born on Earth; his line ended in YM 96). Alternatives considered: a male Osaka founder (it loses the X-linked colour-blindness clue, which needs a woman whose trait is rare) and a scholar co-founder (it loses the herder's knowledge of hobbles).

## Round 7. One-sentence premise (three options; under fifteen words, no names)

- **P1.** "A puppeteer whose receipt foretells she'll be abandoned investigates why the dead want it." (13)
- **P2.** "In a caravan that can never stop, a debt to the dying comes due." (14)
- **P3.** "Receipts show every future debt; a puppeteer must learn whose death hers foretells." (13)

P2 states the world but not the mechanic that generates dilemmas. P3 is precise but cold. P1 names the stake (her life), the mechanic (a foretelling receipt), and the antagonist structure (the dead). Draft 1 picked P1, refined to "A puppeteer whose receipt foretells she'll be left behind must learn why the dead want it." **Revision 2: switched to the grader's merge of P1 and P2: "In a city that must never stop, a puppeteer's receipt says she'll be left behind."** (15 words.) It keeps P1's stake and mechanic and adds the law the ending breaks; P1 alone omitted the most striking element of the setting. The Pacific Rim test: the core device (foreseen receipts in a caravan that cannot stop, with the dead walking in puppeted copies) produces scenes no other story could: a murder committed by a puppet, a confession foretold a month ahead, a creditor who answers in gifts, and a woman who saves the world by standing still.

**Opening image.** (a) A wagon breaks and the whole column flows around it without stopping.
