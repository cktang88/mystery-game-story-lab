## Step 0. Setting (four options)

Constraints: a dense human place of at least hundreds of thousands, outside Europe and North America, from a specific culture, era and logic; must absorb Lalibela, 1980s Seoul and a caravan route; nothing coastal, no village.

**S1. Amole (አሞሌ), the carved city.** A highland megacity of about 19 million cut *downward* into a basalt plateau the way Lalibela's churches were: streets are trenches, buildings are monoliths left standing when the rock around them was removed, and every quarter is a pit with a sky. The old salt-caravan road from the volcanic depression runs through it as the deepest trench, the Narrow, still walked by camel caravans carrying salt bars (*amole*, the old salt currency the city is named after). Founded 2089 as a refugee camp in the Burning Years; carved by Korean-built boring machines whose crews stayed and married in (the Tunnelers). Now in its "1980s Seoul" moment: seventy years of development rule by a council of elders and a military line, a televised housing lottery for newly cut apartments, a student movement, and the first direct election in its history.
- For: every seed lands in the architecture; trenches give stealth, tailing and verticality; the election gives a public, dated climax; the caravan gives an outsider's angle; cutting stone is irreversible, which rhymes with rust and the time rule.
- Against: a blend could read as costume. Fix: the Korean strand is a community with its own naming rule, not decoration, and the carving technique belongs to humans throughout (never credited to the alien; the trope table flags ancient-astronaut readings of real cultures' achievements).

**S2. Hwangto, the terracotta stack.** A Korean-named vertical megacity of 12 million on a steppe caravan crossing, its 1980s-style apartment slabs faced in terracotta, with an Ethiopian Orthodox monastic quarter carved into the bedrock under the towers.
- For: strong Seoul texture; terracotta everywhere.
- Against: the Lalibela seed shrinks to one district; "something carved beneath the city" drifts toward the banned buried-under-community shape; Korean-majority naming makes the Ethiopian seed decorative.

**S3. The pilgrim institution.** A permanent rock-hewn pilgrimage institution of 600,000 resident pilgrims, priests, scribes and caravan porters, which swells to three million at the winter feast. Ruled by an elected abbot; the election is a mass rite.
- For: a mass institution with its own law; the pilgrim route is the caravan route.
- Against: religious governance would make the story about a living church's real rites, which risks misrepresenting Ethiopian Orthodoxy; 1980s Seoul (secular development politics) fits badly.

**S4. The walking city.** A caravan-city of 400,000 that never stops: tents, terracotta water jars and printing trucks moving along the salt route, with a carved winter-city it returns to.
- For: striking; the terrain seed is literal.
- Against: spectacle more than logic; hard to make dense; a moving city makes reconstruction scenes expensive and the convergence point vague.

**Pick: S1, Amole.** It is the only option where all three setting seeds generate rules rather than scenery: the downward-carved plan makes every quarter a sealed pit with a narrow way in (the fig-wasp's sealed fig, at city scale), the caravan trench gives an outside faction and a route for the alien to walk, and the "1980s Seoul" moment gives a dated public ritual (the election) on which the hidden history can be forced into the open. It is inland, Ethiopian-derived, dense, and has no precedent I know of in the genre.

## Step 1. Central question (three options)

**Q1. "When one body makes a promise, which bodies must keep it?"** Cloning asks it of copies who share a legal self; time travel asks it of later selves who already know the promise was kept; the AGI asks it of a ledger that cannot tell the difference; the alien asks it of a species to whom a genome is a person.

**Q2. "If you have already seen that a thing will be done, are you still the one who must do it?"** A question about fixed futures and responsibility.

**Q3. "Can a vote, a ticket or a name belong to more than one person?"** A political question about personhood, sharpened by the election.

Grader's notes: Q2 is strong but tests only the time pillar directly. Q3 is concrete and plays into the election, but it is an answerable legal question, not one with defensible answers on both sides. Q1 holds both: Q2 becomes the time pillar's experiment ("the future shows it was kept, so must I keep it?") and Q3 becomes the cloning pillar's experiment (one vote, one ticket).

**Pick: Q1**, with Q2 and Q3 as the experiments run by time and cloning. The ending takes the side that a promise binds only whoever chooses to take it up, and that the choosing is what makes them a separate person. The other side gets a fair hearing: the city's whole order rests on the belief that the promise binds every body that shares the name.

## Step 2a. Founding crime (three options)

**F1. The hostage in the Guest's house.** In 2090 the founders grew the first human copy, a baby, from their leader, and handed her to the alien as a living surety (*wass*) that they would keep their side of a bargain. They never kept it. To make the handover legal under their own charter, which forbade pledging another person, they declared that a copy *is* its template ("one person"), so the leader had only pledged herself. The whole city's law of copies descends from that cover story.

**F2. The stolen founder.** The woman the city reveres as its founder was a copy who killed her original in the camp and took her place; the doctrine that copies are the same person was written to make that killing self-harm.

**F3. The sold seed.** The founders sold the alien's offspring to foreign buyers and told it the offspring was growing in the city; the city's foreign trade began with that sale.

Grader's notes: F2 is a "the real one was killed by the copy" twist, catalogued and expected (TV Tropes "Tomato in the Mirror" family), and only touches cloning. F3 is a fraud with a vague victim and puts the crime abroad. F1 touches all four pillars: the alien's genome-blindness made the substitution possible, the AGI recorded it, cloning law was built to hide it, and (see the hidden history) a message from the future is why it was sealed the way it was. Its victim is a person who is still alive and still in the house, which gives the present a body to find.

**Pick: F1.** The founding lie is the doctrine of **And Sew** (አንድ ሰው, "one person"): *a body grown from a person is that person.*

## Step 2b. Hidden history (three options)

**H1. The fig and the surety.** The alien is a foundress whose kind reproduces like the fig wasp: it must enter a sealed chamber of the right shape through a narrow mouth, seed and die there. It offered the starving camp its "pollen" (the method of growing bodies in clay and the strange property that lets those bodies send experience backward to earlier bodies of the same genome) in exchange for the chamber. The founders built the chamber, then sealed it, put their own records inside it and set guards on it, because they feared a future-seeing brood. The hostage is the surety for a door that never opened. The founder received a vision from the future in 2090 showing the chamber opened by "her own hand" before "a hall of her bodies", and used it as permission never to open it herself: it had been seen paid.

**H2. The archive mind's victim.** The AGI was built from the recorded mind of an alien that the founders killed for its body; its "ledger" personality is the alien's own counting instinct, and it has been quietly trying to avenge itself through the records.

**H3. The spent children.** The camp survived the Burning Years by growing hundreds of child copies and spending each child's single capacity to receive a message from the future on survival information (where the fires would go). The doctrine of one person was written so that "the leader had only spent her own receipts". The alien was a bystander who taught the method and left.

Grader's notes: H2 is an AGI-built-from-a-victim reveal (adjacent to the "human in a shell" AGI cliché) and turns the AGI into a revenge plot, which the trope table flags. H3 is dark and strong but its victims are a crowd, not a person who can be found, and it leaves the alien decorative. H1 keeps one victim alive in the present, gives the alien a want that is not about Earth's resources, and lets the fixed-future rule supply the founders' excuse, which is the thematic heart.

**Pick: H1**, with H3 kept as *texture* (the city's foresight trade did grow by spending copies' receipts; a later, smaller crime downstream of the first).

## Step 2c. Ending (three options)

**E1. Who pays.** The sealed chamber is opened on election night and the city's founding lie becomes public; that much is fixed, because two people (the founder in 2090 and the steward Almaz thirty-nine days before the election) have already received visions of it. What is not fixed is who pays for it. Someone in the hall must send the founder the vision she received in 2090, and the price of sending a message that far back is every memory you have lived since. The protagonist can pay it herself (losing her whole remembered life, including the child she is raising) or let the hostage pay it (who would lose the only life she ever had). Separately she decides whether to name the killer, who is the woman who raised her. The alien enters its chamber and dies seeding; a hundred days later its successor's first sight is a small copy of the protagonist holding out a campaign fan.

**E2. The new founder.** The protagonist keeps the lie, opens the chamber secretly, and becomes the lineage's new enforcer, carrying the crime forward to protect the child: the dark-crown ending.

**E3. The exodus.** The lie comes out, the city fractures, and the copies leave down the caravan route to found a new camp, mirroring the founding.

Grader's notes: E3 repeats the opening shape at bigger scale (the anti-model). E2 is a strong tragic ending but leaves the external question (what the alien is owed) unresolved, which the checklist forbids. E1 resolves the external question (the chamber opens, the alien seeds), the political question (the doctrine falls on election night), and the thematic one (a promise is kept by whoever chooses it), and it makes the win and the cost one act. Refusal is a respected outcome: if the protagonist does not send, the hostage does.

**Pick: E1.** Its meaning is fixed more firmly than its events: *the future can show that a debt of honour will be paid; it cannot say who must pay it.*

## Step 3. World rules, three options per pillar

### Time travel

**T1. The melikt (መልእክት, "message").** A body grown the clay way (or the person it was grown from) can send the last stretch of its own lived experience, at most one turn of a sixteen-minute hourglass of black sand, back to any earlier moment, into any body of the same genome that was alive then and has never received before. The receiver lives it as one night of fever (*ra'iy*, ራእይ, "vision") and wakes with a rust ring in one iris; a body can receive only once in its life. The sender pays in memory: she loses everything she lived between the target moment and now. History is fixed: every vision ever received was sent.
**T2. The borrowed hour.** Anyone may live an hour twice by borrowing it from their own future; the hour is then missing from the future, and the debt comes due at a random moment.
**T3. Through the alien.** Objects given to the alien arrive in its hands at an earlier time; the alien is the only channel.

Grader's notes: T3 makes the alien a machine for the plot and every time scene runs through one creature. T2 is fun but has no collision with cloning or the AGI and its random due date breaks fair play. T1 is genome-addressed, so it cannot exist without cloning; its fixed history suits a mystery (no evidence is ever invalidated); its cost (memory for reach) creates the climax dilemma; its seeds land (hourglass, fever, rust). **Pick: T1.**
- Hard cap: sixteen minutes, one receipt per body, reach paid in memory.
- Visible cost: the rust ring; a sender who has forgotten years.
- Relationship it damages: Almaz and Aster (a vision sends one copy to kill another). Relationship only it can save: the alien and its offspring (a vision gave the alien seventy-three years of patience).
- Verb no other pillar has: **fever-reading** (reconstruct a degraded first-person vision from the future: date it, place it, find the sender) and **staging** (turn your hourglass and live sixteen minutes knowing they will be sent).

### Cloning

**C1. The clay-born.** A copy is grown from a person's cells in a sealed terracotta jar for about a hundred days and born a baby: a delayed identical twin with no memories, the same genome and different palm lines, scars and bones. Under the doctrine of And Sew all bodies of one genome are one legal person: one name, one vote, one housing-lottery ticket, one guilt. Killing a copy is not murder while another body of the person lives. Bodies grown off the register are *Pagume* (ጳጉሜ, after the small thirteenth month that fits none of the twelve), non-persons.
**C2. The memory copy.** Adult copies printed with the template's memories at sampling.
**C3. The returned.** Copies may only be grown from the dead, and are greeted as the dead returned.

Grader's notes: C2 is the stock "which one is real" engine. C3 is evocative but makes the founder's crime impossible (the hostage was grown from a living woman). C1 is quieter and stranger: it makes the copy question legal and political rather than metaphysical, and it gives the bonesetter her job. **Pick: C1.**
- Hard cap: copies are born babies and remember nothing; growth takes a hundred days in clay.
- Visible cost: shared guilt and shared vote; a copy's single receipt is "spent" by her lineage.
- Damages: Tigist and Almaz (the doctrine makes Almaz's killing legal). Saves: Tigist and Hiwot (the doctrine is why the lineage could not take Hiwot from "herself").
- Verb: **bone-reading** identity attribution among identical bodies, and **passing** (the hostage, playable, must pass as a dead copy among her identical sisters).

### AGI

**A1. The Tsehafi (ጸሐፊ, "the scribe").** The city's record mind: it tallies rations, births, votes and every message sent through time (senders need it to address a receiver). Every night it seals the day into a leaf of iron; a sealed leaf can be woken to testify only by a party to what it records, and a woken leaf rusts and can never be sealed again, so every party can see what was woken. It never lies and never volunteers. Its want: *every record it holds read to the one it concerns.* The founders' first book is the one record it has been forbidden to read to its subject.
**A2. The price-mind.** An AGI that is the market itself, setting prices from the visions it buys.
**A3. The judge.** An AGI that rules on every dispute by the founders' recorded precedents.

Grader's notes: A2 is interesting but makes the AGI a background system with no testimony. A3 is a machine god of law, the "forbidden/evil AI" axis. A1 is a participant with limits and a goal of its own (neither threat nor guardian), and its rust rule turns each question the player asks into a public move the antagonists can see. **Pick: A1.**
- Hard cap: answers only parties; never volunteers; a woken leaf cannot be resealed.
- Damages: privacy between copies (to the Tsehafi, any body of a genome may read that genome's record, so Tigist can read Almaz's night). Saves: the hostage, whose existence it alone recorded.
- Verb: **leaf-waking**, version testimony from sealed iron leaves, each an honest witness confined to its own day.

### Aliens

**X1. The Engida (እንግዳ, "the guest").** A tall, silent, many-pleated being the colour of rust and clay, which arrived in 2089 walking up the salt road with a caravan. It perceives other beings by genome, so it cannot tell copies apart: to it, all 311 bodies of the founder are one person. It speaks only by exchange: it takes an object and gives one back, and the pair is the sentence; it cannot refuse except by returning what it was given. Its kind reproduce like fig-wasp foundresses. Its want: *to enter the chamber it was promised, seed, and end there.*
**X2. The dyer.** An alien that grows living patterns on cloth and speaks through them.
**X
3. The bond-seer.** An alien that sees obligations between people as visible threads, and wants them all cut.

Grader's notes: X3 is a moral instrument rather than a mind, and its want is a thesis. X2 is visually rich but a cloth language is close to reading, and gives no collision with cloning. X1's genome-perception is what made the founding crime possible (the alien could not see that the baby was not the founder), its exchange-speech is a text-free verb, and its fig-wasp want is about its own kind, not Earth. It never communicates by sound; it is silent. **Pick: X1.**
- Hard cap: exchange only; genome-blind; it cannot take without giving.
- Damages: every body of the founder's genome (to it they are all the one who promised). Saves: Hiwot's future (its gift makes her the city's first fluent speaker of exchange).
- Verb: **exchange**, asking and answering by trading objects.

### The collision

The four rules interlock, and each needs another: visions travel only within a genome (time needs cloning); senders need the Tsehafi to address a receiver (time needs the AGI); the Tsehafi follows the doctrine and treats a genome as one account (AGI needs cloning law); copies and visions exist only because of the alien's method (cloning and time need the alien); the alien cannot tell copies apart (the alien needs cloning to be fooled). Together they raise a question none raises alone: *if the law, the ledger, the alien and the future all say that 311 women are one person who has already kept her promise, who in that hall actually has to keep it?*

The one admitted cheat, spent on the premise: why clay-grown bodies can send experience backward is never explained. Everything the plot uses is the rule of use, never the physics.

## Step 4. Factions and names

Factions are derived in `03-world-and-factions.md`. Here are the name rounds for the things the player hears most.

**The city.** (a) *Amole*, after the salt bars it was founded to trade; (b) *Wuqro-Hager*, "carved country"; (c) *Addis Qetema*, "new town". (b) is close to real place names and generic; (c) is bland and too near Addis Ababa. **Pick: Amole.**

**The founder's lineage** (311 living bodies). The founder is **Lemlem Bekele** (ለምለም, "verdant"; her father's name Bekele, "it sprouted"). Ethiopian names are a given name plus the father's given name. Options for how copies are named: (a) copies take the template's name as their "father's name", so every copy is legally *Lemlem Lemlem*, as if she were her own parent; (b) copies are numbered; (c) copies take the name of the jar-house that grew them. (a) is derived from the real patronymic rule, encodes the doctrine in every name, and sounds strange in the right way. **Pick (a).** Copies use personal *use-names*; the lineage is called "the Lemlem".

**The protagonist** (a bonesetter, a Lemlem copy, 34). (a) **Tigist** (ትዕግስት, "patience", the bonesetter's virtue); (b) Meskerem (the first month: a beginning); (c) Almaz (diamond). **Pick: Tigist.** Almaz moves to her foster mother.

**The child** (Tigist's assigned younger copy, 9). (a) **Hiwot** ("life"); (b) Selam ("peace"); (c) Tsion ("Zion"). Selam is overused in fiction; Tsion leans religious. **Pick: Hiwot.**

**The hostage.** (a) She has no given name; the caravaneers call her "the Guest's Shadow" and she has named herself **Sitota** (ስጦታ, "gift"), learned when Tigist finds the object the alien uses for her; (b) she is named for the date she was pledged; (c) she uses the founder's name. **Pick (a)**: the name is a small reveal and carries the theme.

**The AGI.** (a) **the Tsehafi** ("scribe", the old royal chronicler's title, *tsehafi tezaz*); its first designation, in the Tunnelers' Korean, was *Sokgi-1* ("shorthand one"), which is why it speaks with a stenographer's cadence; (b) *the Mezgeb* ("register"); (c) *Hasab* ("count"). (b) is the bland "Register" label the brief warns against. **Pick (a).**

**The alien.** (a) **the Engida** ("guest"); (b) *the Foundress*, the scholars' word; (c) *Amole-Abat*, "father of salt", the caravaneers' name. Keep all three as faction usages (city, scholars, caravan), with *Engida* as the default, which gives Tolkien's "multiple hands" for free.

**The stenographer.** (a) **Jae-won Tesfaye**, a Tunneler: Korean given name from his mother's line, Ethiopian patronymic from his father Tesfaye; (b) Yonas Girma; (c) Min-seo Haile. (b) loses the Tunneler strand; (c) is fine but a woman would make the cast nearly all women in a lineage story that already is. **Pick (a).**

**Others.** Almaz (the lineage's *Azaj*, household steward and enforcer; Tigist's foster mother). Tsehay ("sun"; speaker of the elders). Aster ("star"; the elder who visits the Engida; the first body found). Fitawrari Dawit Asrat (head of the military line). Dahir (master of the salt caravan). All checked against the generic sci-fi pool: none match.

**In-world terms.** Elders' council: *the Shimagile* (ሽማግሌ, "elders", the traditional mediators), not "the Council". Court: *the Chilot*. Election: *the Mircha*. Vision: *ra'iy*. Message: *melikt*. Non-registered copies: *Pagume*. Caravaneers: *the Arho*. Foreign buyers: *the ferenj houses*. The carved record tower: *Bete Tsehafi*. The promised chamber: *the fig* (the Arho call it *the jar that was never opened*).

## Step 12. Key scenes (three options each for the anchors)

**Opening image.**
- O1. Dawn in the Narrow: the alien is giving away everything it owns, one object to each passer-by, and the trench fills with people holding strange things; at its door, the veiled Shadow lies dead.
- O2. A televised housing-lottery draw in which the Lemlem's single ticket wins, and 311 identical women stand up in 311 trench cafes.
- O3. A caravan entering the Narrow at night, salt bars swaying, and one camel carrying a body.
Grader: O2 is a superb establishing image of the doctrine, but contains no question. O3 has a question but is a body-in-transit cliché. O1 has the question (who is she, and why is the alien emptying its house?) and its meaning flips twice. **Pick O1**, preceded by a short "normal" prologue the day before (Outer Wilds' lesson: players must know normal before it breaks). O2 is kept as the end of Act 1's first chapter.

**Midpoint.**
- M1. Tigist sets the wrist of the elder "Aster" and feels, under her hands, the bones of a woman who has carried loads for seventy years: Aster is the hostage.
- M2. The Tsehafi's second book is woken and the founder's own voice says she saw the debt paid.
- M3. Almaz is unmasked as the killer.
Grader: all three are needed. M1 is the most playable and the purest "read the bone, not the cloth" moment, and it flips the investigation from a death into a living conspiracy, so it is the midpoint. M3 closes Act 2; M2 is the centre of Act 3.

**Climactic image.**
- C1. 311 identical women in identical election cloth in the hall of Bete Tsehafi as the fig is unsealed; the alien walks in from the trench, through the crowd, and folds itself into the terracotta mouth.
- C2. The ballot count projected on the rock wall flipping as each copy's vote is counted separately.
- C3. Tigist alone with the hourglass, choosing.
Grader: C2 is information, not image; C3 is interior. C1 holds the ritual, the alien's want, the lineage and the founding crime in one frame. **Pick C1**, with C3 as the beat that follows it.

**Final image (three).** (a) Hiwot, a hundred days later, holding a campaign fan out to the newborn offspring as it opens; (b) the Tsehafi printing Sitota's first lottery ticket; (c) Tigist's hands finding the old break in Hiwot's arm that her memory has lost. All three are kept: (b) and (c) are the codas of their branches, (a) is the shared last image in every branch.

## Revisions by the second Writer B (while sealing the hidden history)

The picks above stand. Sealing the chronology in `02-hidden-history.md` exposed seven gaps, fixed here so that this file and the solution document agree.

1. **Two receivers of the hall, not three.** E1 said three people had received visions of the opening. Only two receipts have a story job: the founder's (2090, the debt) and Almaz's (2163, the murder's cause). A third would need a third sender in the hall and a loop that does nothing, so the count is corrected above.
2. **Reach limit added to T1.** A message cannot land before the sender's genome was first sealed in a jar (for the Lemlem, 1 December 2089). Without it, a copy could message its template's childhood and the founding crime could be rewritten by anyone. This also makes the founder's 2090 fever the first vision in history, which is lore the game can use.
3. **Failed sends.** A send addressed to a body that never received simply fails: the glass runs, nothing goes and the sender loses nothing. This is how the city knows history is fixed, and it is taught in play before the climax depends on it. The Tsehafi keeps a public register of **open receipts** (fevers whose senders have not yet sent): the city's ledger of debts owed to the past.
4. **What the cost takes.** The sender loses lived (episodic) memory between the target moment and the send. Language and skills stay: "the hands keep what the hands learned." The final image (c) needs this, so it is planted early (the glass-widow Etagegn).
5. **Election date.** The Mircha is held on Pagume 5, 2155 in the city's Ethiopian calendar, which is **11 September 2163**. All sealed dates are given in the Gregorian calendar so they can be checked.
6. **Opening image clarified.** The veiled dead woman at the Guest's door is *taken* for the Shadow by everyone, the player included. She is Aster, who had swapped places with the hostage hours earlier. This is what M1 already implied; it is now explicit.
7. **A low-stakes rehearsal of "who pays".** The checklist requires the player to operate sending before the climax relies on it. Act II now contains a small fixed receipt (a teenage copy's vision of a glass room) that someone must send, at the cost of nine days of memory, with three possible payers and refusal respected. It is the ending in miniature, and it delivers a key clue.
