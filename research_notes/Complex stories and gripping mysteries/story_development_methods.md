# Story Development Methods: From a Single Idea to a Full Outline

Scope: step-by-step methods professionals use to grow a story from one idea into a full outline, with a focus on staged expansion. The notes are written to support planning an original, very complex mystery video game story (AGI, aliens, cloning, time travel, many interwoven character threads). All text is paraphrased. Direct quotes are under 15 words, one per source at most. Anything I could not source is listed under Gaps and marked as unverified.

---

## 1. Randy Ingermanson's Snowflake Method: the ten steps

### Takeaway
The Snowflake Method is the clearest example of staged expansion: one sentence, then a paragraph, then a page, then four pages, then a scene spreadsheet. Character documents are built in parallel with the plot documents, and each stage is an expansion of the one before. Ingermanson estimates about 100 hours of design work and says to expect to go back and revise earlier steps.

### Cited Findings
- **Overall budget.** Ingermanson estimates roughly 100 hours of design work and about 150 more hours for a solid first draft. He contrasts that with roughly 500 hours for a draft written without a plan. — [Advanced Fiction Writing: Snowflake Method](https://www.advancedfictionwriting.com/articles/snowflake-method/)
- **Step 1: one-sentence summary** (about 1 hour). Under 15 words, with no character names. It captures the big picture and what the protagonist stands to lose, and doubles as a pitch line. — [Snowflake Method](https://www.advancedfictionwriting.com/articles/snowflake-method/)
- **Step 2: one-paragraph summary** (about 1 hour). About five sentences: the setup, three "disasters," and the ending. Each disaster takes up roughly a quarter of the book, and the ending takes the last quarter.
  - Disaster 1 ends Act 1 and may come from outside events.
  - Disasters 2 (the midpoint of Act 2) and 3 (the end of Act 2) should be caused by the protagonist's own attempts to fix things, so the situation keeps getting worse.
  - Disaster 3 forces the move into Act 3.
  - Back-cover copy should cover only the first quarter of the story. — [Snowflake Method](https://www.advancedfictionwriting.com/articles/snowflake-method/)
- **Step 3: character summaries** (about 1 page and 1 hour per major character). Fields:
  - name
  - one-sentence storyline
  - motivation (the abstract want)
  - goal (the concrete want)
  - conflict (what blocks the goal)
  - epiphany (what the character learns and how they change)
  - one-paragraph storyline — [Snowflake Method](https://www.advancedfictionwriting.com/articles/snowflake-method/)
- **Step 4: expand each sentence of step 2 into a paragraph.** The result is about one single-spaced page. Every paragraph except the last ends in a disaster, and the last paragraph is the ending. Steps 3 and 4 together take a day or two, and the page can serve as a proposal synopsis. — [Snowflake Method](https://www.advancedfictionwriting.com/articles/snowflake-method/)
- **Step 5: character synopses** (a day or two). The story retold from each character's point of view: one page for each major character, half a page for each other important character. Ingermanson now sends these in proposals in place of a plot synopsis. — [Snowflake Method](https://www.advancedfictionwriting.com/articles/snowflake-method/)
- **Step 6: four-page synopsis** (about a week). Each step-4 paragraph grows into a full page. This is where the high-level logic and strategic decisions get settled, and where revising earlier steps is expected. — [Snowflake Method](https://www.advancedfictionwriting.com/articles/snowflake-method/)
- **Step 7: full character charts** (about a week, no set length). Birthdate, appearance, history, motivation, goal, and how the character has changed by the end. Ingermanson says steps 1–7 can take a full month of focused work. — [Snowflake Method](https://www.advancedfictionwriting.com/articles/snowflake-method/)
- **Step 8: scene list as a spreadsheet** (about a week, often more than 100 rows). One row per scene. Columns: POV character, a wide "what happens" column, an optional estimated page count, and later a chapter number. Ingermanson values it because it shows the whole storyline at a glance and makes scenes easy to reorder. — [Snowflake Method](https://www.advancedfictionwriting.com/articles/snowflake-method/)
- **Step 9: narrative scene descriptions** (optional, about a week). Each row expands into several paragraphs with dialogue ideas and the scene's core conflict. A scene with no conflict either gets one or is cut. Ingermanson once wrote about 50 pages of these (1–2 per chapter) as a "prototype first draft," but no longer does this step. — [Snowflake Method](https://www.advancedfictionwriting.com/articles/snowflake-method/)
- **Step 10: first draft.** Drafting goes faster because the large-scale structure is already settled, leaving only small logic problems. Around the midpoint of drafting, go back and fix whatever has broken in the design documents. — [Snowflake Method](https://www.advancedfictionwriting.com/articles/snowflake-method/)
- **Iteration is expected.** Ingermanson treats the documents as living, and revising earlier steps as normal. Character work often exposes plot problems, and fixing them on paper is far cheaper than fixing a manuscript. He also recommends the method for reorganizing a messy draft or planning an editor's revision request. — [Snowflake Method](https://www.advancedfictionwriting.com/articles/snowflake-method/)

### Inferences
- **Fit for a complex mystery game:** good, with changes.
  - Step 5 (the story told from each character's viewpoint) maps naturally onto interwoven character threads. For a mystery, one synopsis should be written from the culprit's or hidden actor's side: the "true" story the player has to reconstruct.
  - The step 8 spreadsheet is the natural place to add mystery-specific columns: clue planted, red herring, what the player knows, what is actually true, and timeline position (essential for time travel and cloning).
- **Weak spot:** the "three disasters" spine assumes a single protagonist arc. A game with several threads probably needs the step 2 paragraph written once for the master plot and once for each major thread.

### Gaps
- I read Ingermanson's own article, not his book (*How to Write a Novel Using the Snowflake Method*, 2014), which tells the method as a parable and may differ in detail.

## 2. Loglines and premise (Snyder, Egri, Cron)

### Takeaway
The professional first stage is a one-to-two-sentence statement of the story's engine. Snyder's screenwriting discipline applies the same test to every beat. Cron's Story Genius approach rejects starting from plot: it starts from the protagonist's misbelief and requires every scene to advance the internal and external story together (the "third rail").

### Cited Findings
- **Snyder's beat-level test.** Snyder held that if you cannot state a beat in one or two sentences, you do not yet have that beat. The logline is the same discipline applied to the whole story. — [Reedsy: Save the Cat Beat Sheet](https://reedsy.com/blog/guide/story-structure/save-the-cat-beat-sheet/)
- **Cron's Story Genius sequence** (as summarized by StorytellingDB), in this order:
  1. a one-sentence core message
  2. a "what if" premise that links the external stakes to inner growth
  3. who the protagonist is at the start
  4. their surface want
  5. the misbelief: a flawed worldview, usually formed early in life
  6. its origin — [StorytellingDB: Story Genius Method](https://storytellingdb.com/story-genius-method-lisa-cron/)
- **Origin scenes.** The writer drafts a misbelief origin scene of about 2,000 words in first person. It shows the healthy belief that was replaced, the expectation going in, how that expectation was shattered, and the meaning the character took from it. Further scenes show the misbelief being reinforced at crossroads moments. These are backstory tools and may never appear in the book. — [StorytellingDB](https://storytellingdb.com/story-genius-method-lisa-cron/)
- **The third rail.** This is Cron's term for the live connection between the inner struggle and the outer plot. Every scene should advance both. The summary's example is McClane crossing broken glass barefoot in *Die Hard*. — [StorytellingDB](https://storytellingdb.com/story-genius-method-lisa-cron/)
- **Plot comes after character.** The external problem is designed to force the protagonist to confront the misbelief. Escalating obstacles are then listed, and each plot point is tested by whether it challenges the misbelief. — [StorytellingDB](https://storytellingdb.com/story-genius-method-lisa-cron/)
- **Scene-by-scene cause-effect matrix.** For each scene: (1) the external event, (2) how the protagonist reads it through the misbelief, (3) the action they take, (4) the consequence, which becomes the cause of the next scene. Scene cards track external events and internal reactions side by side. — [StorytellingDB](https://storytellingdb.com/story-genius-method-lisa-cron/)

### Inferences
- For a twist-heavy game, Cron's misbelief is a useful device. The player character's (or player's) misbelief about the world can double as the mystery's central false assumption, for example "the AGI is the enemy" or "I am the original, not the clone." The reveal then works as plot twist and character epiphany at once.
- Cron's four-question matrix is a ready-made format for per-scene cards.

### Gaps
- **Lajos Egri's premise** (*The Art of Dramatic Writing*): I did not reach a source within the tool budget. From general knowledge, unverified: Egri's premise is a provable thesis in the form "character + conflict + conclusion," and the play exists to prove it. Verify before relying on this.
- **Snyder's logline components:** I could not source the specific elements (irony, a compelling mental picture, audience and cost, a killer title) or Snyder's own logline checklist.
- **Professional logline practice:** I did not fetch a source on wider screenwriting logline practice (protagonist + inciting incident + goal + stakes/antagonist).

## 3. Beat-sheet and structure templates

### Takeaway
The templates differ in how many anchor points they fix and in what they assume about the protagonist. Snyder pins down 15 beats. Wells needs only 7 and is designed backward from the end. Harmon's 8 form a repeatable circle. Truby's 22 are offered as ingredients, not a sequence. Kishōtenketsu needs no conflict. Professionals mostly treat templates as scaffolding: they warn that following beats rigidly produces stale stories, and that most templates assume one protagonist who changes.

### Cited Findings
- **Save the Cat, 15 beats.** Page numbers are for a roughly 110-page script, with Reedsy's novel percentages in brackets.

  | # | Beat | Placement | What happens |
  |---|------|-----------|--------------|
  | 1 | Opening Image | p.1 | Snapshot of the hero's current world |
  | 2 | Theme Stated | p.5 | The theme is voiced, often by another character |
  | 3 | Set-Up | pp.1–10 | Status quo and the hero's flaws |
  | 4 | Catalyst | p.12 [10%] | One event sets the story moving |
  | 5 | Debate | pp.12–25 | The hero hesitates |
  | 6 | Break Into Two | p.25 | The hero commits to the new world |
  | 7 | B Story | p.30 | A helper or relationship subplot starts |
  | 8 | Fun and Games | pp.30–55 | The premise delivers |
  | 9 | Midpoint | p.55 [50%] | False victory or twist; stakes rise |
  | 10 | Bad Guys Close In | pp.55–75 | Pressure mounts |
  | 11 | All Is Lost | p.75 [75%] | Rock bottom |
  | 12 | Dark Night of the Soul | pp.75–85 | The hero reflects on what went wrong |
  | 13 | Break Into Three | p.85 | New resolve and a new approach |
  | 14 | Finale | pp.85–110 | The central conflict is resolved |
  | 15 | Final Image | p.110 | Mirrors the opening image |

  Reedsy's own page-to-percentage conversions do not always match (Break Into Two is listed as both p.25 and 30%), so treat placements as approximate. — [Reedsy](https://reedsy.com/blog/guide/story-structure/save-the-cat-beat-sheet/)
- **Criticism of Save the Cat** (from Reedsy's contributors):
  - One editor saw a writer's work grow staler and less distinctive after adopting the beat sheet, and advised against following it beat for beat.
  - Another said structure provides familiarity and momentum, while originality comes from specific characters, world and choices.
  - Novels have more room in placement and length than screenplays. — [Reedsy](https://reedsy.com/blog/guide/story-structure/save-the-cat-beat-sheet/)
- **Dan Harmon's story circle, 8 steps:**
  1. You (a comfort zone)
  2. Need (a want is sparked)
  3. Go (enter an unfamiliar situation)
  4. Search (adapt and pursue)
  5. Find (get what they wanted; Dabble calls this a false win at the bottom of the circle)
  6. Take (pay a price; beliefs are reconsidered)
  7. Return (to the familiar, not necessarily a physical place)
  8. Change (show the transformation; Dabble calls this the most important step)

  The idea behind it is that audiences respond to cyclical stories. — [Dabble: Story Circle](https://www.dabblewriter.com/articles/the-story-circle)
- **Dan Wells' Seven-Point Structure, built backward:**
  - Build order: first the Resolution (the end state), then the Hook (the opposite starting state), then the Plot Turns and Pinches that connect them.
  - Story order:
    1. Hook
    2. Plot Turn 1 (the call to adventure)
    3. Pinch 1 (pressure forces action)
    4. Midpoint (the shift from reacting to acting)
    5. Pinch 2 (things turn dire, e.g. a mentor is lost)
    6. Plot Turn 2 (the last piece of information needed to win)
    7. Resolution
  - Wells applies the same seven points separately to individual subplots, such as a romance, and to the arc inside a single scene. — [Writing Excuses 7.41](https://writingexcuses.com/writing-excuses-7-41-seven-point-story-structure/)
- **Truby's building blocks** (from Arc Studio's list, which names 21 of the 22):
  - Self-revelation/need/desire
  - Ghost and story world
  - Weakness and need
  - Inciting event
  - Desire
  - Ally
  - **Opponent and/or Mystery**
  - Fake-ally opponent
  - First revelation and decision
  - Plan
  - Opponent's plan and main counterattack
  - Drive
  - Attack by ally
  - Apparent defeat
  - Second revelation and decision
  - **Audience revelation**
  - Third revelation and decision
  - Gate/gauntlet/visit to death
  - Self-revelation
  - Moral decision
  - New equilibrium

  Arc Studio describes these as "a list of ingredients," not a fixed order. — [Arc Studio Pro: Truby's 22 Building Blocks](https://www.arcstudiopro.com/blog/22-building-blocks-john-truby)
- **Syd Field and Vogler** (as summarized by Dramatica):
  - Field's paradigm is Setup / Confrontation / Resolution, with an inciting incident, a midpoint, and a climax.
  - Vogler's journey runs from Ordinary World and Call to Adventure through Ordeal and Resurrection to Return with the Elixir. Dramatica reads it as four acts disguised as three. — [Dramatica: Comparison of Seven Story Paradigms](https://dramatica.com/theory/articles/a-comparison-of-seven-story-paradigms)
- **Dramatica's critique** (a competing theory, so read it as partisan): these paradigms assume the main character is also the protagonist, changes, and wins. They track only one or two throughlines, and the author calls them "structurally rigid." — [Dramatica](https://dramatica.com/theory/articles/a-comparison-of-seven-story-paradigms)
- **Kishōtenketsu:**
  - Ki: introduce
  - Shō: develop, deepening without raising the stakes
  - Ten: a twist or turn, which can be as small as a change of viewpoint
  - Ketsu: a conclusion that reconciles the twist with what came before

  It does not depend on conflict. — [Medium: Kishotenketsu Part 2](https://medium.com/@hafiztajuddin/story-structure-kishotenketsu-part-2-fbda733d5382)

### Inferences
- **Best fit for a multi-thread twist game:**
  - Truby, because his list explicitly includes "Mystery," a "Fake-Ally Opponent," and an "Audience Revelation," and allows several revelations.
  - Wells' seven points, because they are designed backward and can be applied per thread. Each character thread can get its own Hook → Resolution, with Pinches and Turns slotted into a master timeline.
- **Harmon's circle suits episodic or chapter-level units.** Each game chapter or character route can be one circle, and the circle can repeat.
- **Kishōtenketsu's "ten" fits well for reframing reveals,** where a change of viewpoint recontextualizes everything (for example, revealing the narrator is a clone).
- **Save the Cat and Field fit a single protagonist and a fixed runtime.** They are less useful as the master spine of a non-linear, multi-route game, though they can still check pacing inside one route.

### Gaps
- I did not fetch Harmon's original Channel 101 posts.
- I could not source Syd Field's page-count plot points (traditionally put at about p.25–27 and p.85–90 of a 120-page script; unverified here).
- I could not source Vogler's full 12 stages from a fetched page.
- I could not source Truby's view of "mechanical" three-act structure, his 7 key steps, or his premise and designing-principle process.

## 4. Outlining versus discovery writing

### Takeaway
Sanderson frames plot as promises made, progress shown, and payoffs delivered, and he distinguishes the big story-level Plot from chapter-level plot. Martin calls himself a "gardener" who knows the key landmarks but discovers the route between them. That hybrid, with fixed landmarks and a discovered path, is the common professional middle ground.

### Cited Findings
- **Sanderson's lecture 2 (2025), from student notes:**
  - Promise: the expectations a story sets up.
  - Progress: how the story signals movement. Types include information/puzzle, relationship, and external action.
  - Payoff: delivering on the promises, ideally both surprising and logical.
  - He separates "Big P Plot" (the story in a sentence or two) from "little p plot" (chapter-level problems), and covers tone promises, an opening "microcosm" that previews the whole story, and outlining versus discovery writing. — [Knowt notes on Sanderson Lecture 2](https://knowt.com/note/2dcde185-d0cc-4fa1-8bd6-ac2c590a4800/Brandon-Sanderson-Lecture-2)
- **Martin's architects and gardeners:**
  - Architects plan in advance. Gardeners discover the story's beats while writing.
  - Martin says he is "mostly a gardener when I write a novel."
  - He knows the major landmarks, which are fixed, and enjoys finding his way between them. — [Winter Is Coming](https://winteriscoming.net/2017/03/24/small-council-gardeners-vs-architects-whats-the-better-way-to-write-fiction)

### Inferences
- **A mystery needs at least the architect layer for the solution and clue logic.** Discovery writing can still work for character voice and scene texture inside a locked frame.
- **Sanderson's "progress" types matter most here.** "Information/puzzle" progress is the main kind in a mystery game: each chapter should visibly move the player's understanding forward.
- **Ways to keep surprise while outlining** (my synthesis, not a single sourced claim):
  - Outline the payoffs, and discover the route to them (Martin).
  - Keep candidate solutions open late (Christie; see section 5).
  - Design payoffs to be surprising yet logical (Sanderson).

### Gaps
- **Stephen King, *On Writing*:** I found no source within the tool budget. From general knowledge, unverified: King distrusts plotting, starts from a "situation" or "what if," likens stories to fossils that are dug up rather than built, and lets characters drive. Verify before use.
- **Sanderson's specifics:** I could not source his "outline plot, character and setting separately" breakdown or his bullet-point outline style.

## 5. Designing from the ending and reverse outlining (mysteries and twists)

### Takeaway
Twist writers tend to fix the final effect or revelation first and build backward. Poe argued this explicitly, and Uchikoshi works this way. Christie is a notable exception: she often kept the murderer's identity open until late. Her method was generating and discarding alternatives, not a single backward pass.

### Cited Findings
- **Poe, "The Philosophy of Composition":**
  - A plot worth the name must be worked out to its ending before writing begins. Keeping the conclusion in view is what gives events their sense of consequence.
  - Poe begins by choosing the effect he wants, then decides whether incident, tone, or both will produce it.
  - He cites Dickens's claim that Godwin wrote *Caleb Williams* backward. He doubts it, but endorses the principle.
  - For "The Raven," he says he fixed the length, effect, tone and refrain first, wrote the climactic stanza first, and built the earlier stanzas toward it. — [Lit2Go: Philosophy of Composition](https://etc.usf.edu/lit2go/147/the-works-of-edgar-allan-poe/5371/the-philosophy-of-composition)
- **Uchikoshi (Zero Escape):**
  - He decides the twist or ending first and works backward. His examples are 999's reveal that they were not on a ship and Virtue's Last Reward's moon reveal. He did the same for Zero Escape 3 so that everything fits together.
  - He builds the overall story first, then designs props and gimmicks, which become the puzzles. — [Anime News Network interview, 2015](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/feature/2015-08-13/interview-zero-escape-series-creator-kotaro-uchikoshi/.91629)
- **Christie's notebooks** (John Curran, as relayed):
  - 73 notebooks, about 7,000 pages, covering 1920–1976, with notes for nearly every novel and a section of unused ideas. — [Irish Times](https://www.irishtimes.com/culture/books/the-secrets-of-agatha-christie-s-success-1.2820812)
  - Curran: the murderer's identity was rarely settled at the start of plotting. — [Present Tense (Substack), citing Curran's *Murder in the Making*](https://presenttense.substack.com/p/agatha-christies-working-notebooks)
  - She generated, selected, rejected and polished options. Some plans ran to about 75–100 pages. Ideas sat unused for years and were recycled, sometimes beyond recognition.
  - Her use of the notebooks was random (whatever blank page came next, sometimes writing from the back), and Curran describes her as thriving on that chaos. — [Present Tense](https://presenttense.substack.com/p/agatha-christies-working-notebooks); [Irish Times](https://www.irishtimes.com/culture/books/the-secrets-of-agatha-christie-s-success-1.2820812)
- **Christie's puzzle design:**
  - Her complicated-looking puzzles often resolve into one simple fact that makes every other clue fall into place.
  - Her misdirection exploits everyday truths readers already know: names that can belong to either sex, narrators who may be unreliable, mirrors that reverse images. — [Irish Times](https://www.irishtimes.com/culture/books/the-secrets-of-agatha-christie-s-success-1.2820812)

### Inferences
- **A recommended pipeline for the game,** combining the sources:
  1. Fix the central revelations first (Poe, Uchikoshi, Wells). This includes the "one simple fact" each major twist rests on (Christie).
  2. Write the hidden "true" chronology.
  3. Write the player-facing "apparent" story.
  4. Derive clues as the places where the true timeline leaks into the apparent one.
  5. Explore alternatives Christie-style: generate several candidate culprits or solutions and keep them open while the clue set is designed. That way clues can point plausibly at several answers (red herrings).
- **Uchikoshi's order** (twist → story → props → puzzles) translates directly to game design: the puzzles serve the reveal.
- **Time travel and cloning make the "true chronology" document essential.** It is effectively a reverse outline, kept separately from the presentation order.

### Gaps
- I could not confirm the claim that Christie's plans used lettered scene lists; the source I reached did not mention them.
- I found no sourced, general "plant clues forward" checklist from a named mystery author. The fair-play rules of Knox and Van Dine were not researched.

## 6. Tools of expansion: grids, spreadsheets, cards, timelines, bibles

### Takeaway
The most-cited professional tool for multi-thread plotting is Rowling's hand-drawn grid: one row per chapter, a timeline column, and one column per subplot, including events that happen off-page. Ingermanson's step 8 spreadsheet is the digital equivalent. Both make it possible to see every thread at once and reorder scenes.

### Cited Findings
- **Rowling's grid for *Order of the Phoenix*:**
  - Columns: chapter number, month, chapter title, plot outline.
  - Then six subplot columns: Prophecy; Cho/Ginny; D.A. (Dumbledore's Army); O of P (the Order); Snape/Harry; Hagrid and Grawp.
  - Rows appear to be chapters. Cells record what each subplot is doing in that chapter, including off-page developments. — [Electric Literature](https://electricliterature.com/j-k-rowlings-spreadsheet-shows-how-she-wrote-harry-potter/)
- **Ingermanson's scene spreadsheet:** columns for POV, what happens, page estimate, and chapter. It shows the whole storyline at a glance and makes reordering easy. — [Snowflake Method](https://www.advancedfictionwriting.com/articles/snowflake-method/)
- **South Park's board:** the writers' room lays the episode outline out on a board in three acts, and every scene has to work as a funny sketch on its own. — [Cheatsheet](https://www.cheatsheet.com/entertainment/south-park-trey-parker-and-matt-stone-1-rule.html/)
- **Cron's scene cards:** each card tracks the external event alongside the internal reaction. — [StorytellingDB](https://storytellingdb.com/story-genius-method-lisa-cron/)

### Inferences
- **A Rowling-style grid adapted for the game:**
  - Rows: chapters, or scenes/routes.
  - Columns: in-world date (plus a "true time" column for time travel); the main mystery; one column per character thread (AGI, alien contact, clone, and so on); clues planted; clues paid off; player knowledge state.
  - Rowling tracked off-page events too. That habit is especially important when hidden actors (an AGI, clones) act off-screen.
- **A story bible should hold:**
  - the canonical true timeline
  - the rules of time travel and cloning (Sanderson-style promises the logic must keep)
  - the character charts (Snowflake step 7)

### Gaps
- **The "W" plot diagram:** I did not find a sourced description. It is commonly described as tracking alternating highs and lows (two dips, a midpoint peak, a climax), but that is unverified.
- **Index cards and corkboards:** I found no specific sourced professional account beyond the South Park board.
- **Story bibles:** I found no sourced professional account.

## 7. Testing an outline before drafting

### Takeaway
The most concrete, widely cited test is Parker and Stone's: if "and then" fits between two beats, the outline is weak, and the beats should be joined by "therefore" or "but." The other sourced tests are Snyder's one-or-two-sentence beat test, Ingermanson's "cut any scene without conflict," and Cron's "does each plot point challenge the misbelief."

### Cited Findings
- **Parker and Stone, 2011 NYU class (aired on mtvU's *Stand In*).** Read the outline beats in order. If "and then" fits between them, the story is a dull sequence. Each link should be "therefore" or "but," because causation is what makes one beat drive the next. — [Cheatsheet](https://www.cheatsheet.com/entertainment/south-park-trey-parker-and-matt-stone-1-rule.html/)
- **Snyder:** a beat that cannot be stated in one or two sentences is not yet a beat. — [Reedsy](https://reedsy.com/blog/guide/story-structure/save-the-cat-beat-sheet/)
- **Ingermanson:**
  - In step 9, a scene with no conflict either gets one or is cut.
  - In step 2, disasters 2 and 3 should come from the hero's own attempts to fix things. That doubles as a check on causality. — [Snowflake Method](https://www.advancedfictionwriting.com/articles/snowflake-method/)
- **Cron:** test every plot point by whether it challenges the protagonist's misbelief, and chain scenes so that each consequence becomes the next scene's cause. — [StorytellingDB](https://storytellingdb.com/story-genius-method-lisa-cron/)
- **Sanderson:** check that every promise gets a payoff that is both surprising and logical. — [Knowt notes on Sanderson Lecture 2](https://knowt.com/note/2dcde185-d0cc-4fa1-8bd6-ac2c590a4800/Brandon-Sanderson-Lecture-2)

### Inferences
- **A checklist for the game outline,** built from the sources:
  1. Read the one-sentence summary and each thread's one-liner aloud.
  2. Run "therefore/but" across the beats of each thread and across thread intersections.
  3. Check that every scene row has conflict.
  4. Check that every twist's "one simple fact" is planted at least once before the reveal, and that the player is shown it fairly.
  5. Check that every promise (rule of time travel, AGI capability, clone mystery) has a payoff row.
  6. Check that every subplot column touches the theme or the protagonist's misbelief.

### Gaps
- I found no sourced professional statement of the specific test that "every subplot touches the theme." It is consistent with Cron's and Truby's approaches but was not directly sourced.
- I found no source on table reads or reading the logline aloud as formal practice.
