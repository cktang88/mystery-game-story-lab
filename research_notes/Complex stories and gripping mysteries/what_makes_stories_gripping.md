# What Makes Stories Gripping and Memorable (craft theory + psychology), for a complex mystery game

Source-quality note for the report writer: psychology items come from primary papers or university/academic summaries where possible. Several craft-book items (McKee, Truby, Storr, Cron, Aristotle) come from reputable secondary summaries or reader notes rather than the books themselves; these are flagged "(secondary)". All wording is paraphrase unless in quotation marks.

## 1. What does research say about curiosity, suspense and surprise?

### Takeaway
Curiosity is an aversive itch created by a salient, important, nearly-closable gap in knowledge; suspense and surprise are produced mainly by the ORDER in which a story releases information, not by the events themselves. The popular claims "cliffhangers work because of the Zeigarnik effect" and "spoilers improve stories" are both weaker than commonly stated: the urge to RESUME unfinished things is robust, the memory advantage is not, and spoiler findings conflict.

### Cited Findings

**Information-gap theory (Loewenstein; Golman & Loewenstein)**
- Curiosity is modelled as desire for information for its own sake; it arises when a question has been activated in someone's mind but the answer is uncertain, and that uncertainty is unpleasant even if the answer changes nothing practical. — [Golman & Loewenstein, "Curiosity, Information Gaps, and the Utility of Knowledge" (CMU)](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/golman/golman_loewenstein_curiosity.pdf)
- Three drivers raise curiosity: importance (the answer matters to the person, e.g. bears on self-image), salience (the gap is made vivid; their example escalates from a test not yet taken, to taken, to scored, to someone visibly holding the score), and "epiphany" potential (one piece of information that would resolve several open questions at once is wanted most). — [Golman & Loewenstein](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/golman/golman_loewenstein_curiosity.pdf)
- Curiosity vanishes when the person feels already certain of the answer; no perceived uncertainty, no pull. — [Golman & Loewenstein](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/golman/golman_loewenstein_curiosity.pdf)
- Satisfaction is short-lived: after an answer lands, attention to that question drops (their "belief resolution effect"), so gossip is "eagerly received and soon forgotten." Surprise at the answer briefly spikes attention, then fades. — [Golman & Loewenstein](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/golman/golman_loewenstein_curiosity.pdf)
- Evidence they cite: Kang et al. (2009) found curiosity tracked activity in reward-anticipation brain regions, pupil dilation, and better later recall of answers people had been curious about; other studies show people pay for information with no practical use. — [Golman & Loewenstein](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/golman/golman_loewenstein_curiosity.pdf)
- Uncertain threat is more arousing than certain threat: in a study they cite (Arntz et al., 1992), people awaiting an uncertain shock showed more physiological arousal than people who knew a strong shock was coming. — [Golman & Loewenstein](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/golman/golman_loewenstein_curiosity.pdf)

**Structural-affect theory (Brewer & Lichtenstein, 1981/1982)**
- The experiment rewrote one base narrative into versions differing only in information order. Mean liking on a 7-point scale: no-bomb base version 2.15; standard suspense (reader told about the bomb early, outcome pending) 4.85; suspense with foreshadowing 4.30; outcome moved to page one 3.65; resolution removed 3.85; surprise version (bomb only revealed at the explosion) 4.30. — [Brewer & Lichtenstein 1981 summary, D. Miall, Univ. of Alberta](https://sites.ualberta.ca/~dmiall/694Empirical/Brewer_Lichtenstein_1981.htm)
- "Is this a story?" ratings: suspense-standard 5.05, but the version with no resolution dropped to 2.75 (near the eventless base version's 2.45). Readers do not accept unresolved tension as a story. — [Brewer & Lichtenstein 1981 summary](https://sites.ualberta.ca/~dmiall/694Empirical/Brewer_Lichtenstein_1981.htm)
- Giving away the outcome first cut liking (3.65 vs 4.85) while leaving "story-ness" high (4.50). — [Brewer & Lichtenstein 1981 summary](https://sites.ualberta.ca/~dmiall/694Empirical/Brewer_Lichtenstein_1981.htm)

**Hitchcock's practitioner version of the same idea**
- Bomb under the table: if the audience is unaware, the explosion yields roughly fifteen seconds of shock; if the audience watched it being planted and can see a clock, the same dull conversation yields roughly fifteen minutes of tension, with viewers wanting to warn the characters. His rule is to inform the audience wherever possible, with one exception: when the unexpected ending itself is the point of the story. — [No Film School, Hitchcock/Truffaut on surprise vs suspense](https://nofilmschool.com/alfred-hitchcock-and-francois-truffaut-explain-surprise-vs-suspense)

**Narrative transportation (Green & Brock)**
- Transportation is absorption into a story world, described as attention, mental imagery and emotion engaged together; Green & Brock (2000) built the standard Transportation Scale and found more-transported readers were more persuaded by the narrative. — [Schreiner et al., review section, PMC5954649](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5954649)
- Transportation rises with craftsmanship and clear narrative structure, and with reader traits (need for affect, "transportability"); scrambling event order while keeping content identical lowers it. — [PMC5954649](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5954649)
- In that paper's own data, transportation declined at later positions in the text. — [PMC5954649](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5954649)

**Zeigarnik / Ovsiankina effects**
- A 2025 meta-analysis found no reliable memory advantage for unfinished over finished tasks (the Zeigarnik effect), but did find a general tendency to resume interrupted tasks (the Ovsiankina effect). The authors say the Zeigarnik effect lacks universal validity and may depend on situational factors. — [Ghibellini & Meier, Humanities and Social Sciences Communications 2025 (abstract via IDEAS/RePEc)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/palcom/v12y2025i1d10.1057_s41599-025-05000-w.html)

**Spoilers (conflicting evidence)**
- Leavitt & Christenfeld (2011; 819 undergraduates; stories by e.g. Dahl and Chekhov) found spoiled stories rated slightly MORE enjoyable on average, including mysteries and twist stories. A follow-up (240 undergraduates) with simple, predictable stories found no difference. Proposed mechanism: processing fluency; knowing the destination makes the story easier to follow. — [The Conversation, "Plot spoilers often increase enjoyment"](https://theconversation.com/enough-with-the-spoiler-alerts-plot-spoilers-often-increase-enjoyment-62154)
- Individual differences: Rosenbaum & Johnson (368 undergraduates) found people high in need for affect enjoyed UNSPOILED stories more. — [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/enough-with-the-spoiler-alerts-plot-spoilers-often-increase-enjoyment-62154)
- Contradicting result: Levine, Betzner & Autry (Discourse Processes, 2016), using three ironic-twist stories and one-sentence spoilers, found that a spoiler before the story REDUCED enjoyment, a mid-story spoiler had no detectable effect, and no enjoyment boost appeared. — [University of Arkansas news](https://news.uark.edu/articles/38085/fulbright-college-research-team-explores-how-spoilers-affect-reading-enjoyment); the lab's own blog reports Johnson & Rosenbaum independently also found spoilers reduce enjoyment — [W. H. Levine lab blog](https://wordpressua.uark.edu/whlevine/?p=122)

### Inferences
- Design each mystery question to score high on Loewenstein's three levers: make it matter to someone the player cares about (importance), keep it visibly in front of the player (salience: a board, a locked door, a name with a blank), and structure the web so a few key facts collapse many questions at once (epiphany).
- Curiosity needs a felt "nearly there." A player who thinks they already know, or who has no foothold at all, feels little. Give partial knowledge first, then show the hole.
- Because satisfaction decays quickly after an answer, every major answer should open a new, bigger question in the same beat, or attention drops.
- Use all three Brewer-Lichtenstein structures deliberately: curiosity (player knows something happened, not how), suspense (player knows a threat the characters do not), surprise (critical fact withheld until it detonates). Suspense-with-resolution was the best-liked form; pure surprise is cheaper per minute.
- Never leave a major tension without resolution: the no-resolution version was barely judged a story.
- Cliffhangers are better justified by the urge to resume (Ovsiankina) than by memory (Zeigarnik). End sessions/chapters mid-action or mid-question to pull people back; do not rely on interruption to make content memorable.
- The spoiler literature suggests enjoyment does not depend only on not knowing: a twist story must still work when the twist is known (replay value, fluency). But the conflicting replications mean you should not assume twists are worthless either; protect them, and also build for the second playthrough.

### Gaps
- I did not obtain the full text of Loewenstein (1994) itself, Green & Brock (2000) itself, or effect sizes from the Zeigarnik meta-analysis (paywalled; abstract only).
- No direct experimental study of cliffhangers in serial fiction or games was found in this pass; the cliffhanger link to Ovsiankina is an inference.
- Brewer & Lichtenstein's per-page suspense/curiosity curves were only available as images; curiosity-structure numbers are not reported here.
- The spoiler studies use short written stories, not long games or mysteries played over tens of hours; transfer is uncertain.

## 2. What do leading craft authorities say drives engagement?

### Takeaway
Across very different vocabularies the authorities converge on four things: causality (every beat caused by the last), a gap between what a character expects and what happens, a protagonist whose flawed belief is tested by the plot, and promises made early that are visibly progressed and then paid off in a surprising-but-fitting way.

### Cited Findings

**Parker & Stone: therefore / but**
- When beats are laid out, the link between each pair should be "therefore" or "but"; if "and then" fits, the outline is a list of events without cause and effect and will bore. Stone said plenty of big films fall into this, and both stressed clarity and doing one thing well. — [Cracked, on the NYU class](https://cracked.com/article_43358_trey-parker-and-matt-stones-surprise-nyu-class-contains-some-great-writing-advice.html)

**Aristotle (secondary)**
- A plot should move in a probable or necessary sequence. Simple plots have neither reversal (peripeteia: a situation flips to its opposite) nor recognition (anagnorisis: a move from ignorance to knowledge); complex plots have one or both, and the strongest recognition arrives together with a reversal, as when Oedipus's discovery of who he is destroys his fortune. Both must grow out of prior events, not be bolted on to keep the audience guessing. — [SparkNotes, Poetics ch. 10-12](https://www.sparknotes.com/philosophy/poetics/section5/)

**Robert McKee (secondary, via Emily Short)**
- McKee's engine is the gap between what the protagonist expects from an action and what actually results; a scene where the protagonist gets exactly the expected result is not worth including. He also wants a subconscious desire that contradicts the conscious one. — [Emily Short, "Story (Robert McKee) and the Expectation Gap in Interactive Story"](https://emshort.blog/2019/02/05/story-robert-mckee/)

**John Truby (secondary, reader notes on Anatomy of Story)**
- Seven core steps: weakness and need, desire, opponent, plan, battle, self-revelation, new equilibrium. — [Medium summary of Truby's seven steps](https://medium.com/@pirangy/the-seven-key-steps-of-story-structure-john-truby-the-anatomy-of-story-d04a9c60031f)
- Need is internal and unknown to the hero; desire is the external goal that gives direction. Stronger stories give the hero a moral need (a flaw that hurts other people) on top of a psychological one. Decide the final self-revelation first and work backward. — [Readwise notes on The Anatomy of Story](https://publish.obsidian.md/aidanhelfant/Literature+Notes/Readwise/%F0%9F%93%9ABooks/The+Anatomy+of+Story)
- In a "reveals plot" the hero mostly stays in one arena while capable opponents scheme out of sight; each revelation must be big enough to change the hero's course, and revelations must increase in intensity or the plot stalls. — [Readwise notes on The Anatomy of Story](https://publish.obsidian.md/aidanhelfant/Literature+Notes/Readwise/%F0%9F%93%9ABooks/The+Anatomy+of+Story)

**Brandon Sanderson**
- Promise / progress / payoff. Promises are set in the opening pages (tone, the story's direction, the character and their conflict, structural/genre signals). Progress is the reader's FELT sense of movement, created by signposts such as clues found, suspects eliminated, relationship shifts, belief changes; too few signposts is why readers quit. A payoff is "a surprising but fulfilling answer to your promise." — [Brandon Sanderson, 2025 Guide to Plot, Lecture 2](https://brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/brandon-sandersons-2025-guide-to-plot-lecture-2)
- A twist is not required if the payoff is cathartic: by Helm's Deep the situation is so bleak that the audience has forgotten Gandalf's promised return, so its fulfilment lands hard. — [Sanderson Lecture 2](https://brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/brandon-sandersons-2025-guide-to-plot-lecture-2)
- First Law: "An author's ability to solve conflict with magic is DIRECTLY PROPORTIONAL to how well the reader understands said magic." Unexplained ("soft") powers are fine for atmosphere and for causing problems but should not solve them; understood ("hard") rules let characters win by ingenuity. He prefers hidden rules that allow later discovery. — [Sanderson's First Law](https://brandonsanderson.com/sandersons-first-law/)

**Dan Harmon's story circle (secondary)**
- Eight beats: comfort zone; want; enter the unfamiliar; adapt; get what they wanted; pay a heavy price; return; changed. The price step means winning is not the end, and without change the return is just a reset. — [Reedsy guide to the Story Circle](https://reedsy.com/blog/guide/story-structure/dan-harmon-story-circle/)

**Pixar's rules (Emma Coats)**
- Audiences admire a character for trying more than for succeeding; coincidence may get characters INTO trouble but using it to get them out is cheating; know the ending before building the middle; make clear what the character stands to lose and stack the odds; throw away the first obvious ideas; the story spine runs from a routine, through a disruption, through a chain of "because of that" consequences. — [Open Culture, Pixar's 22 rules](https://www.openculture.com/2013/03/pixars_22_rules_of_good_storytelling.html)

**Lisa Cron, Wired for Story (secondary)**
- Plot is the external events; story is how those events force the protagonist to change internally. A story starts when something contradicts a belief the protagonist holds. Readers look at once for whose story it is, what is happening, and what is at stake. Withheld information creates suspense only if hints are specific enough for readers to form (possibly wrong) guesses; a reveal should reframe what came before. Any out-of-character action needs earlier preparation. — [Shortform overview of Wired for Story](https://www.shortform.com/blog/wired-for-story-by-lisa-cron/)

**Will Storr, The Science of Storytelling (secondary)**
- The brain is a change detector, so stories open with unexpected change; Storr leans on Loewenstein's information gaps. Each character has a "theory of control" (beliefs about how the world works and how to get what they want) built around a "sacred flaw," a mistaken belief their identity rests on. Plot exists to test and break it. The underlying dramatic question is who this person really is, and a good ending answers it decisively. — [SuperSummary, The Science of Storytelling](https://www.supersummary.com/the-science-of-storytelling/summary/)

**K.M. Weiland**
- The arc is rooted in a Lie the character believes. The Want is an external goal the character thinks will fix things; the Need is the Truth that counters the Lie. Their conflict produces the inner struggle and feeds the outer one. — [K.M. Weiland, Character Arcs pt. 3](https://www.helpingwritersbecomeauthors.com/character-arcs-3/)

### Inferences
- Practical outline test for the mystery: write the full event chain (both the hidden backstory crime chain and the player-facing discovery chain) and require "therefore" or "but" between every pair. Any "and then" is a coincidence or a dead beat.
- Sanderson's First Law generalises to mysteries and game mechanics: the solution (and any device, technology, or supernatural rule that enables it) is only satisfying to the degree the player understood the rules beforehand. Teach the rules early, hide their implications.
- Pixar's coincidence rule plus Aristotle's probability rule give a fairness check: coincidences may create the crime or complicate the investigation; they may never supply the answer.
- McKee's gap and Storr's "unexpected change" are the same engine as Loewenstein's violated expectation: every scene/interrogation/location should return something other than what the player expected.
- In a mystery, "progress" signposts must be made visible in the interface (confirmed facts, eliminated suspects), since Sanderson links drop-off to missing signposts.

### Gaps
- McKee's own text on values turning, progressive complications, crisis and climax was not retrieved; only the expectation gap and subconscious desire are sourced here.
- Truby's own definitions of theme/designing principle were not retrieved; Truby material is from reader notes, not the book.
- Sanderson's Second and Third Laws (limitations over powers; expand before adding) were not fetched and are not cited here.
- Harmon's own "Story Structure 101" posts were not fetched; the order/chaos reading of the circle's halves is not confirmed by the source used.

## 3. What makes a twist land emotionally rather than feel cheap?

### Takeaway
A good twist is prepared in plain sight and exploits how readers lose track of who knows what; afterwards the audience can look back and see it was fair. Cheap twists are improbable, unprepared, or solve problems with rules the audience never knew.

### Cited Findings
- Cognitive scientist Vera Tobin explains twists through the curse of knowledge and anchoring: audiences assume characters perceive scenes as they themselves do (so in The Sixth Sense they miss that one character never looks at another), and the first framing given stays as an anchor even when doubted, so red herrings keep steering guesses. Narrators with a stake shape what seems real (The Usual Suspects). — [NPR Hidden Brain transcript, "Spoiler Alert: The Psychology of Surprise Endings"](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/661878959)
- After the reveal, hindsight bias makes people feel they half-saw it, which persuades them the story played fair; a well-built twist gives a re-seeing of earlier scenes rather than a feeling of being blindsided. — [NPR Hidden Brain transcript](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/661878959)
- Sanderson's standard: payoffs should feel both unexpected and inevitable. His method for twists is expectation substitution: promise one thing, then gradually make the audience want something else that the story then delivers, so the swap delights instead of betraying the core promise. — [Sanderson Lecture 2](https://brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/brandon-sandersons-2025-guide-to-plot-lecture-2)
- Aristotle (secondary): reversals and recognitions must follow from what came before by probability or necessity; reversals that exist only to keep the audience guessing feel forced. Best case is recognition and reversal in the same moment. — [SparkNotes, Poetics](https://www.sparknotes.com/philosophy/poetics/section5/)
- Cron (secondary): foreshadowing gives readers a memory to recall at the payoff; hints must be concrete enough to guess from; the reveal should make earlier events mean something new. — [Shortform, Wired for Story](https://www.shortform.com/blog/wired-for-story-by-lisa-cron/)
- Dramatic irony as the alternative to a twist: Hitchcock's rule is that letting the audience know what characters do not turns minutes of ordinary action into tension; withholding is reserved for when the twist itself is the story's high point. — [No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/alfred-hitchcock-and-francois-truffaut-explain-surprise-vs-suspense)
- Golman & Loewenstein's model predicts the most-wanted information is that which resolves several open questions at once. — [Golman & Loewenstein](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/golman/golman_loewenstein_curiosity.pdf)
- Pixar: coincidence that rescues is a cheat. — [Open Culture](https://www.openculture.com/2013/03/pixars_22_rules_of_good_storytelling.html)

### Inferences
- Twist checklist: (1) every fact needed was shown on screen before the reveal; (2) the audience misread it because of a natural assumption (shared perspective, trusted narrator, first framing, stereotype), not because the author lied; (3) the reveal answers several standing questions at once; (4) it flips the protagonist's situation (recognition + reversal together); (5) it changes the meaning of earlier scenes so replay is rewarding; (6) it serves, not replaces, the story's core promise.
- Plant the first (false) framing early and confidently: anchoring means later doubts adjust from it rather than discard it.
- Choose per secret whether it is worth more as a twist (seconds of shock plus recontextualisation) or as dramatic irony (long suspense). In a long game most secrets should be converted to dramatic irony or active investigation; reserve true blindside twists for a few structural pivots.
- In a game, the curse-of-knowledge trick can target the player's assumptions about their own character or interface (what the player-character knows vs. what the player knows).

### Gaps
- Tobin's book Elements of Surprise was not read directly; findings come from an interview transcript.
- I found no controlled experiment comparing "fair" and "unfair" twists on satisfaction; the standard is craft consensus plus cognitive explanation.
- The origin of the phrase "surprising yet inevitable" (often attributed to Aristotle or others) was not verified.

## 4. What makes characters compelling in complex plots?

### Takeaway
Compelling characters hold a wrong belief that the plot attacks, want something external that conflicts with what they need, and face opponents who want the same thing for coherent reasons and who strike at their specific weakness; the peak choice is a true dilemma between goods.

### Cited Findings
- Want vs. Need (Weiland): the Want is a concrete goal the character believes will solve their problem; the Need is the Truth opposed to their Lie. The Need may require giving up the Want (bittersweet: inner gain, outer loss), or embracing the Need may be what finally makes the Want reachable. The Lie has a backstory cause she calls the Ghost. — [K.M. Weiland, Character Arcs pt. 3](https://www.helpingwritersbecomeauthors.com/character-arcs-3/)
- Truby (secondary): the best opponent competes for the SAME goal as the hero, otherwise they never truly collide; opponents should attack the hero's great weakness, each in a different way; use a hero, a main opponent and at least two secondary opponents whose values also clash with one another ("four-corner opposition"). — [Readwise notes on The Anatomy of Story](https://publish.obsidian.md/aidanhelfant/Literature+Notes/Readwise/%F0%9F%93%9ABooks/The+Anatomy+of+Story)
- Truby (secondary): moral argument should be carried by structure and action, not speeches; showing why a character acts builds empathy without endorsing the act; the final moral choice should be a real dilemma between two positives (rarely two negatives). A moral weakness can be made by pushing a virtue until it becomes a vice. — [Readwise notes on The Anatomy of Story](https://publish.obsidian.md/aidanhelfant/Literature+Notes/Readwise/%F0%9F%93%9ABooks/The+Anatomy+of+Story)
- Storr (secondary): characters do not fully know themselves because the mind invents explanations for its own behaviour; people cast themselves as moral heroes, and Storr argues conviction of moral superiority drives more real harm than greed or sadism. Example: Michael Corleone's flaw is believing he is a decent family man rather than a gangster. — [SuperSummary, The Science of Storytelling](https://www.supersummary.com/the-science-of-storytelling/summary/)
- McKee (secondary): give characters a subconscious desire that contradicts the conscious one. — [Emily Short on McKee](https://emshort.blog/2019/02/05/story-robert-mckee/)
- Cron (secondary): protect nobody; change is painful, so the plot must hurt the protagonist where their belief is; the protagonist should feel differently at the end of each scene than at its start. — [Shortform, Wired for Story](https://www.shortform.com/blog/wired-for-story-by-lisa-cron/)
- Pixar: effort is more admirable than success; show what the character can lose. — [Open Culture](https://www.openculture.com/2013/03/pixars_22_rules_of_good_storytelling.html)
- Loewenstein's "importance" driver: curiosity is strongest for answers that bear on how one sees oneself. — [Golman & Loewenstein](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/golman/golman_loewenstein_curiosity.pdf)

### Inferences
- Antagonist recipe: a culprit who believes they are the hero of the story (Storr), wants the same thing the protagonist wants (Truby), and whose method is a virtue pushed into vice. This yields a coherent motive the player can reconstruct, which is also what makes a mystery solvable.
- Anti-hero recipe: sympathetic reasons shown before the bad acts (Truby's empathy-without-endorsement), plus a self-justifying inner narrator (Storr). The audience stays because the question "who is this person really?" is still open.
- Tie the central mystery to the protagonist's Lie: the solution should be something the protagonist could not see BECAUSE of their flawed belief, so solving the case and facing the Truth are one event (recognition + reversal).
- Give every major suspect a want, a lie and a secret; in a complex plot the cast should form several value oppositions so each suspect embodies a different answer to the theme question.
- Stakes become character-driven when the big idea at issue is exactly the belief the protagonist's identity depends on.

### Gaps
- No empirical study on why audiences like anti-heroes (e.g. affective disposition theory, moral disengagement research) was retrieved in this pass; anti-hero points are craft-derived inferences.
- Weiland's detailed treatment of the Ghost and of negative/flat arcs was not fetched.

## 5. How do long-form stories pace questions, answers, stakes and theme so complexity stays engaging?

### Takeaway
Keep the audience oriented and moving: make promises early, show visible progress constantly, answer questions in ways that raise larger ones, escalate the size of each revelation, and anchor every plot question to an emotional question about a person.

### Cited Findings
- Sanderson: progress is about felt movement; signposts (new information, obstacles, decisions, changes of direction) tell readers they are advancing, and their absence is why readers abandon books. Kinds of progress include information (clues, cleared suspects), relationships, and internal belief change. Every section should advance at least one. — [Sanderson Lecture 2](https://brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/brandon-sandersons-2025-guide-to-plot-lecture-2)
- Truby (secondary): revelations must each alter the hero's course and must grow in intensity; otherwise the plot stalls. — [Readwise notes on The Anatomy of Story](https://publish.obsidian.md/aidanhelfant/Literature+Notes/Readwise/%F0%9F%93%9ABooks/The+Anatomy+of+Story)
- Cron (secondary): answer "who, what, what's at stake" fast and readers will tolerate slow detail; each scene must cause the next. — [Shortform, Wired for Story](https://www.shortform.com/blog/wired-for-story-by-lisa-cron/)
- Parker/Stone: causal connectives between all beats. — [Cracked](https://cracked.com/article_43358_trey-parker-and-matt-stones-surprise-nyu-class-contains-some-great-writing-advice.html)
- Golman & Loewenstein: attention to a question collapses after it is answered; curiosity requires salience and remaining uncertainty. — [Golman & Loewenstein](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/golman/golman_loewenstein_curiosity.pdf)
- Transportation research: disordered structure reduces absorption, and in one dataset transportation fell at later text positions. — [PMC5954649](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5954649)
- Leavitt & Christenfeld's proposed fluency mechanism: stories that are easier to process are liked more. — [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/enough-with-the-spoiler-alerts-plot-spoilers-often-increase-enjoyment-62154)
- Pixar: theme may only become clear at the end, so rewrite once you know it; find the most economical telling of the core. Truby (secondary): theme should emerge from morally loaded actions, not dialogue. — [Open Culture](https://www.openculture.com/2013/03/pixars_22_rules_of_good_storytelling.html); [Readwise notes on Truby](https://publish.obsidian.md/aidanhelfant/Literature+Notes/Readwise/%F0%9F%93%9ABooks/The+Anatomy+of+Story)
- Golden Idol developers: clues were organised into "thought paths" where one finding leads to the next; only necessary information or deliberate misdirection was kept; multiple routes to the same conclusion helped balance difficulty; the high-level story was fixed early with room between cases to fill gaps and revisit earlier cases. — [Game Developer, The Case of the Golden Idol](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/case-of-the-golden-idol)
- Outer Wilds: a compact world favouring depth over sprawl; each location holds several clues feeding larger questions; text mattered more to players when the people in it faced the same situation as the player; players must be "ready to learn" (an early dramatic event failed because players did not yet know the place it disrupted). — [Game Developer, Outer Wilds](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/live-die-repeat-how-i-outer-wilds-i-piques-curiosity-in-an-ambivalent-solar-system)

### Inferences
- Question ledger: keep a small number of headline questions open at once; when one closes, the answer should open a larger one in the same beat (curiosity decays on resolution). Mix horizons: scene-level (minutes), chapter-level (hours), whole-story.
- Complexity is tolerable when fluency is high: recap tools, a clue board, consistent naming, and clear causal links carry the load so that cognitive effort goes into deduction rather than bookkeeping.
- Escalate on two axes: stakes widen (personal to communal to idea-level) and deepen (closer to the protagonist's identity). Each reveal should force a change of plan.
- Establish normality before breaking it (Outer Wilds' "ready to learn"): a twist about a place or person only lands after the player has a settled picture of them.
- Since absorption tends to sag late, plan fresh structural energy (a major recontextualisation, new mechanic, or viewpoint) in the back half rather than only more content.
- Cut everything that is neither needed information nor intentional misdirection; noise is what makes complexity feel exhausting.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed research specifically on question/answer pacing in serial TV, long novels or long games was found; this section rests on craft sources and inference from curiosity research.
- "Mystery box" criticism and showrunner practice (e.g. Lost, Dark, Severance) were not researched in this pass.

## 6. What makes an ending memorable and satisfying?

### Takeaway
Remembered evaluations are dominated by the most intense moment and the final moments, and lasting impact is tied to being moved and made to think, which usually means mixed emotion. Craft sources agree the ending must settle who the protagonist is and must cost something.

### Cited Findings
- Peak-end rule: people judge a bounded experience mostly by its most intense point and its end, largely ignoring duration. In the 1993 cold-water study, most participants chose to repeat a longer trial that ended slightly less painfully over a shorter one; in a 2003 randomised colonoscopy trial, adding a few milder final minutes made patients rate the procedure as less unpleasant and more willing to return. — [Wikipedia, Peak-end rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peak%E2%80%93end_rule)
- Limits: a 2008 study found the rule only a moderate predictor of remembered value; peaks fade faster than ends over time; expectations and goals alter the weights; it did not clearly hold for day-long experiences. — [Wikipedia, Peak-end rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peak%E2%80%93end_rule)
- Oliver & Bartsch distinguish enjoyment (fun, suspense) from appreciation (being moved, finding the work thought-provoking). Across three studies, these gratifications were associated with the sense that a work leaves a lasting impression. — [Oliver & Bartsch 2010, Human Communication Research (abstract, Penn State)](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/appreciation-as-audience-response-exploring-entertainment-gratifi/)
- Bartsch & Oliver: serious drama typically yields mixed affect (negative feelings alongside positive meta-emotions) and prompts reflection linking the story to the viewer's own life; dramas are rated more thought-provoking than comedies, while horror rates low on this, probably because arousal is too high. — [Bartsch & Oliver, "Making sense of entertainment"](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/20997/1/11-02%20-%20making%20sense%20of%20entertainment_web.pdf)
- Brewer & Lichtenstein: removing the resolution dropped "is it a story" ratings to near the level of an eventless narrative. — [Brewer & Lichtenstein 1981 summary](https://sites.ualberta.ca/~dmiall/694Empirical/Brewer_Lichtenstein_1981.htm)
- Weiland: two satisfying shapes: surrender the Want to gain the Need (bittersweet), or embrace the Need and thereby win the Want. — [Weiland, Character Arcs pt. 3](https://www.helpingwritersbecomeauthors.com/character-arcs-3/)
- Harmon (secondary): after getting what they wanted the protagonist pays heavily, and must come back changed. — [Reedsy](https://reedsy.com/blog/guide/story-structure/dan-harmon-story-circle/)
- Storr (secondary): the ending answers the dramatic question of who the person is; there is a brief point where the protagonist controls both outer events and inner identity. — [SuperSummary](https://www.supersummary.com/the-science-of-storytelling/summary/)
- Truby (secondary): a neat self-revelation followed by calm is a "false, closed" ending; the final choice should be between two goods. — [Readwise notes on Truby](https://publish.obsidian.md/aidanhelfant/Literature+Notes/Readwise/%F0%9F%93%9ABooks/The+Anatomy+of+Story)
- Pixar: fix the ending before the middle. Sanderson: the climax must match the core promise; a cathartic fulfilment can beat a twist. — [Open Culture](https://www.openculture.com/2013/03/pixars_22_rules_of_good_storytelling.html); [Sanderson Lecture 2](https://brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/brandon-sandersons-2025-guide-to-plot-lecture-2)

### Inferences
- Budget the best material for one or two peaks and the final sequence; a long, uneven middle will be forgiven more readily than a weak last hour (duration neglect), though the rule's limits mean this is a tendency, not a law.
- Aim for an ending that is moving and thought-provoking, not only thrilling: victory with a real, chosen cost; solving the mystery should take something from the protagonist or player.
- The final decision should be a dilemma between two goods that embodies the theme, with the mystery's solution supplying the information that makes the dilemma visible.
- Resolve the plot questions fully (unresolved = "not a story") but leave the thematic/moral question open enough to keep the audience thinking afterwards.
- Avoid ending at maximum horror/arousal; reflection needs a quieter beat after the climax.

### Gaps
- No study was found that directly tests the peak-end rule on films, novels or games; applying it to stories is an extrapolation from pain/queue/consumer studies. The peak-end summary is from Wikipedia rather than the primary papers.
- Oliver & Bartsch statistics were not available from the abstract.
- No empirical comparison of bittersweet vs. happy vs. tragic endings on long-term memorability was found.

## 7. Games: how do interactivity, agency and complicity change emotional impact?

### Takeaway
Games can produce emotions passive media cannot, notably guilt and the pride of personal deduction, because the player performs the act. This works when choices are made through the core mechanics, the game withholds judgment, progress is stored in the player's own understanding, and consequences follow from the player's choice with a twist of cost rather than negating it.

### Cited Findings

**Guilt and moral engagement (research)**
- Grizzard et al. (2014): players who spent ten minutes as a terrorist killing civilians in a shooter reported more guilt than those playing a UN soldier, and afterwards rated care and fairness concerns (the ones the scenario violated) as more important; that increase was related to how guilty they felt. — [BPS Research Digest on Grizzard et al.](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/could-violent-video-games-make-players-more-moral-real-world)
- Weaver & Lewis (2012), Fallout 3: most players made moral decisions and treated non-player characters as if they were real people; choices were predicted by players' real moral-foundations scores; antisocial play raised guilt without lowering enjoyment. — [PubMed, "Mirrored Morality"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23017118/)

**Complicity design (Spec Ops: The Line)**
- Choices are expressed through the same aim-and-shoot mechanics as the rest of play rather than menus, so the player's physical action is the character's moral action; doing nothing or walking away also counts. The game neither rewards nor condemns, aiming for the player to ask what they themselves would do instead of what the designers want. Events are clear but their meaning is left ambiguous. The game opens as a conventional heroic rescue and piles on horror gradually; the protagonist's animations and combat shouts degrade at scripted points. — [Game Developer, "No Good Deed: narrative design in Spec Ops: The Line"](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/no-good-deed-narrative-design-in-i-spec-ops-the-line-i-)
- After the white-phosphorus scene, loading-screen tips turn into accusations directed at the player; the scene shows targets through an abstracting thermal view, then makes the player walk through the result. Criticism: the player is forced to fire for the story to continue, which reviewers called heavy-handed. — [GDC Game Narrative Review, Spec Ops: The Line](https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc2015/GameNarrativeReview/Wrampelmeier_Spec Ops_the_Line .pdf)

**Ludonarrative dissonance and harmony**
- The term was coined by Clint Hocking in 2007 about BioShock: gameplay rewarded self-interest while the story demanded selflessness and removed choice, pulling him out of the experience. Cited examples of harmony include Papers, Please (time-pressured paperwork IS the theme) and Pathologic 2 (hard controls convey illness). Spec Ops writer Walt Williams argues dissonance can be used on purpose to make a character a hypocrite and force players to rationalise. — [Wikipedia, Ludonarrative dissonance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludonarrative_dissonance)

**Expectation gap vs. agency (Emily Short)**
- When the player is exploring for information, surprising results are welcome. When the player commits to a decision, a totally unexpected result feels like stolen agency and a fully predictable one has no drama; "yes, but" results (the choice works, with a cost or side effect that advances the story) are best. Mere chance of failure does not create a gap. Costs land when they stem from character, not coincidence, and the player opted in. — [Emily Short on McKee](https://emshort.blog/2019/02/05/story-robert-mckee/)

**Mystery/deduction design**
- Outer Wilds removed collectibles and upgrades so curiosity is the only motive; the time loop means only what the player has learned carries over, with one designer describing "knowledge being the only real thing of value in this world." The world runs on its own schedule whether or not the player is there. — [Game Developer, Outer Wilds](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/live-die-repeat-how-i-outer-wilds-i-piques-curiosity-in-an-ambivalent-solar-system)
- Golden Idol: free-text answers frustrate and multiple choice removes deduction, so they used constrained word-slot filling; all-or-nothing grading frustrated, so a "two or fewer wrong" indicator was added (at some cost to difficulty) and smaller sub-puzzles gave a feeling of progress; designers cannot judge difficulty because they know the answer, so they ran repeated silent playtests and let players stay stuck, since insight can arrive suddenly. — [Game Developer, The Case of the Golden Idol](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/case-of-the-golden-idol)

### Inferences
- A mystery game can make the PLAYER, not just the character, experience recognition: if the solution is assembled in the player's head (knowledge-gated progress, constrained deduction input), the "aha" belongs to them. This is the strongest available version of Aristotle's anagnorisis.
- Complicity twist pattern: let the player perform, through ordinary mechanics and with partial information, an action whose meaning is later recontextualised. Research suggests guilt arises and does not reduce enjoyment. Give at least the appearance of an alternative (including inaction); the main recorded criticism of Spec Ops is the forced act.
- Do not moralise in the interface. No karma meter: withheld judgment keeps the question with the player.
- Make the investigation mechanics a metaphor for the theme (as paperwork is in Papers, Please); and if the story is about, say, the cost of knowing, then knowing should cost the player something mechanically.
- Apply Short's rule: surprise freely on information-seeking actions (examining, questioning), but on commitments (accusing, choosing sides) deliver "yes, but" outcomes.
- Sanderson's First Law applies to mechanics: the player can only fairly solve with rules the game has taught.
- Prevent brute force without killing momentum: partial-correctness feedback and sub-deductions act as Sanderson-style progress signposts.

### Gaps
- Game Developer article on Outer Wilds did not cover the ship log/rumor map or the ending; Return of the Obra Dinn design sources were not fetched.
- Hocking's original blog post was not read directly (Wikipedia summary used).
- No research was retrieved on whether player-authored deduction is remembered longer than narrated revelation, nor on long-term memorability of guilt-inducing game scenes; the Grizzard study measured short sessions and immediate self-report (no sample sizes available from the summary used).
- Other frequently cited complicity examples (Undertale, Brothers: A Tale of Two Sons, Disco Elysium, Her Story, Nier) were not researched in this pass.
